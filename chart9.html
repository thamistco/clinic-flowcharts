    1

    Could this be an acute coronary syndrome?

    URGENT

    +

    Check now whether the person has chest pain and, if pain free, when the last episode was, particularly within the last 12 hours (1.2.1.1). Symptoms that may indicate ACS (1.2.1.3): pain in the chest and/or other areas (arms, back or jaw) lasting longer than 15 minutes; chest pain with nausea and vomiting, marked sweating or breathlessness; chest pain with haemodynamic instability; new onset chest pain, or abrupt deterioration in previously stable angina with recurrent pain at little or no exertion. Do not use the response to GTN to make a diagnosis (1.2.1.4), and do not assess symptoms differently in men and women or in different ethnic groups (1.2.1.5–1.2.1.6).

    2

    Decide the urgency of referral by timing and ECG

    URGENTREFER

    +

    Refer to hospital as an emergency if ACS is suspected and the person currently has chest pain, or is pain free but had pain in the last 12 hours and a resting 12-lead ECG is abnormal or not available (1.2.1.7). Urgent same-day assessment if pain in the last 12 hours but now pain free with a normal ECG, or the last episode was 12 to 72 hours ago (1.2.1.8). Signs of complications such as pulmonary oedema after the pain has resolved: refer for hospital assessment (1.2.1.9). Last pain more than 72 hours ago with no complications: detailed clinical assessment, ECG and troponin, then decide whether and how urgently to refer (1.2.1.10). Further chest pain after a recent ACS: emergency referral (1.2.1.11).

    3

    Immediate management while arranging transfer

    DRUG

    +

    Start management as soon as ACS is suspected without delaying transfer (1.2.1.12). Take a resting 12-lead ECG as soon as possible and send it with the person; recording it must not delay transfer (1.2.2.1). Offer pain relief (GTN sublingual or buccal) (1.2.3.1). Offer a single loading dose of 300 mg aspirin as soon as possible unless there is clear evidence of allergy, and send a written record that it was given; other antiplatelets only in hospital (1.2.3.2). Do not routinely give oxygen: monitor SpO2 and offer oxygen only if SpO2 is below 94% (target 94% to 98%), or 88% to 92% in COPD at risk of hypercapnic respiratory failure (1.2.3.3).

    4

    If ACS is not suspected, think of other serious causes

    URGENT

    +

    If an acute coronary syndrome is not suspected, consider other causes of the chest pain, some of which may be life-threatening (1.2.1.13), for example aortic dissection, which NICE names among the non-coronary causes of acute chest pain to look for on examination (1.2.4.2). Use the history and examination to decide the next step.

    5

    Stable chest pain: classify typicality and arrange tests

    ASSESS

    +

    Take a detailed history (age, sex, character, location, radiation, duration, provoking and relieving factors, cardiovascular history and risk factors) and examine (1.3.2.1–1.3.2.2). Anginal pain is: constricting discomfort in the front of the chest, or in the neck, shoulders, jaw or arms; precipitated by physical exertion; relieved by rest or GTN within about 5 minutes. Three features = typical angina, two = atypical angina, one or none = non-anginal chest pain (1.3.3.1). Offer 64-slice (or above) CT coronary angiography if assessment indicates typical or atypical angina, or non-anginal pain with ST-T changes or Q waves on the resting ECG (1.3.4.3). Most people with non-anginal chest pain need no further diagnostic testing; include the typicality in test requests (1.3.4.1).

    1

    Sudden neurological symptoms: screen with FAST and check glucose

    URGENT

    +

    Use a validated tool such as FAST (Face Arm Speech Test) outside hospital to screen people with sudden onset of neurological symptoms for stroke or TIA (1.1.1), and exclude hypoglycaemia as the cause (1.1.2). Suspected stroke: the NHS FAST message is face, arms, speech and time to call 999. In the emergency department the diagnosis is then established rapidly with a validated tool such as ROSIER (1.1.3).

    2

    Suspected TIA: aspirin 300 mg now

    DRUG

    +

    Offer aspirin (300 mg daily), unless contraindicated, to people who have had a suspected TIA, to be started immediately (1.1.4). The committee noted that general practices need adequate supplies of aspirin to give it immediately.

    3

    Refer every suspected TIA to be seen within 24 hours

    URGENTREFER

    +

    Refer immediately everyone who has had a suspected TIA for specialist assessment and investigation, to be seen within 24 hours of onset of symptoms (1.1.5). Do not use scoring systems such as ABCD2 to assess stroke risk or to decide the urgency of referral (1.1.6): all suspected TIAs are treated as potentially high risk.

    4

    After specialist confirmation: secondary prevention

    REVIEW/LIMIT

    +

    Offer secondary prevention, in addition to aspirin, as soon as possible after the diagnosis of TIA is confirmed (1.1.7). Expect that some suspected TIAs turn out to have an alternative diagnosis; specialist assessment confirms or refutes it.

    1

    Swollen or painful leg: assess, then 2-level DVT Wells score

    ASSESS

    +

    Assess the general medical history and examine to exclude other causes (1.1.1). If DVT is suspected, use the 2-level DVT Wells score to estimate clinical probability (1.1.2): likely is 2 points or more; unlikely is 1 point or less.

    2

    DVT likely: proximal leg vein ultrasound within 4 hours

    URGENT

    +

    Wells 2 or more: offer a proximal leg vein ultrasound scan with the result available within 4 hours if possible, and a D-dimer test if the scan is negative (1.1.3). If the scan result cannot be obtained within 4 hours: D-dimer test, then interim therapeutic anticoagulation and a scan with the result available within 24 hours (1.1.4). Wells 1 or less: D-dimer with the result within 4 hours, or interim anticoagulation while awaiting it (1.1.8); if positive, scan as above (1.1.10).

    3

    Suspected PE: PERC if low suspicion, otherwise PE Wells score

    URGENT

    +

    For signs or symptoms of PE (such as chest pain, shortness of breath or coughing up blood): history, examination and chest X-ray to exclude other causes (1.1.15). If clinical suspicion is low and other diagnoses are feasible, consider the PERC rule (not validated in COVID-19) (1.1.16). If PE is suspected use the 2-level PE Wells score (1.1.17). Likely (more than 4 points): CTPA immediately if possible (1.1.18), so refer for same-day hospital assessment. Unlikely (4 points or less): D-dimer within 4 hours if possible, or interim anticoagulation while awaiting the result (1.1.21).

    4

    Interim and confirmed anticoagulation

    DRUG

    +

    If interim therapeutic anticoagulation is needed, choose an anticoagulant that can be continued if DVT or PE is confirmed (1.3.3). Take baseline blood tests (full blood count, renal and hepatic function, PT and APTT) but do not wait for results before starting; review them within 24 hours (1.3.4). For confirmed proximal DVT or PE, offer apixaban or rivaroxaban (1.3.8), for at least 3 months (1.3.5), taking into account comorbidities, contraindications and preferences (1.3.7). Local (Sussex Formulary, read 24 November 2026): apixaban note "GREEN – for DVT / PE / AF"; rivaroxaban note "GREEN – DVT / PE / AF"; both entries carry black triangle and MHRA warning icons.

    5

    Negative tests and follow-up

    REVIEW/LIMIT

    +

    Negative scan and negative D-dimer: stop interim anticoagulation (but not long-term secondary prevention), think about alternative diagnoses and tell the person DVT is not likely (1.1.7). Negative scan with positive D-dimer: repeat the scan 6 to 8 days later (1.1.6). For outpatient treatment of low-risk PE, agree monitoring and follow-up and give written information on symptoms to look out for and direct contact details of a thrombosis team (1.2.4).

    Suspected sepsis in people aged 16 or over: community risk and action

    New pathway drafted 24 November 2026 from the cited UK guidance, opened that day; recommendation numbers are given in each step. Every step phrase and number was checked by automated comparison with the cited source. Not clinically validated.

    1

    Think 'could this be sepsis?'

    URGENT

    +

    Think 'could this be sepsis?' if a person has symptoms or signs of possible infection (1.1.1). Presentations may be non-specific, for example feeling very unwell, and there may be no high temperature (1.1.2). Pay particular attention to concerns from the person, family or carers, such as changes from usual behaviour (1.1.3), and take extra care when the history is difficult to obtain (1.1.4). Identify the likely source, risk factors and new abnormalities of behaviour, circulation or respiration (1.1.5). NG253 replaced NG51 for this age group.

    2

    Grade risk with the community criteria (table 1)

    ASSESS

    +

    In the community, grade risk from the history, examination and the age-based criteria in table 1, for people aged 16 or over who are not and have not recently been pregnant (1.5.1). High risk includes: objective evidence of new altered mental state; respiratory rate 25 breaths per minute or more; new need for oxygen (40% FiO2 or more) to keep saturation above 92% (88% in chronic hypercapnic respiratory failure); systolic blood pressure 90 mmHg or less, or more than 40 mmHg below normal; heart rate more than 130 beats per minute; not passed urine in the previous 18 hours; mottled or ashen appearance; cyanosis of skin, lips or tongue; non-blanching petechial or purpuric rash. Medium risk includes: respiratory rate 21 to 24; systolic 91 to 100 mmHg; heart rate 91 to 130 or new arrhythmia; no urine for 12 to 18 hours; tympanic temperature less than 36°C; impaired immunity; trauma, surgery or invasive procedure in the last 6 weeks; new altered behaviour reported by others; acute deterioration in function (table 1). No high or medium criteria: low risk (1.5.3).

    3

    Any high-risk criterion: emergency transfer with pre-alert

    URGENT

    +

    Refer for emergency medical care using the most appropriate transport, usually a 999 ambulance (1.7.1), pre-alert secondary care and transfer immediately (1.7.2). In remote and rural locations where transfer routinely takes more than 1 hour, GPs should have mechanisms to give antibiotics to people with high-risk criteria before transfer (1.7.7).

    4

    Medium risk: definitive diagnosis or refer urgently

    ASSESSREFER

    +

    Assess people who meet any medium-risk criterion to make a definitive diagnosis and decide whether their condition can be treated safely outside hospital; if no definitive diagnosis is reached, or it cannot be treated safely outside an acute hospital, refer urgently for emergency care (1.7.9).

    5

    Low risk: safety-net

    REVIEW/LIMIT

    +

    For people with suspected sepsis who meet no high-risk or medium-risk criteria, give information about symptoms to monitor and how to access medical care if they are concerned (1.7.10).

    1

    Injury or incomplete recovery: act first

    URGENT

    +

    If the person has sustained an injury or has not made a full recovery of consciousness, use clinical judgement to decide management and urgency (1.1.1.1). If TLoC is secondary to a condition needing immediate action, manage that first (1.1.4.1).

    2

    Event history, examination and 12-lead ECG for everyone

    ASSESS

    +

    From the person and witnesses (phone witnesses who are not present), record circumstances, posture, prodromal symptoms, appearance and colour, movements and their duration, tongue-biting (side or tip), injury, duration and recovery, including confusion or weakness down 1 side (1.1.1.2). If uncertain, assume TLoC until proven otherwise (1.1.1.4). Record previous episodes, medical and family history of cardiac disease, medicines that may have contributed (for example, diuretics), vital signs, lying and standing blood pressure if appropriate, and cardiovascular and neurological signs (1.1.2.1). Record a 12-lead ECG with automated interpretation (1.1.2.2).

    3

    Red flags: refer for cardiovascular assessment within 24 hours

    URGENTREFER

    +

    Refer urgently, with the referral reviewed by a specialist within 24 hours, anyone with TLoC and: an ECG abnormality (conduction abnormality, long or short QT, any ST segment or T wave abnormality); heart failure; TLoC during exertion; family history of sudden cardiac death under 40 years and/or an inherited cardiac condition; new or unexplained breathlessness; or a heart murmur. Consider the same for anyone older than 65 years with TLoC without prodromal symptoms (1.1.4.2).

    4

    Uncomplicated faint, situational syncope or postural hypotension

    ASSESS

    +

    Diagnose uncomplicated faint when nothing suggests another diagnosis and there are the 3 'P's: posture (prolonged standing), provoking factors (such as pain or a procedure), prodromal symptoms (such as sweating or feeling warm) (1.1.4.3); brief seizure-like activity can occur in a faint. Situational syncope: clearly provoked by micturition, coughing or swallowing (1.1.4.4). With either, and no clinical or social concern, no further immediate management is needed (1.1.4.5). Suspect postural hypotension when the history is typical and measure it as in the NICE hypertension guideline (1.2.1.1).

    5

    Suspected epilepsy or unexplained TLoC: refer

    REVIEW/LIMITREFER

    +

    Refer for an epilepsy specialist to see the person within 2 weeks if there is a bitten tongue, head-turning to 1 side, no memory of witnessed abnormal behaviour, unusual posturing, prolonged limb-jerking, confusion after the event, or prodromal déjà vu or jamais vu (1.2.2.1); do not routinely use EEG. Refer everyone else with TLoC for specialist cardiovascular assessment, except those firmly diagnosed with uncomplicated faint, situational syncope or postural hypotension, or whose presentation strongly suggests epilepsy (1.2.3.1).
