    1

    Could this be cancer or another serious cause?

    URGENT

    +

    If cancer is suspected, follow the NICE suspected cancer guideline (1.3.2). Ask about related symptoms such as persistent intermenstrual bleeding, pelvic pain and/or pressure symptoms, which suggest uterine cavity or histological abnormality, adenomyosis or fibroids (1.2.1).

    2

    History, examination and blood tests

    ASSESS

    +

    History: nature of the bleeding, related symptoms, impact on quality of life and other factors affecting treatment (1.2.1). HMB without related symptoms: consider drug treatment without examination, unless an LNG-IUS is chosen (1.2.3). With related symptoms: offer a physical examination (1.2.4); examine before any investigation or LNG-IUS fitting (1.2.5). Full blood count for all women with HMB, in parallel with treatment (1.2.6). Consider coagulation testing if HMB since periods started plus a personal or family history suggesting a coagulation disorder (1.2.7). Do not test female hormones (1.2.9), or thyroid function unless there are other features of thyroid disease (1.2.10).

    3

    When to investigate the cause

    ASSESS

    +

    Consider starting drug treatment without investigation if history and examination suggest low risk of fibroids, uterine cavity or histological abnormality, or adenomyosis (1.3.1). Outpatient hysteroscopy if submucosal fibroids, polyps or endometrial pathology are suggested, for example persistent intermenstrual bleeding or risk factors for endometrial pathology (1.3.4). Pelvic ultrasound if the uterus is palpable abdominally, a pelvic mass is suggested, or examination is inconclusive or difficult (1.3.12). Transvaginal ultrasound if there is significant dysmenorrhoea or a bulky, tender uterus suggesting adenomyosis (1.3.13). No blind endometrial biopsy (1.3.11).

    4

    Treatment: LNG-IUS first, then other options

    DRUG

    +

    Agree treatment taking into account preferences, comorbidities, fibroids (size, number, location), polyps, endometrial pathology, adenomyosis and other symptoms (1.5.1). No identified pathology, fibroids less than 3 cm not distorting the cavity, or suspected or diagnosed adenomyosis: consider an LNG-IUS first (1.5.2). If declined or unsuitable: tranexamic acid or NSAIDs (non-hormonal), or combined hormonal contraception or cyclical oral progestogens (hormonal) (1.5.3). Progestogen-only contraception may suppress menstruation (1.5.5). BNF tranexamic acid for menorrhagia: 1 g 3 times a day for up to 4 days, started once menstruation has started; can be increased if necessary up to a maximum of 4 g per day. Before starting, exclude structural or histological causes or fibroids distorting the uterine cavity, and establish the cause of irregular bleeding. Contraindicated with thromboembolic disease or a history of convulsions; caution with oral contraceptives (increased risk of thrombosis); reduce the dose in renal impairment; stop if colour vision changes. BNF mefenamic acid for menorrhagia: 500 mg 3 times a day (adults and 12–17 years); contraindicated with active or previous NSAID-related gastro-intestinal bleeding or ulceration, inflammatory bowel disease, severe heart failure and aspirin or NSAID hypersensitivity (including NSAID-triggered asthma); avoid in the third trimester and avoid in the first and second trimesters unless essential (MHRA, June 2023); avoid in severe renal or hepatic impairment; can cause convulsions in overdose. BNF norethisterone for menorrhagia: to arrest bleeding, 5 mg 3 times a day for 10 days; to prevent bleeding, 5 mg twice daily from day 19 to day 26 of the cycle; contraindicated (non-contraceptive use) with a history of thromboembolism or undiagnosed vaginal bleeding, and with current breast cancer and acute porphyrias; caution with risk factors for venous thromboembolism; masculinisation of female fetuses reported. These non-contraceptive doses are not contraceptive (site owner note; not stated on the BNF page). Combined hormonal contraception doses are not covered here. Local (Sussex Formulary, read 24 November 2026): levonorgestrel intrauterine systems "Prescribe by brand" (MHRA warning icon); tranexamic acid 500mg tablets listed. Sussex Formulary status and line preference (AI-reviewed decision, awaiting clinician sign-off; read from the formulary page text on 24 November 2026; check current formulary): levonorgestrel intrauterine system: Specialist recommendation (or initiation where noted) (no line preference shown); tranexamic acid: Mixed Status – see individual drug entries (no line preference shown).

    5

    Refer if treatment fails, symptoms are severe, or fibroids 3 cm or more

    REVIEW/LIMITREFER

    +

    If treatment is unsuccessful, declined or symptoms are severe, consider referral to specialist care for investigation and alternative treatments, including surgical options (1.5.6). Consider referral for fibroids of 3 cm or more in diameter (1.5.8). Pain with HMB may be endometriosis rather than adenomyosis (1.3.15).

    1

    Unstable or severe pain or bleeding: A&E now

    URGENT

    +

    Refer women who are haemodynamically unstable, or in whom there is significant concern about the degree of pain or bleeding, directly to A&E (1.4.1). Signs include tachycardia (more than 100 beats per minute) or hypotension (less than 100/60 mmHg), shock or collapse (1.4.4).

    2

    Think ectopic: pregnancy test and look for its features

    ASSESS

    +

    Atypical presentation is common (1.4.2). Symptoms include abdominal or pelvic pain, amenorrhoea or missed period, vaginal bleeding; also breast tenderness, gastrointestinal symptoms, dizziness or syncope, shoulder tip pain, urinary symptoms, passage of tissue, rectal pressure or pain on defecation (1.4.3). Signs include pelvic, adnexal or abdominal tenderness, cervical motion tenderness, rebound tenderness, pallor (1.4.4). Think about a pregnancy test in women of reproductive age even with non-specific symptoms (1.4.5). Exclude ectopic even without risk factors: about a third have none (1.4.8).

    3

    Refer to the early pregnancy assessment service

    URGENTREFER

    +

    Positive test with pain and abdominal, pelvic or cervical motion tenderness: refer immediately to the early pregnancy assessment service (or out-of-hours gynaecology) (1.4.7). Bleeding or other symptoms with pain, or a pregnancy of 6 weeks or more, or of uncertain gestation: refer, with urgency depending on the situation (1.4.9). Services should allow attendance within 24 hours when warranted (1.3.4). Explain why she is referred and what to expect (1.4.12). Women with recurrent miscarriage or a previous ectopic or molar pregnancy can self-refer (1.3.3).

    4

    Under 6 weeks, bleeding without pain: expectant management

    REVIEW/LIMIT

    +

    Less than 6 weeks, bleeding but no pain and no risk factors (such as previous ectopic): expectant management. Advise her to return if bleeding continues or pain develops, to repeat a urine pregnancy test after 7 to 10 days and return if positive; a negative test means the pregnancy has miscarried (1.4.10). Worsening symptoms suggesting ectopic: refer, seen immediately or within 24 hours depending on the situation (1.4.11).

    5

    Support after a complication or loss

    SUPPORT

    +

    Treat women with dignity and respect, give information and support sensitively, and recognise the distress early pregnancy complications can cause for women and partners (1.1.1–1.1.2). After an early pregnancy loss, offer a follow-up appointment with a healthcare professional of her choice (1.1.3).

    Nausea and vomiting in pregnancy and hyperemesis gravidarum

    New pathway drafted 24 November 2026 from the cited UK guidance, opened that day; recommendation numbers are given in each step. Every step phrase and number was checked by automated comparison with the cited source. Not clinically validated.

    1

    Severe vomiting or dehydration: think hyperemesis gravidarum

    URGENT

    +

    Hyperemesis gravidarum (HG) is much worse than normal pregnancy sickness: prolonged and severe nausea and vomiting, dehydration (thirsty, tired, dizzy or lightheaded, not peeing much, dark strong-smelling urine) and weight loss, and it may not improve by 16 to 20 weeks (NHS). Consider inpatient care if vomiting is severe and not responding to primary care or outpatient management, including women with hyperemesis gravidarum (NG201 1.4.7); see the RCOG guideline and venous thromboembolism advice.

    2

    Mild to moderate: reassure and non-drug options

    SUPPORT

    +

    Reassure that mild to moderate nausea and vomiting are common and likely to resolve before 16 to 20 weeks (1.4.1). Recognise she may already have tried several interventions (1.4.2). If she prefers a non-pharmacological option, suggest ginger (1.4.3).

    3

    Antiemetic by shared decision

    DRUG

    +

    Discuss the advantages and disadvantages of different antiemetics, taking into account her preferences and experience in previous pregnancies (1.4.4), and offer an antiemetic if she chooses drug treatment (1.4.5). Ondansetron in the first trimester is associated with a small increased risk of cleft lip and/or palate (about an additional 3 oral clefts per 10,000 births) (MHRA, January 2020): discuss this before prescribing in the first 12 weeks. BNF ondansetron: the indications listed are chemotherapy or radiotherapy and postoperative nausea and vomiting, with no dose listed for pregnancy; the manufacturer advises avoiding it in the first trimester, while the MHRA advises that if there is a clinical need in pregnancy, benefits and risks are discussed and the final decision made jointly (source difference shown, not resolved). Contraindicated in congenital long QT syndrome; caution with susceptibility to QT-interval prolongation, including electrolyte disturbances; maximum 8 mg daily in moderate to severe hepatic impairment. BNF doses (adults). Doxylamine/pyridoxine: 20/20 mg at bedtime for 2 days, increased if necessary to 10/10 mg in the morning and 20/20 mg at bedtime, then if necessary 10/10 mg in the morning, 10/10 mg mid-afternoon and 20/20 mg at bedtime (maximum 40/40 mg per day); caution with asthma, increased intra-ocular pressure, narrow angle glaucoma, bladder neck or pyloroduodenal obstruction and stenosing peptic ulcer; drowsiness and dizziness may affect driving; SMC (2019) and AWMSG (2019) did not recommend it and the BNF lists no NICE TA (Scottish and Welsh funding decisions; the Sussex Formulary lists it first line). Cyclizine: 50 mg up to 3 times a day. Prochlorperazine (prevention of nausea and vomiting): 5–10 mg 2–3 times a day by mouth; extrapyramidal effects and withdrawal have occasionally been reported in newborns after use in the third trimester. Promethazine hydrochloride: no separate pregnancy dose is given; the dose listed for nausea, vomiting, vertigo and motion sickness is 20–25 mg once daily at bedtime. Cyclizine and promethazine: manufacturers advise avoiding in pregnancy, although there is no evidence of teratogenicity; use late in the third trimester may affect the newborn. Metoclopramide: 10 mg up to 3 times a day (body weight 60 kg and above; under 60 kg, up to 500 micrograms/kg daily in 3 divided doses) for a maximum of 5 days (MHRA, August 2013); acute dystonic reactions are more common in young women; contraindicated with epilepsy and gastro-intestinal obstruction, perforation or haemorrhage; not known to be harmful in pregnancy. Domperidone: 10 mg up to 3 times a day (body weight 35 kg and above), usually for no more than 1 week; contraindicated with cardiac disease or impaired cardiac conduction, prolactinoma, and suspected or confirmed phaeochromocytoma (MHRA, July 2026); in pregnancy use only if the potential benefit outweighs the risk. Local (Sussex Formulary, read 24 November 2026): chapter 17 lists cyclizine, doxylamine 10mg / pyridoxine 10mg, prochlorperazine and promethazine ("Additional 1st line drug please refer to the Management of Nausea and Vomiting in Pregnancy and Hyperemesis Gravidarum guidance"), domperidone, metoclopramide (MHRA warning icon) and ondansetron. The linked local guidance is on the NHS Sussex intranet and was not readable from here. Sussex Formulary status and line preference (AI-reviewed decision, awaiting clinician sign-off; read from the formulary page text on 24 November 2026; check current formulary): cyclizine: Non-Specialist Drugs (first line); doxylamine/pyridoxine: Non-Specialist Drugs (first line); prochlorperazine: Non-Specialist Drugs (first line); promethazine: Non-Specialist Drugs (first line); domperidone: Non-Specialist Drugs (second line); metoclopramide: Non-Specialist Drugs (second line); ondansetron: Non-Specialist Drugs (second line).

    4

    Moderate to severe: fluids and escalation

    REVIEW/LIMIT

    +

    Moderate to severe: consider intravenous fluids, ideally as an outpatient, and acupressure as an adjunct (1.4.6). Not responding to primary care or outpatient management: consider inpatient care (1.4.7). Advise her to see her GP or midwife if nausea and vomiting are severe (NHS).
