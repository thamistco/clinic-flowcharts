    1

    Rectal or anal mass, or anal ulceration: consider direct referral

    URGENTREFER

    +

    Consider a suspected cancer pathway referral for a rectal mass (1.3.5), or for an unexplained anal mass or unexplained anal ulceration (1.3.6). FIT is not needed before referral is considered for these people (1.3.1).

    2

    Who should have FIT

    ASSESS

    +

    Offer quantitative FIT (HM-JACKarc or OC-Sensor) to guide referral in adults with: an abdominal mass; a change in bowel habit; iron-deficiency anaemia; aged 40 and over with unexplained weight loss and abdominal pain; aged under 50 with rectal bleeding and either unexplained abdominal pain or weight loss; aged 50 and over with unexplained rectal bleeding, abdominal pain or weight loss; aged 60 and over with anaemia even without iron deficiency. Offer FIT even after a previous negative screening FIT (1.3.1).

    3

    FIT 10 or more: suspected cancer pathway referral

    URGENTREFER

    +

    Refer adults using a suspected cancer pathway referral for colorectal cancer if FIT is at least 10 micrograms of haemoglobin per gram of faeces (1.3.2).

    4

    FIT below 10 or not returned: safety-net, do not delay if concerned

    REVIEW/LIMIT

    +

    If no sample is returned or FIT is below 10: safety-netting processes should be in place, and referral should not be delayed if there is strong clinical concern because of ongoing unexplained symptoms such as an abdominal mass (1.3.3). Consider whether the person needs help or support to return the sample (1.3.4). Explain which symptoms to look out for and when to return (1.14.6); results should be reviewed and acted on, aware of false-negative faecal occult blood tests (1.15.1).

    1

    Erythroderma or generalised pustular psoriasis: same day

    URGENT

    +

    Refer people with generalised pustular psoriasis or erythroderma immediately for same-day specialist assessment and treatment (1.2.1.11). Refer children and young people with any type of psoriasis to a specialist at presentation (1.2.1.12).

    2

    Assess severity, impact and joints

    ASSESS

    +

    Assess severity (static Physician's Global Assessment, body surface area, nails, high-impact sites such as face, scalp, palms, soles, flexures and genitals, and any systemic upset), impact on wellbeing, psoriatic arthritis and comorbidities (1.2.1.1–1.2.1.3). Offer annual assessment for psoriatic arthritis using a validated tool such as PEST (which does not detect axial arthritis) (1.2.2.1–1.2.2.2); refer to a rheumatologist as soon as it is suspected (1.2.2.3).

    3

    Topical treatment by site

    DRUG

    +

    Trunk and limbs (adults): a potent corticosteroid once daily plus vitamin D or an analogue once daily, applied separately (one morning, one evening), for up to 4 weeks (1.3.2.1); if not controlled after a maximum of 8 weeks, vitamin D or an analogue alone twice daily (1.3.2.2). Scalp: a potent corticosteroid once daily for up to 4 weeks (1.3.3.1). Face, flexures or genitals: a short-term mild or moderate potency corticosteroid once or twice daily for a maximum of 2 weeks (1.3.4.1). Do not use potent corticosteroids continuously for longer than 8 weeks, or very potent ones for longer than 4 weeks, at any site; no very potent corticosteroids in children and young people (1.3.1.13–1.3.1.15). Local (Sussex Formulary, read 24 November 2026): calcipotriol "Maximum application: Adults: 100g weekly"; calcipotriol + betamethasone (Dovobet) "should not exceed 30% of total body surface" and "should not normally exceed 4 weeks". Sussex Formulary status and line preference (AI-reviewed decision, awaiting clinician sign-off; read from the formulary page text on 24 November 2026; check current formulary): calcipotriol: Non-Specialist Drugs (no line preference shown); calcipotriol + betamethasone: Non-Specialist Drugs (second line).

    4

    Review, safety and dermatology referral

    REVIEW/LIMITREFER

    +

    Offer at least annual review to adults using intermittent or short-term potent or very potent corticosteroids, to check for atrophy and other adverse effects (1.3.1.16). Refer for dermatology advice if the diagnosis is uncertain, psoriasis is severe or extensive (for example more than 10% of body surface area), cannot be controlled with topical treatment, acute guttate psoriasis needs phototherapy, nail disease has a major impact, or psoriasis has a major impact on wellbeing (1.2.1.10).

    Alcohol: identification, assessment, withdrawal and treatment

    New pathway drafted 24 November 2026 from the cited UK guidance, opened that day; recommendation numbers are given in each step. Every step phrase and number was checked by automated comparison with the cited source. Not clinically validated.

    1

    Acute withdrawal at risk of seizures or delirium tremens: admit

    URGENT

    +

    For people in acute alcohol withdrawal with, or at high risk of, withdrawal seizures or delirium tremens, offer admission to hospital for medically assisted withdrawal (CG100 1.1.1.1). Under 16s in acute withdrawal: offer admission (1.1.1.2). Offer thiamine to people at high risk of, or with suspected, Wernicke's encephalopathy, in doses toward the upper end of the BNF range (1.2.1.1). BNF thiamine by mouth (adults): mild deficiency 25–100 mg daily; severe deficiency 200–300 mg daily in divided doses. Parenteral thiamine (MHRA/CHM 2007): rare serious allergic reactions should not stop its use where needed, particularly in people at risk of Wernicke-Korsakoff syndrome; give intravenously by infusion over 30 minutes, with facilities for treating anaphylaxis available.

    2

    Identify and assess

    ASSESS

    +

    Be competent to identify harmful (high-risk) drinking and dependence, or refer to a service that can assess need (CG115 1.2.1.2). Assess risk to self (including unplanned withdrawal, suicidality and neglect) and to others (1.2.1.1). Use formal tools: AUDIT for identification, SADQ or LDQ for severity of dependence, CIWA-Ar for withdrawal, APQ for problems (1.2.1.4). Adjust dependence criteria for women, older people, young people and people with liver disease (1.2.1.5). Consider a comprehensive assessment for adults referred to specialist services who score more than 15 on the AUDIT (1.2.2.6).

    3

    Motivational intervention and planned withdrawal

    SUPPORT

    +

    Carry out a motivational intervention for everyone who misuses alcohol (CG115 1.3.1.1). Drinking over 15 units per day and/or AUDIT 20 or more: consider a community-based assisted withdrawal, or specialist assessment if there are safety concerns (1.3.4.1). Consider inpatient or residential withdrawal if, for example, drinking over 30 units per day, SADQ more than 30, history of epilepsy or of withdrawal seizures or delirium tremens, concurrent benzodiazepine withdrawal, or 15 to 30 units per day with significant comorbidities (1.3.4.5). Offer prophylactic oral thiamine to harmful or dependent drinkers who are malnourished or at risk, or have decompensated liver disease (CG100 1.2.1.2).

    4

    Relapse prevention medicines

    DRUG

    +

    For harmful drinkers and mild dependence not responding to psychological interventions alone, or on request, consider acamprosate or oral naltrexone with an individual psychological intervention (CG115). Work with specialist alcohol services. BNF acamprosate (maintenance of abstinence, adults 18–65 years): body weight 60 kg and above, 666 mg 3 times a day; under 60 kg, 666 mg at breakfast and 333 mg at midday and at night. Avoid if serum creatinine is greater than 120 micromol/litre and when breastfeeding; in pregnancy avoid unless the potential benefit outweighs the risk; caution in severe hepatic failure. BNF naltrexone (relapse prevention in formerly alcohol-dependent people, initiated under specialist supervision): 25 mg once daily on the first day, then 50 mg daily if tolerated (the 25 mg starting dose is unlicensed). Contraindicated in people currently dependent on opioids: test for opioid dependence with naloxone before treatment; liver function tests before and during treatment; avoid in severe hepatic or renal impairment, acute hepatitis and when breastfeeding; warn that trying to overcome the opioid blockade could cause acute opioid intoxication. Local (Sussex Formulary, read 24 November 2026): acamprosate 333mg listed; naltrexone is "NON-FORMULARY in East Sussex" (PURPLE in Coastal West Sussex and Brighton & Hove, RED in Crawley, Horsham and Mid-Sussex). Sussex Formulary status and line preference (AI-reviewed decision, awaiting clinician sign-off; read from the formulary page text on 24 November 2026; check current formulary): acamprosate: Specialist recommendation (or initiation where noted) (no line preference shown); naltrexone: Mixed Status – see individual drug entries (no line preference shown).

    Stopping smoking: advice, support and treatment options

    New pathway drafted 24 November 2026 from the cited UK guidance, opened that day; recommendation numbers are given in each step. Every step phrase and number was checked by automated comparison with the cited source. Not clinically validated.

    1

    Ask at every opportunity and advise

    ASSESS

    +

    At every opportunity, ask whether people smoke or have recently stopped (1.11.1). If they smoke, advise them to stop in a way sensitive to their needs, that stopping in one go is the best approach, and how stop-smoking support helps (1.11.2). Discuss aids used before, including personally purchased products (1.11.3), and advise on over-the-counter NRT and nicotine-containing e-cigarettes (1.11.4).

    2

    Options and behavioural support

    SUPPORT

    +

    Tell people a range of interventions is available and refer to stop-smoking support if appropriate (1.12.1). Options include behavioural support, very brief advice, cytisinicline, NRT (short and long acting), varenicline, bupropion, nicotine-containing e-cigarettes and the Allen Carr Easyway seminar (1.12.2). Offer behavioural support whichever option is chosen, except with Allen Carr Easyway (1.12.6). Discuss options taking into account preferences, circumstances, medicines, contraindications and previous experience (1.12.7).

    3

    Most effective options and age limits

    DRUG

    +

    With behavioural support, more likely to succeed: cytisinicline, a combination of short-acting and long-acting NRT, varenicline, or nicotine-containing e-cigarettes (1.12.8). Less likely: bupropion, or short-acting or long-acting NRT used alone (1.12.9). Do not offer cytisinicline, varenicline or bupropion to people under 18 (1.12.4); do not offer cytisinicline to people aged 66 and over (1.12.5). Prescribe or provide before the quit attempt and agree a quit date (1.12.10). BNF doses (adults). Varenicline: 500 micrograms once daily for 3 days, then 500 micrograms twice daily for 4 days, then 1 mg twice daily for 11 weeks (reduce to 500 micrograms twice daily if not tolerated), usually started 1–2 weeks before the target stop date; the 12-week course can be repeated in abstinent people; creatinine clearance less than 30 mL/minute: 500 micrograms once daily, increased after 3 days to 1 mg once daily; avoid in pregnancy and breastfeeding; caution with psychiatric illness, cardiovascular disease or a predisposition to seizures; consider tapering at the end of the course. Cytisinicline (adults 18–65 years): reducing course from 1.5 mg every 2 hours (maximum 9 mg per day) on days 1 to 3 down to 1.5–3 mg per day on days 21 to 25; smoking should be stopped no later than day 5; contraindicated with arrhythmias, recent myocardial infarction, recent stroke or unstable angina; highly effective contraception, plus a barrier method if using hormonal contraception; avoid in pregnancy and breastfeeding. Bupropion: 150 mg daily for 6 days, then 150 mg twice daily (at least 8 hours between doses) for 7–9 weeks, starting 1–2 weeks before the target stop date; stop if not abstinent at 7 weeks; maximum 150 mg daily in older people and in renal impairment, mild to moderate hepatic impairment or seizure risk factors (consider); contraindicated with a history of seizures, eating disorders, bipolar disorder, CNS tumour, acute alcohol or benzodiazepine withdrawal, or severe hepatic cirrhosis; MHRA (November 2020): risk of serotonin syndrome with SSRIs, SNRIs and other serotonergic drugs, so do not exceed the recommended dose; monitor blood pressure before and during treatment. NRT doses: BNF not yet supplied.
