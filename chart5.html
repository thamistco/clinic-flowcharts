    The polysymptomatic patient: assess, prioritise, review

    Checked against the cited sources by automated comparison, November 2026. Steps labelled Direction-supported follow the cited sources' direction in this chart's own wording, not the sources' verbatim text. Not clinically validated.

    1

    Check what cannot wait

    URGENT

    +

    Adults presenting with several physical symptoms, whether new, recurrent or persistent; multiple symptoms are a presentation, not a diagnosis of medically unexplained symptoms; physical disease, medicine effects, functional symptoms and mental-health problems may coexist; not a paediatric pathway. Assess clinical stability and symptom-specific warning features before negotiating a routine agenda. Act on acute deterioration or an urgent physical, mental-health or safeguarding concern. Use the relevant emergency/urgent pathway; do not delay it to finish this chart. This is not an exhaustive red-flag checklist; urgency depends on the actual presentation, observations and clinical judgement.

    2

    Hear the whole agenda, then agree priorities

    ASSESS

    +

    Let the person describe their symptoms and ask what else they wanted to discuss. Clarify their main worry, desired help and effect on life. Summarise the full list, explain what can safely be covered today, and agree follow-up for remaining concerns rather than dismissing them.

    3

    Build one timeline, not disconnected complaints

    ASSESS

    +

    Map onset, course, triggers, associated symptoms and changes from baseline. Review prior records, examinations, tests and referrals before duplicating them. Ask what previous results meant to the person. Reassess new findings even if earlier tests were normal.

    4

    Explore function and context without assuming cause

    ASSESS

    +

    Ask about sleep, daily activity, work/caring roles, mood, anxiety, stressors, support and coping. Explore sensitive issues with permission. Physical and psychological problems can interact; neither distress nor normal tests proves that a symptom is psychological.

    5

    Check medicines and treatment burden

    ASSESS

    +

    Reconcile prescribed, over-the-counter and other substances, recent changes, adverse effects, adherence and allergies. Where multimorbidity applies, review benefits, harms and the burden of multiple appointments. Agree changes safely; do not stop medicines solely because several symptoms are present.

    6

    Examine and test to answer a clinical question

    ASSESS

    +

    Choose observations, examination and investigations from the history, differential and relevant condition guidance. Explain what each test can and cannot show and who will act on results. There is no single blood panel that diagnoses or excludes every cause of multiple symptoms.

    7

    Keep the differential open

    ASSESS

    +

    Consider a unifying condition, several coexisting conditions and medicine effects. Use the relevant diagnostic pathway when a recognisable pattern emerges. A normal investigation is not itself a diagnosis of functional symptoms, fibromyalgia, ME/CFS or chronic primary pain.

    8

    Do not miss non-specific cancer features

    URGENT

    +

    Apply current NG12 to the actual symptoms, age and findings. Its 2026 recommendation for age 60+ with unexplained weight loss greater than 5% over 6 months calls for further assessment and urgent investigation or an appropriate cancer/non-specific-symptoms referral. Unexplained appetite loss has a separate recommendation. Younger age is not reassurance against other concerning features. Use the full source; this box does not replace other cancer criteria or safety-netting.

    9

    Explain findings and uncertainty honestly

    TREATMENT

    +

    Acknowledge that symptoms are real and disruptive. Explain the working assessment, what has and has not been ruled out, and what happens next. When chronic pain is present, avoid making normal results sound as though the experience is invalid. Do not promise that further investigation will never be needed.

    10

    Agree a function-focused, tailored plan

    TREATMENT

    +

    Treat identified conditions and agree achievable goals, support and review. Offer relevant psychological care by indication and preference, not as proof symptoms are imagined. Avoid blanket exercise advice: if post-exertional malaise suggests ME/CFS, follow NG206 and do not prescribe fixed incremental exercise increases.

    11

    Refer for a reason, not a symptom count

    TREATMENTREFER

    +

    Choose a service to answer a clinical question: urgent disease assessment, diagnostic uncertainty or a condition-specific specialist need. Do not automatically send every multisymptom patient to rheumatology or mental health. Explain the purpose, urgency, interim plan and what to do while waiting; use advice-and-guidance only if appropriate, never instead of indicated urgent care.

    12

    Close the loop and book continuity

    MONITORING

    +

    Agree who reviews unresolved symptoms, test results, treatment effects and referrals, and when. Give specific warning signs and an access route for worsening or non-response. Record what was deferred, the reason and next appointment. Reassess changed symptoms rather than labelling every new problem as the old condition.

    SCA consultation and real-world admin

    Checked against the cited sources by automated comparison, November 2026. Steps labelled Direction-supported follow the cited sources' direction in this chart's own wording, not the sources' verbatim text. Not clinically validated.

    1

    Official format: 3 + 12 minutes

    MONITORING

    +

    A training guide for the RCGP Simulated Consultation Assessment, with a separate practical example for real GP notes and referrals; the exam structure is official, but the minute-by-minute allocation and admin estimates are suggestions to discuss with a trainer, not RCGP requirements or a pass formula. Each of 12 simulated consultations has 3 minutes of reading time followed by a 12-minute video or telephone consultation. The platform gives a one-minute warning. The 3 minutes prepare for the next case; do not count them as a post-case notes or referral-writing allowance.

    2

    Before the call: use the reading time

    ASSESS

    +

    Read every page of the brief, identify the task, relevant history, medicines and results, then form initial hypotheses and questions. Private typed notes carry into the consultation and are not visible to examiners. This is preparation, not a substitute for checking the patient's agenda.

    3

    0-1 minute: connect and set the agenda

    ASSESS

    +

    Confirm who you are speaking to and the context as appropriate. Start openly, hear the concern, notice cues and check for additional issues. Agree the focus without shutting down an urgent or important concern. Suggested coaching checkpoint, not an official RCGP time target.

    4

    1-3 minutes: story, impact and concerns

    ASSESS

    +

    Clarify symptoms or the issue presented, patient ideas, concerns and expectations. Ask about impact and the context that will change decisions. Use active listening and purposeful questions rather than running a generic checklist. Suggested coaching checkpoint; communication continues throughout.

    5

    3-6 minutes: targeted assessment

    ASSESS

    +

    Test your hypotheses with focused history, relevant red flags, background, medicines and supplied examination or test information. For remote cases, explain if an in-person examination is needed; never invent findings. Adjust the pace for complexity and safety. Suggested coaching checkpoint, not a hard stop on needed assessment.

    6

    Around 6-7 minutes: summarise and explain

    ASSESS

    +

    Check your understanding, share the working diagnosis or analysis and acknowledge uncertainty. Aim to leave enough time for a real discussion of management. The RCGP-linked overview suggests finishing data gathering around 6-7 minutes; this is educational guidance, not a standalone marking rule.

    7

    7-10 1/2 minutes: agree a workable plan

    TREATMENT

    +

    Discuss appropriate options, benefits/harms and the person's preference. Include investigations, treatment, self-care or a referral when needed. For a referral, explain why, urgency, what the service may do and the interim plan. Check feasibility, comorbidity and concerns. Suggested coaching checkpoint. Do not promise a service, shared care or waiting time you have not checked.

    8

    10 1/2-12 minutes: safety-net and follow up

    MONITORINGSAFETY-NET

    +

    Agree specific warning signs, where and when to seek help, who handles results/referrals, and planned review. Check understanding and unresolved questions. This is part of clinical management, not a rushed phrase at the buzzer. Suggested coaching checkpoint, not a mandatory 90-second closing script.

    9

    Exam notes and referrals: say the plan

    MONITORINGREFER

    +

    Use private notes sparingly without losing the conversation. The official schedule does not list a separate post-case clinical-note or referral-letter station. Communicate the proposed referral, rationale, urgency and follow-up within the consultation. Do not confuse unmarked private notes with a real clinical record.

    10

    Real notes: try a 2-3 minute allowance

    MONITORING

    +

    For a straightforward encounter, start by reserving about 2-3 minutes to check and complete a concise record, then measure and adjust with your trainer. This is an author-suggested planning estimate, not a national standard. Complex, safeguarding, interpreter or uncertainty-heavy consultations may need much longer. Record during or as soon as possible after care. Include relevant findings, assessment/uncertainty, preferences, information given, decisions/actions, review and author/time. Record only what actually happened.

    11

    Real referrals: reserve separate time

    TREATMENTREFER

    +

    A simple routine referral with records ready might need a 3-5 minute planning allowance; complex letters, forms and coordination can take longer. This estimate is not RCGP/GMC guidance. Emergency or time-critical handover happens immediately, not in a later admin block. Do not mark a referral sent when it is only planned. Check the receiving service, question, urgency, relevant history/examination/tests, medicines/allergies, patient understanding and information-sharing needs. Track submission and response.

    12

    A 20-minute real-clinic planning example

    MONITORING

    +

    An illustrative block is 12 minutes for the patient, 3 for notes and 5 for a straightforward referral. If no referral is needed, use the remaining time for other clinical work or review. This is arithmetic and a training suggestion, not an appointment-length recommendation. A multi-symptom consultation may need a longer booked review or double appointment. Do not compress necessary assessment to fit this example. Agree workload and protected admin arrangements locally; urgent care takes priority.
