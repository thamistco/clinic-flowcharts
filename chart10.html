    1

    Think of other serious diagnoses

    URGENT

    +

    Consider diagnoses other than gastroenteritis if there is: fever of 38°C or higher under 3 months, or 39°C or higher aged 3 months or older; shortness of breath or tachypnoea; altered conscious state; neck stiffness; bulging fontanelle; non-blanching rash; blood and/or mucus in stool; bilious (green) vomit; severe or localised abdominal pain; abdominal distension or rebound tenderness (1.1.1.4). Usual course: diarrhoea 5–7 days (most stop within 2 weeks), vomiting 1–2 days (most stop within 3 days) (1.1.1.3).

    2

    Assess dehydration and shock (table 1)

    ASSESS

    +

    Higher risk of dehydration: under 1 year (especially under 6 months), low birth weight, more than five diarrhoeal stools or more than two vomits in the previous 24 hours, not offered or not tolerating supplementary fluids, stopped breastfeeding, signs of malnutrition (1.2.1.2). Use table 1 (1.2.1.3). Red flags for progression to shock include: appears unwell or deteriorating; altered responsiveness (irritable, lethargic); sunken eyes; tachycardia; tachypnoea; reduced skin turgor. Clinical shock: decreased level of consciousness, pale or mottled skin, cold extremities, weak peripheral pulses, prolonged capillary refill, hypotension. If in doubt, manage as if there are red flags. Suspect hypernatraemic dehydration with jittery movements, increased muscle tone, hyperreflexia, convulsions, drowsiness or coma (1.2.1.4). Do not routinely do blood tests (1.2.2.1).

    3

    Shock or red flags: escalate

    URGENT

    +

    Remote assessment: emergency transfer for symptoms suggesting shock; face-to-face assessment for symptoms suggesting an alternative serious diagnosis, high risk of dehydration, symptoms of clinical dehydration, or social circumstances making remote assessment unreliable (1.7.1.1). Face-to-face: arrange emergency transfer for symptoms or signs suggesting shock (1.7.1.2).

    4

    Fluids: prevent and treat dehydration with ORS

    SUPPORT

    +

    No clinical dehydration: continue breastfeeding and other milk feeds, encourage fluids, discourage fruit juices and carbonated drinks, and offer ORS solution as supplemental fluid to those at increased risk (1.3.1.1). Clinical dehydration (including hypernatraemic): use low-osmolarity ORS solution (240–250 mOsm/l); give 50 ml/kg for fluid deficit replacement over 4 hours as well as maintenance fluid, frequently and in small amounts (1.3.2.2). Do not use antidiarrhoeal medications (1.6.1.1).

    5

    Safety-net and preventing spread

    REVIEW/LIMIT

    +

    Give parents and carers a 'safety net' on how to recognise developing red flag symptoms and get immediate help (1.7.1.1). Advise handwashing and not sharing towels; no school or childcare while they have diarrhoea or vomiting, and not until at least 48 hours after the last episode; no swimming pools for 2 weeks after the last episode of diarrhoea (1.8).

    Constipation in children: red flags, disimpaction and maintenance

    1

    Is it constipation? (table 1)

    ASSESS

    +

    Two or more findings from table 1 indicate constipation (1.1.1), for example fewer than three complete stools per week (not applicable to exclusively breastfed babies after 6 weeks), hard large stools, "rabbit droppings", or overflow soiling.

    2

    Red flags: do not treat, refer urgently

    URGENTREFER

    +

    If any 'red flag' is present, do not treat for constipation; refer urgently to a healthcare professional with experience in the relevant aspect of child health (1.1.3). History red flags (table 2) include: reported from birth or the first few weeks of life; failure or delay in passing meconium (more than 48 hours after birth in a term baby); 'ribbon stools'; previously unknown weakness in legs or locomotor delay; abdominal distension with vomiting. Examination red flags (table 3) include: abnormal appearance, position or patency of the anus; gross abdominal distension; abnormal lumbosacral or gluteal findings (for example sacral dimple you cannot see the bottom of, hairy patch, naevi, sinus, scoliosis); abnormal lower limb neuromuscular signs or reflexes.

    3

    Other findings to act on

    ASSESS

    +

    Faltering growth: treat for constipation and test for coeliac disease and hypothyroidism (1.1.4). Possible maltreatment: treat for constipation and follow the NICE child maltreatment guideline (1.1.5). Perianal streptococcal infection: treat the infection as well (1.1.6). Otherwise, tell the family it is idiopathic constipation and underlying causes have been excluded (1.1.7).

    4

    Disimpaction if impacted; otherwise start maintenance

    DRUG

    +

    Assess everyone for faecal impaction (overflow soiling and/or faecal mass palpable abdominally or rectally if indicated) (1.4.1); if not impacted, start maintenance therapy (1.4.2). Disimpaction: polyethylene glycol 3350 + electrolytes using an escalating dose regimen first line (BNFC doses below); add a stimulant laxative if no disimpaction after 2 weeks; substitute a stimulant (with or without lactulose) if not tolerated. Warn that soiling and abdominal pain can initially increase (1.4.3). Do not use rectal medications unless all oral medications have failed and with consent (1.4.4). Review within 1 week (1.4.8). BNFC macrogol 'paediatric' sachets for faecal impaction: 1–11 months 0.5–1 sachet daily; 12 months–4 years 2 sachets on the first day, then 4 sachets per day for 2 days, then 6 sachets per day for 2 days, then 8 sachets per day; 5–11 years 4 sachets on the first day, then increased in steps of 2 sachets per day up to a maximum of 12 sachets per day; take the daily total within 12 hours, and switch to maintenance after disimpaction. 12–17 years ('full-strength' sachets): 4 sachets on the first day, increased in steps of 2 sachets per day up to a maximum of 8 sachets per day, taken within 6 hours. Unlicensed uses include faecal impaction under 5 years and use under 1 year. Contraindicated with intestinal obstruction or perforation, paralytic ileus and severe inflammatory bowel conditions; do not use 'paediatric' sachets for impaction with impaired cardiovascular or renal function; avoid mixing directly with starch-based thickeners (MHRA, April 2021). Dissolve each 'paediatric' sachet in 62.5 mL of water. BNFC lactulose: 1–11 months 2.5 mL twice daily; 1–4 years 2.5–10 mL twice daily; 5–17 years 5–20 mL twice daily, adjusted to response (may take up to 48 hours to act); contraindicated in galactosaemia. BNFC stimulant laxatives (doses follow national guidelines and are partly unlicensed), adjusted to response: senna tablets 2–3 years 3.75–15 mg once daily, 4–5 years 3.75–30 mg, 6–17 years 7.5–30 mg; senna syrup 1 month–3 years 3.75–15 mg once daily, 4–17 years 3.75–30 mg (onset 8–12 hours); bisacodyl by mouth 4–17 years 5–20 mg at night, or by rectum 2–17 years 5–10 mg once daily; sodium picosulfate 1 month–3 years 2.5–10 mg once daily, 4–17 years 2.5–20 mg. Contraindicated with intestinal obstruction (senna and sodium picosulfate also with undiagnosed abdominal pain); prolonged or excessive use can cause hypokalaemia. MHRA (August 2020): over-the-counter stimulant laxatives should not be used in children under 12 years without a prescriber's advice. Senokot® tablets are not prescribable in NHS primary care. Local (Sussex Formulary, read 24 November 2026): macrogol is listed as Laxido and CosmoCol; "Laxido Paediatric is a POM not available OTC"; CosmoCol Paediatric note "GREEN – (Paediatric formulation) for use in Coastal West Sussex, East Sussex and Crawley, Horsham & Mid-Sussex" (RED in Brighton and Hove). Sussex Formulary status and line preference (AI-reviewed decision, awaiting clinician sign-off; read from the formulary page text on 24 November 2026; check current formulary): Laxido: Non-Specialist Drugs (no line preference shown); CosmoCol: Mixed Status – see individual drug entries (no line preference shown).

    5

    Maintenance, diet and review

    REVIEW/LIMIT

    +

    Start maintenance as soon as disimpacted, first line polyethylene glycol 3350 + electrolytes (1.4.9, 1.4.11). Continue for several weeks after regular bowel habit is established (this may take several months); do not stop abruptly, reduce gradually over months; children toilet training stay on laxatives until it is well established. Reassess frequently, ideally by the same person or team (1.4.10). Do not use dietary interventions alone first line; combine laxatives with non-punitive behavioural interventions, a balanced diet and sufficient fluids (1.5.1–1.5.2). BNFC macrogol maintenance ('paediatric' sachets): 1–11 months 0.5–1 sachet daily; 12 months–5 years 1 sachet daily; 6–11 years 2 sachets daily; in 1–2 divided doses, adjusted to produce regular soft stools (maximum 4 sachets per day). 12–17 years ('full-strength' sachets): 1 sachet 1–3 times a day, usually for up to 2 weeks, then 1 sachet 1–2 times a day.

    UTI in babies, children and young people under 16

    New pathway drafted 24 November 2026 from the cited UK guidance, opened that day; recommendation numbers are given in each step. Every step phrase and number was checked by automated comparison with the cited source. Not clinically validated.

    1

    Under 3 months, or high risk of serious illness: refer now

    URGENTREFER

    +

    Immediately refer babies under 3 months with a suspected UTI to a paediatric specialist, sending urine for urgent microscopy and culture (1.1.3, 1.2.2). Immediately refer any baby or child with a high risk of serious illness (1.2.1). Suspected sepsis: follow the NICE sepsis guideline for under 16s (1.1.8).

    2

    Who to test (table 1) and how to collect urine

    ASSESS

    +

    Test urine when symptoms and signs increase the likelihood of UTI (table 1), for example dysuria, more frequent urination, new bedwetting, foul-smelling, darker or cloudy urine, frank haematuria, fever, shivering, abdominal pain, loin or suprapubic tenderness, capillary refill longer than 3 seconds, previous confirmed UTI (1.1.1). Use a clean catch sample wherever possible, otherwise urine collection pads; not cotton wool, gauze or sanitary towels (1.1.13–1.1.15). Refrigerate or use boric acid if it cannot be cultured within 4 hours (1.1.17). If not collected at the visit, return it ideally within 24 hours (1.1.7).

    3

    Interpret the dipstick by age

    ASSESS

    +

    3 months to 3 years: if leukocyte esterase and nitrite are both negative, no antibiotics and no culture unless culture criteria apply; if either or both positive, send culture and give antibiotics (1.1.19). 3 years or older (table 2): both positive, treat as UTI; nitrite positive alone, treat if fresh sample and send culture; leukocyte esterase positive alone, send microscopy and culture and treat only with good clinical evidence of UTI; both negative, assume no UTI and explore other causes (1.1.20). Send culture for suspected upper UTI, high or intermediate risk of serious illness, under 3 months, and other listed criteria (1.1.21).

    4

    Antibiotics for lower UTI aged 3 months and over

    DRUG

    +

    Offer an immediate antibiotic, taking account of previous culture results and antibiotic use (NG109 1.1.11); upper UTI follows the pyelonephritis guideline (NG224 1.2.5). First choices (NG109 table 4): trimethoprim (if low risk of resistance) for 3 days: 3 to 5 months 4 mg/kg (maximum 200 mg per dose) or 25 mg twice a day; 6 months to 5 years 4 mg/kg or 50 mg twice a day; 6 to 11 years 4 mg/kg or 100 mg twice a day; 12 to 15 years 200 mg twice a day. Or nitrofurantoin (if eGFR 45 ml/minute or more) for 3 days: 3 months to 11 years 750 micrograms/kg four times a day; 12 to 15 years 50 mg four times a day or 100 mg modified-release twice a day. Review the choice when culture results arrive (NG109 1.1.12). Local (Sussex Formulary antimicrobial guidance, read 24 November 2026): East Sussex primary care follows the NICE and PHE summary of antimicrobial prescribing guidance, the same source as the doses above.

    5

    Imaging and follow-up

    REVIEW/LIMIT

    +

    Consider referring children over 3 months with upper UTI to a paediatric specialist (1.2.4). Atypical UTI: urinary tract ultrasound during the acute infection (1.3.1). Under 6 months with a first UTI responding to treatment: ultrasound within 6 weeks (1.3.2). Over 6 months with a first UTI responding to treatment: no routine ultrasound unless atypical (1.3.3). Do not routinely follow up children who have not had imaging (1.5.1).

    1

    Suspected eczema herpeticum: aciclovir and same-day referral

    URGENTREFER

    +

    If eczema herpeticum (widespread herpes simplex) is suspected, immediately start systemic aciclovir and refer for same-day specialist dermatological advice; start systemic antibiotics if secondary bacterial infection is also suspected (1.5.1.40). Around the eyes: systemic aciclovir and same-day ophthalmological and dermatological advice (1.5.1.41, 1.7.1.1).

    2

    Emollients always, then step treatment by severity

    SUPPORT

    +

    Use the stepped approach (table 2). Emollients are the basis of management and should always be used, even when eczema is clear (1.5.1.1). Mild: emollients plus mild-potency topical corticosteroids. Moderate: emollients, moderate-potency topical corticosteroids, topical calcineurin inhibitors, bandages. Severe: emollients, potent topical corticosteroids, topical calcineurin inhibitors, bandages, phototherapy, systemic therapy. Local (Sussex Formulary, read 24 November 2026): the emollient section links an MHRA skin cream fire hazard leaflet; share it with families.

    3

    Topical corticosteroids: potency, site and frequency

    DRUG

    +

    Match potency to severity (which may vary by site): mild for mild, moderate for moderate, potent for severe. Use mild potency for the face and neck, except short-term (3 to 5 days) moderate potency for severe flares; moderate or potent only for short periods (7 to 14 days) in axillae and groin; no very potent preparations in children without specialist dermatological advice (1.5.1.13). Apply only once or twice daily (1.5.1.14), only to areas of active eczema or eczema active within the past 48 hours (1.5.1.15). Local (Sussex Formulary, read 24 November 2026): hydrocortisone "MILD POTENCY, First Choice"; Eumovate "MODERATE POTENCY. Second line. Prescribe by brand." Sussex Formulary status and line preference (AI-reviewed decision, awaiting clinician sign-off; read from the formulary page text on 24 November 2026; check current formulary): hydrocortisone: Non-Specialist Drugs (no line preference shown); Eumovate: Non-Specialist Drugs (no line preference shown).

    4

    Infection signs and referral

    REVIEW/LIMITREFER

    +

    Teach families the signs of bacterial infection: weeping, pustules, crusts, eczema failing to respond, rapidly worsening eczema, fever, malaise (1.5.1.36); see NICE guidance on secondary bacterial infection of eczema. Refer urgently (within 2 weeks) if severe eczema has not responded to optimal topical therapy after 1 week, or treatment of infected eczema has failed (1.7.1.2). Refer for specialist advice if the diagnosis is uncertain, control is unsatisfactory (for example 1 to 2 weeks of flares per month), facial eczema has not responded, or contact allergic dermatitis is suspected (1.7.1.3).

    1

    Red flags for meningococcal disease

    URGENT

    +

    Strongly suspect meningococcal disease with: a haemorrhagic, non-blanching rash with lesions larger than 2 mm (purpura); a rapidly progressive and/or spreading non-blanching petechial or purpuric rash; or symptoms and signs of bacterial meningitis combined with a non-blanching petechial or purpuric rash (1.1.9). Do not rule it out because there is no rash (1.1.10).

    2

    Look carefully: rash and meningitis features

    ASSESS

    +

    Check all over the body, including nappy areas, and look for petechiae in the conjunctivae; rashes can be hard to see on brown, black or tanned skin; tell families a rash can change from blanching to non-blanching (1.1.12). Red flag combination for bacterial meningitis: fever, headache, neck stiffness and altered level of consciousness or cognition; fever and neck stiffness are less common in babies; look for a bulging fontanelle (table 1). Presentations can be non-specific, especially in young babies and older adults, and meningitis and sepsis can occur together (1.1.1).

    3

    Suspected: emergency transfer and pre-alert

    URGENT

    +

    Transfer people with suspected bacterial meningitis or meningococcal disease to hospital as an emergency (1.1.13, 1.2.1) and tell the hospital they will need assessment by a senior clinical decision maker (1.2.2). Do not delay transfer to give antibiotics (1.2.3).

    4

    Antibiotics before hospital

    DRUG

    +

    Strongly suspected meningococcal disease: give intravenous or intramuscular ceftriaxone or benzylpenicillin as soon as possible outside hospital, unless this will delay transfer (1.2.5). Strongly suspected bacterial meningitis with a likely clinically significant delay in transfer: give the same (1.2.4). Not if there is severe allergy to either antibiotic (1.2.6). BNFC benzylpenicillin, pre-hospital, single dose by intravenous or intramuscular injection: neonate and child 1–11 months 300 mg; child 1–9 years 600 mg; child 10–17 years 1.2 g (suspected meningococcal disease: give before urgent transfer unless this will delay it; suspected bacterial meningitis with a likely clinically significant delay: no neonatal dose is listed). Do not give a penicillin to anyone with a history of anaphylaxis, urticaria or rash immediately after a penicillin; they may also react to cephalosporins. BNF ceftriaxone, pre-hospital, single dose (child doses as listed on the BNF page; BNFC not separately supplied): by deep intramuscular injection, child 1 month 250 mg, 2–11 months 500 mg, 1–4 years 1 g, 5–8 years 1.5 g, 9–11 years 2 g; by intravenous or deep intramuscular injection, child 12–17 years and adult 2 g. Give before urgent transfer unless this will delay it, and tell the receiving hospital the dose given so the child can be weighed and the rest of the dose given if needed. In adults the maximum single intramuscular dose is 2 g, and doses over 1 g should be divided between more than one site. Do not give a cephalosporin to anyone with a history of immediate hypersensitivity to penicillin or other beta-lactams. BNF benzylpenicillin, adults: 1.2 g by intravenous or intramuscular injection as a single dose before transfer, for both indications. Check the preparation: benzylpenicillin sodium is not interchangeable with benzathine benzylpenicillin.

    5

    Not suspected now: safety-net

    REVIEW/LIMIT

    +

    If bacterial meningitis and meningococcal disease are not suspected, give safety-netting advice and ask the person to return if new symptoms develop, if a rash changes from blanching to non-blanching, or if existing symptoms get worse. Remember other serious causes such as sepsis, non-bacterial meningitis, intracranial bleed and pneumonia (1.1.17).
