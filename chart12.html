    1

    Complicated LUTS or suspected cancer: refer

    URGENTREFER

    +

    Refer for specialist assessment if LUTS are complicated by recurrent or persistent urinary tract infection, retention, renal impairment suspected to be caused by lower urinary tract dysfunction, or suspected urological cancer (1.1.14). Prostate feels malignant on digital rectal examination: suspected cancer pathway referral (NG12 1.6.1). Manage suspected prostate cancer in line with NICE guidance (1.1.6).

    2

    Initial assessment

    ASSESS

    +

    Take a general medical history and review current medicines, including herbal and over-the-counter medicines (1.1.1). Examine the abdomen and external genitalia and do a digital rectal examination (1.1.2). Ask men with bothersome LUTS to complete a frequency volume chart (1.1.3). Urine dipstick for blood, glucose, protein, leucocytes and nitrites (1.1.4). Serum creatinine and eGFR only if renal impairment is suspected (palpable bladder, nocturnal enuresis, recurrent UTIs or renal stones) (1.1.7). Use a validated symptom score such as the IPSS before treatment (1.1.15). Do not routinely offer cystoscopy, upper tract imaging, flow-rate or post-void residual measurement at initial assessment (1.1.8–1.1.11).

    3

    PSA: informed choice

    ASSESS

    +

    Offer information, advice and time to decide about PSA testing if LUTS suggest bladder outlet obstruction from benign prostate enlargement, the prostate feels abnormal on examination, or he is concerned about prostate cancer (1.1.5). See the PSA and prostate pathway for age-specific referral thresholds (NG12 1.6.2–1.6.3).

    4

    Not bothersome, or storage symptoms: conservative care

    SUPPORT

    +

    LUTS not bothersome or complicated: reassurance, lifestyle advice (for example fluid intake) and information; review if symptoms change (1.1.12). Post-micturition dribble: teach urethral milking (1.3.1). Storage symptoms suggesting overactive bladder: supervised bladder training, fluid and lifestyle advice, and containment products if needed (1.3.4). Stress incontinence: refer for specialist assessment (1.3.7).

    5

    Drug treatment when conservative care is not enough

    DRUG

    +

    Offer drugs only when conservative options have failed or are not appropriate, taking account of comorbidities and current treatment (1.4.1–1.4.2). Moderate to severe LUTS: an alpha blocker (alfuzosin, doxazosin, tamsulosin or terazosin) (1.4.3). Overactive bladder: an anticholinergic (1.4.4). Prostate estimated larger than 30 g or PSA greater than 1.4 ng/ml and high risk of progression: a 5-alpha reductase inhibitor (1.4.6), or consider combination with an alpha blocker if bothersome moderate to severe (1.4.7). Persistent storage symptoms on an alpha blocker: consider adding an anticholinergic (1.4.8). Do not offer phosphodiesterase-5 inhibitors solely for LUTS (1.4.11). Check doses and cautions in the BNF.

    6

    Review and referral

    REVIEW/LIMITREFER

    +

    Review alpha blockers at 4 to 6 weeks then every 6 to 12 months (1.4.14); 5-alpha reductase inhibitors at 3 to 6 months then every 6 to 12 months (1.4.15); anticholinergics every 4 to 6 weeks until stable, then every 6 to 12 months (1.4.16). Offer specialist referral if bothersome LUTS have not responded to conservative management or drug treatment (1.1.13).

    PSA testing and suspected prostate cancer

    1

    Malignant-feeling prostate: refer

    URGENTREFER

    +

    Refer using a suspected cancer pathway referral for prostate cancer if the prostate feels malignant on digital rectal examination (1.6.1). This applies to men, and trans women and non-binary people with male reproductive organs.

    2

    Who to test: symptoms

    ASSESS

    +

    Consider a PSA test and digital rectal examination for any lower urinary tract symptoms (such as nocturia, urinary frequency, hesitancy, urgency or retention), erectile dysfunction, or visible haematuria (1.6.2).

    3

    Informed decision before testing

    ASSESS

    +

    Routine PSA testing is not offered on the NHS (except for people with a BRCA2 gene variant); anyone with a prostate can ask a GP about it. Discuss benefits and risks, including that results may be inaccurate and lead to tests and treatment that are not needed (NHS PSA test page).

    4

    Age-specific PSA thresholds for referral

    URGENTREFER

    +

    With possible symptoms (1.6.2), consider a suspected cancer pathway referral if PSA is above the threshold for age (table 2; micrograms/litre): below 40, clinical judgement; 40 to 49, more than 2.5; 50 to 59, more than 3.5; 60 to 69, more than 4.5; 70 to 79, more than 6.5; above 79, clinical judgement. Take into account preferences and comorbidities (1.6.3).

    5

    Below threshold: discuss and safety-net

    REVIEW/LIMIT

    +

    Symptoms associated with increased cancer risk but not meeting referral criteria: consider a review, planned within an agreed time frame or patient-initiated if new symptoms develop, concern continues, or symptoms recur, persist or worsen (NG12 1.15.2); explain which symptoms to look out for and when to return (1.14.6). For people treated for a prostate condition, regular PSA tests may be offered (NHS). Use the LUTS pathway for symptom management.
