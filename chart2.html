Headache assessment and migraine

Checked against the cited sources by automated comparison, November 2026. Steps labelled Direction-supported follow the cited sources' direction in this chart's own wording, not the sources' verbatim text. Not clinically validated.

  1. Box 1URGENTEvaluate secondary causes first

    Selected warning features, not a complete screen: sudden maximum within 5 minutes, fever/worsening, new neurological/cognitive/personality change, altered consciousness, trauma, cough/Valsalva/sneeze, exertional/postural trigger, GCA/glaucoma features or major pattern change. New-onset headache has additional malignancy/immunity/vomiting criteria: open both source lists before deciding investigation/referral.

  2. Box 2ASSESSMake a positive diagnosis

    Use the original feature table for migraine/tension/cluster classification; atypical aura needs investigation/referral consideration. Consider a diary for at least 8 weeks recording pattern, associated symptoms, medicines and triggers. Imaging is not for reassurance alone after a primary-headache diagnosis.

  3. Box 3DRUGAcute migraine: choose together

    Offer oral triptan + NSAID, or triptan + paracetamol, accounting for preference and risks. If one drug preferred, consider triptan/NSAID/aspirin/paracetamol alone. Consider an antiemetic even without nausea. No opioids or ergots.

  4. Box 4DRUGPreventive treatment if appropriate

    Consider propranolol, topiramate or amitriptyline after full suitability discussion; no automatic first choice. Consider propranolol overdose risk with depression. Topiramate is not for migraine prevention in pregnancy; anyone who could become pregnant requires the Pregnancy Prevention Programme: highly effective contraception during treatment and for at least 4 weeks after stopping; topiramate can reduce the efficacy of systemic hormonal contraceptives - also use a barrier method and report bleeding-pattern changes (SmPC 4.5); give the Patient Guide; repeat pregnancy testing during treatment as needed; the PPP applies even to women not currently sexually active unless the prescriber records compelling reasons for no pregnancy risk; if pregnancy occurs on topiramate for migraine - stop straight away, contact the GP urgently, and arrange prompt specialist reassessment (MHRA/DSU; SmPC 4.6); if planning pregnancy - see the GP or specialist before stopping topiramate or contraception. Breastfeeding: decide between suspending breastfeeding and discontinuing or abstaining from topiramate, weighing the benefit of each; observed infant effects include diarrhoea, drowsiness, irritability and inadequate weight gain (SmPC 4.6). Dose framing (side by side, not decided): BNF usual 50-100 mg/day, maximum 200 mg/day; SmPC recommended total daily dose 100 mg/day, benefit seen at 50 mg/day, up to 200 mg/day received with caution about side effects. (Topamax SmPC emc 1977 wording; the 25 mg nightly 1-week start, 25 mg weekly increments and CrCl <=70 mL/min half-dose rule agree with the SmPC. Follow the dispensed product's SmPC and record which framing guided titration.)

  5. Box 5REVIEW/LIMITPrevent medication overuse

    Suspect overuse when headache develops/worsens with at least 3 months of triptan/opioid/ergot/combination use on 10+ days/month, or simple analgesic/NSAID use on 15+ days/month. Explain risk and use the full supported withdrawal/referral pathway.

  6. Box 6REVIEW/LIMITReview; escalate selected cases

    Review prevention at 3–6 months. CGRP inhibitors: stop at 12 weeks for <50% episodic or <30% chronic migraine-frequency reduction. Botulinum toxin: follow the distinct 2-cycle/episodic-conversion stopping rules. Use diary, tolerability and the full appraisal.

  7. Box 7DRUGAcute and preventive appraisal routes

    Acute: TA1172 atogepant or TA919 rimegepant after ≥2 triptans failed, or triptans unsuitable plus NSAID/paracetamol failure. Prevention: CGRP options have ≥3 preventive failures/unsuitability and frequency criteria; chronic-migraine botulinum toxin also requires medication-overuse management.

  • Topamax (topiramate) SmPC (4.2 migraine dose; 4.4 PPP; 4.5 contraceptive interaction; 4.6 pregnancy and breastfeeding)
  • MHRA DSU June 2024: topiramate Pregnancy Prevention Programme

Acne vulgaris

Checked against the cited sources by automated comparison, November 2026. Steps labelled Direction-supported follow the cited sources' direction in this chart's own wording, not the sources' verbatim text. Not clinically validated.

  1. Box 1URGENTIdentify urgent / referral needs

    Acne fulminans: same-day referral to on-call dermatology for assessment within 24 hours. Refer diagnostic uncertainty, conglobata or nodulo-cystic acne. Consider scarring, persistent pigment change and psychological distress when deciding referral.

  2. Box 2ASSESSSkin care and expectations

    Use a pH-neutral/slightly acidic syndet twice daily; avoid oil-based/comedogenic products and picking. Explain benefits may take 6-8 weeks. Topical retinoids and oral tetracyclines are contraindicated in pregnancy or when planning pregnancy.

  3. Box 3DRUGChoose a 12-week first course

    Use severity and preference to select a full-table regimen. Options include topical adapalene/benzoyl peroxide for any severity, and fixed combination topicals or oral antibiotic plus topical for moderate-severe. NICE initial combination course is 12 weeks, then review; never oral antibiotic monotherapy. Doxycycline card licence conflict (presented, not resolved): the card's 100 mg once daily is the BNF regimen (BNF doxycycline, acne vulgaris adjunct to topical treatment: 100 mg once daily), outside the product licence; the generic doxycycline 100 mg capsules SmPC (emc 13082) licenses acne vulgaris at 50 mg daily with food or fluid for 6 to 12 weeks. NG198 Table 1 names 'oral lymecycline or oral doxycycline taken once daily' with no mg dose, so the 100 mg figure is BNF practice, not NICE or the licence. Licensing varies by product - check the SmPC of the product actually supplied and document the off-label rationale. Source limit: the exact BNF 100 mg once-daily figure could not be re-confirmed against the current BNF page; the off-label status is independently confirmed by the BNF unlicensed-use section.

  4. Box 4SUPPORTReduce irritation and resistance

    Start irritating topical treatment alternate-day or short-contact, then increase if tolerated. Do not use topical antibiotic alone, oral antibiotic alone, or topical + oral antibiotic together. An oral antibiotic must be paired with an appropriate non-antibiotic topical regimen.

  5. Box 5REVIEW/LIMITReview at 12 weeks

    Assess response and side effects. If cleared on oral antibiotic, consider stopping it and continuing topical treatment; if improved but not clear, consider up to 12 more weeks. Antibiotic treatment beyond 6 months is exceptional and needs 3-monthly review.

  6. Box 6REVIEW/LIMITNon-response / relapse

    Change an unsuccessful mild-moderate regimen; consider referral after two different 12-week courses. Moderate-severe acne failing oral-antibiotic combination merits referral consideration. Maintenance is not always needed; consider for frequent relapse and review at 12 weeks.

Sources: NG198 recommendations and Table 1; emc product 13082 doxycycline 100 mg capsules SmPC.

Recurrent UTI

Checked against the cited sources by automated comparison, November 2026. Steps labelled Direction-supported follow the cited sources' direction in this chart's own wording, not the sources' verbatim text. Not clinically validated.

  1. Box 1URGENTTreat infection; select referral

    Treat a current UTI using the acute lower-UTI/pyelonephritis pathway first. Refer/seek specialist advice for male urinary systems, recurrent upper UTI, unexplained recurrent lower UTI, pregnancy, suspected cancer or structurally altered urethra after surgery.

  2. Box 2SUPPORTSelf-care products: what NICE says some people may wish to try

    Discuss behavioural/personal-hygiene measures and self-care. NG112 1.3): be aware that some women, and trans men and non-binary people with a female urinary system, who have recurrent UTI and are not pregnant may wish to try D-MANNOSE (a sugar available to buy as powder or tablets - not a medicine; evidence based on 200 ml of 1% solution once daily in the evening) or CRANBERRY products (evidence of benefit uncertain; NO evidence of benefit for older women). Children and young people under 16 may wish to try cranberry products with the advice of a paediatric specialist (1.3.2). Advise people taking cranberry products or D-mannose about the SUGAR CONTENT, to be counted in daily sugar intake (1.3.3). Evidence is INCONCLUSIVE on whether probiotics (lactobacillus) reduce UTI risk (1.3.4). These do not replace assessment when a new acute infection occurs. SCOPE NOTE: NG112's antibiotic tables cover people aged 16 AND OVER; this route's stated 18+ scope is narrower than the source - 16-17-year-olds fall under the NICE tables.

  3. Box 3DRUGVaginal oestrogen when applicable

    For peri/postmenopausal people covered by this branch, consider vaginal oestrogen if behavioural measures are ineffective or inappropriate; discuss risks/benefits and preferred product. Review within 12 months. Do not use systemic HRT specifically to prevent recurrent UTI.

  4. Box 4DRUGTrigger-related single dose

    If earlier measures and vaginal oestrogen are ineffective/inappropriate, consider single-dose antibiotic prophylaxis for an identifiable trigger after current infection is treated. Use cultures, past antibiotics and local policy; review within 6 months.

  5. Box 5DRUGConsider methenamine alternative

    After appropriate earlier measures, consider methenamine hippurate instead of daily antibiotic prophylaxis in eligible non-pregnant people (NG112 1.2.8-1.2.10; 1.2.8 is the consider-methenamine clause, 1.2.9 the specialist-advice clause, 1.2.10 the citrate clause - all): 1 g twice daily for prophylaxis of uncomplicated lower UTI; not acute-infection treatment or the catheter-associated regimen. Seek specialist advice for male urinary systems, pregnancy, upper/complicated UTI. Contraindications (Hiprex SmPC, emc 1530): hepatic dysfunction, gout, metabolic acidosis, renal parenchymal infection, severe dehydration, severe renal failure (creatinine clearance or GFR <10 ml/min). Do not administer concurrently with sulphonamides because of the possibility of crystalluria, or with alkalinising agents such as potassium citrate mixtures (SmPC; the citrate point also matches NG112 1.2.10).

  6. Box 6REVIEW/LIMITDaily antibiotics and planned review

    If no improvement after applicable earlier options, consider daily prophylaxis after treating current UTI and considering underlying causes. Review antibiotics at least every 6 months. Review methenamine within 6 months, then annually; maintain rapid access to acute treatment.

Sources: NG112 recommendations; emc product 1530 Hiprex 1 g Tablets SmPC.

Acute cough

Checked against the cited sources by automated comparison, November 2026. Steps labelled Direction-supported follow the cited sources' direction in this chart's own wording, not the sources' verbatim text. Not clinically validated.

  1. Box 1URGENTCheck for serious alternative illness

    Hospital referral/specialist advice if features suggest sepsis, pulmonary embolism, lung cancer or another serious cause. Reassess deterioration for pneumonia or cardiorespiratory failure rather than assuming uncomplicated bronchitis.

  2. Box 2ASSESSExplain the expected course

    Acute cough commonly resolves in 3-4 weeks without antibiotics. Explain symptom care and when to seek help: rapid/significant worsening, systemic illness, or no improvement after 3-4 weeks.

  3. Box 3SUPPORTMost: no antibiotic

    Do not offer antibiotics for uncomplicated URTI cough; do not routinely offer them for uncomplicated acute bronchitis. Antibiotics shorten bronchitis cough by about half a day on average and can cause diarrhoea/nausea.

  4. Box 4ASSESSIdentify higher-risk cases

    Higher risk of complications (NG120 1.1.15): pre-existing comorbidity such as significant heart, lung, renal, liver or neuromuscular disease, immunosuppression or cystic fibrosis; young children who were born prematurely; older than 65 years with 2 or more of the following, or older than 80 years with 1 or more: hospitalisation in the previous year, type 1 or type 2 diabetes, history of congestive heart failure, current use of oral corticosteroids. For people at higher risk of complications (ideally at a face-to-face clinical examination): consider an immediate antibiotic prescription or a back-up prescription (1.1.16).

  5. Box 5DRUGSystemically very unwell / prescribing

    Offer immediate antibiotic if systemically very unwell, ideally assessed face to face; this does not replace hospital referral where needed. Use the original adult choice table and allergy/pregnancy/local-resistance checks.

  6. Box 6SUPPORTSelf-care: the full NG120 limited-evidence list

    No bronchodilator or oral/inhaled steroid unless underlying airways disease; no mucolytic. NG120 1.2.1): some people may wish to try these self-care treatments, with LIMITED evidence of some benefit - honey (people aged OVER 1 YEAR); pelargonium (a herbal medicine; 12 and over); over-the-counter cough medicines containing the expectorant guaifenesin (12 and over); over-the-counter cough medicines containing cough suppressants, EXCEPT codeine (12 and over who do NOT have a persistent cough, such as in asthma, or excessive secretions). 1.2.2: limited evidence suggests that antihistamines, decongestants and codeine-containing cough medicines DO NOT help cough symptoms.

Sources: NICE NG120 1.2.1/1.2.2.

COPD exacerbation

Checked against the cited sources by automated comparison, November 2026. Steps labelled Direction-supported follow the cited sources' direction in this chart's own wording, not the sources' verbatim text. Not clinically validated.

  1. Box 1URGENTDecide place of care first

    Use NG115 Table 7 (all rowsagainst the full-guideline PDF). Factors favouring hospital care: severe breathlessness; general condition poor/deteriorating; level of activity poor/confined to bed; cyanosis; worsening peripheral oedema; impaired level of consciousness; acute confusion; rapid rate of onset; already receiving long-term oxygen therapy; social circumstances living alone/not coping; unable to cope at home; significant comorbidity (particularly cardiac disease and insulin-dependent diabetes); SaO2 below 90%; changes on chest radiograph; arterial pH below 7.35; arterial PaO2 below 7 kPa. Home-care factors are the converse for each row.

  2. Box 2ASSESSAssess the exacerbation

    Compare with usual symptoms; assess possible pneumonia and other serious causes. Pulse oximetry is useful with severe features but may overestimate borderline readings in darker skin; it does not show CO2 or pH.

  3. Box 3DRUGInhaled treatment, oxygen rules and oral steroids

    Inhaler/nebuliser choice depends on dose, device ability and supervision (NG115 1.3.8/1.3.9). NEBULISER GAS (1.3.11/1.3.12): if the person is hypercapnic or acidotic, the nebuliser should be driven by COMPRESSED AIR rather than oxygen (to avoid worsening hypercapnia) - if oxygen therapy is needed, administer it simultaneously by nasal cannulae; the driving gas for nebulised therapy should ALWAYS be specified in the prescription. OXYGEN TARGET (1.3.27): if necessary, prescribe oxygen to keep the SaO2 within the INDIVIDUALISED TARGET RANGE. Pulse oximetry gives NO information about PaCO2 or pH (1.3.28). Table 7 thresholds that favour hospital care include: SaO2 below 90%, arterial pH below 7.35, arterial PaO2 below 7 kPa (Table 7,. ORAL STEROIDS: if there are no contraindications, consider oral corticosteroids for people in the community with a significant increase in breathlessness that interferes with daily activities - offer 30 mg oral prednisolone once daily for 5 days (NG115 1.3.14 and 1.3.16; CKS COPD acute-exacerbation scenario, last revised December 2025). Discuss adverse effects of prolonged therapy, and consider the need for osteoporosis prophylaxis in people requiring frequent courses of oral corticosteroids (3-4 courses per year) (same CKS page).

  4. Box 4DRUGAntibiotics: the full NG114 Table 1 oral ladder

    Antibiotics are not automatic: a range of factors (including viral infections and smoking) can trigger an exacerbation, and many exacerbations (including some severe exacerbations) are not caused by bacterial infections so will not respond to antibiotics (NG114 1.1.1). Consider an antibiotic only after taking into account: the severity of symptoms, particularly sputum colour changes and increases in volume or thickness beyond the person's normal day-to-day variation; whether they may need to go into hospital for treatment; previous exacerbation and hospital admission history, and the risk of developing complications; previous sputum culture and susceptibility results; and the risk of antimicrobial resistance with repeated courses of antibiotics (NG114 1.1.2). Oral first line if suitable (NG114 Table 1). FIRST-CHOICE oral (empirical or guided by most recent sputum culture): amoxicillin 500 mg three times a day for 5 days; doxycycline 200 mg on first day then 100 mg once a day (5-day course in total); clarithromycin 500 mg twice a day for 5 days. SECOND-CHOICE (no improvement on first choice taken for at least 2-3 days): use an alternative first choice FROM A DIFFERENT CLASS. ALTERNATIVE choices if at higher risk of treatment failure (repeated antibiotic courses, resistant bacteria on sputum culture, higher complication risk): co-amoxiclav 500/125 mg three times a day for 5 days; co-trimoxazole 960 mg twice a day for 5 days (only with bacteriological evidence of sensitivity and good reason to prefer it - BNF caveat); levofloxacin 500 mg once a day for 5 days ONLY if other alternatives unsuitable, with specialist advice (MHRA January 2024 fluoroquinolone restrictions). If the person is on antibiotic prophylaxis, treat with an antibiotic from a different class. IV antibiotics: review by 48 hours and step down to oral where possible.

  5. Box 5REVIEW/LIMITResponse and cultures

    If antibiotic given, seek help for worsening, systemic illness or failure to start improving in 2-3 days. Reassess and send sputum if not improved after antibiotic treatment and not already sampled. Change for resistant results only if not already improving.

  6. Box 6REVIEW/LIMITEscalate repeated failure: specialist triggers and recovery review

    Seek specialist advice for an acute exacerbation of COPD if symptoms are NOT IMPROVING WITH REPEATED COURSES of antibiotics, or bacteria are RESISTANT TO ORAL antibiotics, or the person CANNOT TAKE ORAL medicines (NG114 1.1.8). If no antibiotic is given, advise: an antibiotic is not needed currently, and to seek medical help WITHOUT DELAY if symptoms worsen rapidly or significantly, or the person becomes systemically unwell (1.1.5, close paraphrase). RECOVERY: give people (particularly those discharged from hospital) clear instructions on why, when and how to stop their corticosteroid treatment (NG115 1.3.20); monitor recovery by regular clinical assessment of symptoms and observation of functional capacity (1.3.37).

Sources: NG115 recommendations + full-guideline PDF Table 7; NG114.

Cellulitis and erysipelas

Checked against the cited sources by automated comparison, November 2026. Steps labelled Direction-supported follow the cited sources' direction in this chart's own wording, not the sources' verbatim text. Not clinically validated.

  1. Box 1URGENTIdentify hospital / specialist need

    Hospital referral for suspected orbital cellulitis, bone/joint infection, necrotising fasciitis or sepsis. Consider referral/advice for severe illness, infection near eyes/nose, unusual exposure, lymphangitis, oral-treatment failure or inability to take oral medicines.

  2. Box 2ASSESSCheck mimics and extent

    Exclude inflammatory bites/immunisation reactions and venous causes. Consider marking extent with a single-use surgical marker; redness may be less visible on darker skin. Initial swab only for broken skin plus penetrating injury, water exposure or infection acquired abroad.

  3. Box 3DRUGOffer antibiotic; choose by risk

    Offer antibiotic after considering severity/site, unusual pathogens, previous microbiology and MRSA status. Flucloxacillin is adult first choice for the uncomplicated source branch: 500 mg to 1 g four times a day orally for 5-7 days, then review (NG141 Table 1 - which also notes that in September 2019, 1 g orally four times a day was off label). High-dose conflict (presented, not resolved): the flucloxacillin 500 mg capsules SmPC (emc 12636) states the adult oral dose is 250 mg four times a day, doubled in serious infections. Its statement that the maximum recommended dose in adults is 1 g every 8 to 12 hours sits at the end of the SmPC's renal-impairment passage, and the same SmPC's posology allows up to 8 g daily in divided doses six to eight hourly for osteomyelitis or endocarditis (both) - so that cap is not a universal maximum. For this uncomplicated oral skin-infection context, 1 g four times daily (4 g/day) still sits above the SmPC's stated standard-dose range: high-dose use needs explicit specialist or local-policy grounds with the rationale recorded. Verify penicillin/beta-lactam allergy; not the near-eye/nose, MRSA or deep-infection regimen.

  4. DRUGFlucloxacillin first choice: alternatives and how to take it

    Flucloxacillin is adult first choice for the uncomplicated source branch (NG141 Table 1: 500 mg to 1 g four times a day orally for 5 to 7 days; 1 g four times a day orally was off-label in September 2019). Verify penicillin/beta-lactam allergy. ALTERNATIVES for penicillin allergy or flucloxacillin unsuitable (Table 1, close paraphrase): clarithromycin 500 mg twice a day (5-7 days); doxycycline 200 mg on the first day then 100 mg once a day (5-7 days); erythromycin 500 mg four times a day in pregnancy. NEAR THE EYES OR NOSE (higher concern for intracranial complication; consider specialist advice): co-amoxiclav 500/125 mg three times a day for 7 days; penicillin allergy - clarithromycin WITH metronidazole 400 mg three times a day, 7 days. SEVERE INFECTION alternatives: co-amoxiclav, cefuroxime, clindamycin or ceftriaxone per Table 1; add vancomycin/teicoplanin/linezolid if MRSA suspected or confirmed. Not the MRSA or deep-infection regimen on this card. Administration (flucloxacillin SmPC section 4.2): take on an EMPTY STOMACH - at least 1 hour before or 2 hours after meals; swallow capsules with a FULL GLASS OF WATER (250 ml) to reduce the risk of oesophageal pain; patients should NOT LIE DOWN immediately after intake.

  5. Box 4REVIEW/LIMITCourse length: extension to 14 days is sanctioned

    NG141 general-principles text (from the guideline recommendations, not Table 1): a longer course length (up to 14 days in total) may be needed based on clinical assessment; however, skin does take some time to return to normal, and full resolution of symptoms at 5 to 7 days is not expected. Oral first line when possible; if IV antibiotics are given, review by 48 hours and consider switching to oral. Hypokalaemia monitoring (SmPC section 4.4): potentially life-threatening hypokalaemia can occur, especially in high doses, and can be resistant to potassium supplementation; regular measurements of potassium levels are recommended during therapy with higher doses - take care with hypokalaemia-inducing diuretics or other risk factors (e.g. malnutrition, renal tubule dysfunction). Manage any underlying condition that may predispose to cellulitis or erysipelas, for example diabetes, venous insufficiency, eczema, and oedema (which may be an adverse effect of medicines such as calcium channel blockers) (NG141 1.1.7). Advise that skin may take some time to return to normal after the course of antibiotics has finished, and to seek medical help if symptoms worsen rapidly or significantly at any time or do not start to improve within 2 to 3 days (NG141 1.1.8, close paraphrase). Reassess if symptoms worsen rapidly or significantly, do not start to improve within 2 to 3 days, or the person becomes systemically very unwell, has severe pain out of proportion to the infection, or has redness or swelling spreading beyond the initial area (NG141 1.1.9, close paraphrase): reassess deterioration or lack of improvement rather than judging by appearance alone.

  6. Box 5REVIEW/LIMITReassess at 2-3 days or sooner

    Reassess rapid/significant worsening, no improvement in 2-3 days, systemic illness, pain out of proportion or spreading swelling/redness. Revisit mimics and deep infection; take a swab if broken skin and not already done.

  7. Box 6REVIEW/LIMITRecurrent disease: not routine prophylaxis

    Do not routinely offer preventive antibiotics. Specialists may consider a trial after at least 2 separate episodes in 12 months treated in hospital or under specialist advice, with shared decision and at least 6-monthly review.

Sources: NG141 recommendations and Table 1; emc product 12636 flucloxacillin 500 mg capsules SmPC.

Otitis media (acute)

Checked against the cited sources by automated comparison, November 2026. Steps labelled Direction-supported follow the cited sources' direction in this chart's own wording, not the sources' verbatim text. Not clinically validated.

  1. Box 1URGENTUrgent illness / complications

    Hospital referral for severe systemic infection or mastoiditis, meningitis, intracranial abscess, sinus thrombosis or facial nerve paralysis. Systemically very unwell, serious illness or high complication risk: immediate antibiotic with advice, alongside appropriate escalation.

  2. Box 2ASSESSExplain course and assess fever

    Symptoms usually last about 3 days and may last a week; most improve without antibiotics. Assess/manage fever under 5 using the separate NICE fever guideline.

  3. Box 3SUPPORTRelieve pain safely: the full analgesia rule

    NG91 1.1.4): offer regular doses of paracetamol or ibuprofen for pain - use the RIGHT DOSE FOR THE AGE OR WEIGHT of the child at the right time, and use MAXIMUM DOSES FOR SEVERE PAIN. Eardrops containing an anaesthetic and analgesic (phenazone 40 mg/g with lidocaine 10 mg/g, 4 drops two or three times a day for up to 7 days) may be considered ONLY if an immediate oral antibiotic is not given AND there is no eardrum perforation or otorrhoea (NG91 Table 1). Explain that evidence suggests decongestants and antihistamines do not help symptoms (NG91 1.1.6).

  4. Box 4SUPPORTMost: no or back-up antibiotic

    For those outside the higher-benefit/seriously-unwell groups, consider no antibiotic or a back-up prescription. Explain small benefit, possible diarrhoea/nausea and rare mastoiditis with or without antibiotics.

  5. Box 5DRUGAntibiotic choices by age (NG91 Table 1)

    NG91 Table 1) starts at 1 MONTH - there is no NG91 dose for infants under 1 month (specialist territory). First choice: amoxicillin - 1 month-11 months 125 mg three times a day, 1-4 years 250 mg three times a day, 5-17 years 500 mg three times a day, all for 5-7 days. Penicillin allergy (not pregnant): clarithromycin weight-banded twice daily for 5-7 days. Penicillin allergy in pregnancy: erythromycin (8-17 years). Second choice (worsening on first choice taken for at least 2-3 days): co-amoxiclav age/weight-banded for 5-7 days; allergy - consult local microbiologist. These features are not an automatic immediate-antibiotic rule: for children who may be more likely to benefit (otorrhoea at any age, or under 2 years with infection in both ears), consider no antibiotic prescription with advice, a back-up antibiotic prescription with advice, OR an immediate antibiotic prescription, taking account of the evidence and the child's circumstances (NG91 1.1.11).

  6. Box 6REVIEW/LIMITSafety-net and review

    Seek help for worsening/systemic illness or failure to start improving after 3 days. Back-up: use for no improvement within 3 days or rapid/significant worsening. Reassess alternatives and complications; eardrop review if no improvement within 7 days.

Sources: NICE NG91 1.1.4 + Table 1.

Sinusitis (acute)

Checked against the cited sources by automated comparison, November 2026. Steps labelled Direction-supported follow the cited sources' direction in this chart's own wording, not the sources' verbatim text. Not clinically validated.

  1. Box 1URGENTLook for complications

    Hospital referral for severe systemic infection, orbital/periorbital complications (eye swelling, displaced globe, diplopia, ophthalmoplegia or reduced vision), or intracranial features such as frontal swelling, meningism, severe frontal headache or focal neurology.

  2. Box 2ASSESSAround 10 days or less

    Do not offer antibiotic. Explain usual course is 2-3 weeks; support symptom care and advise reassessment for deterioration, systemic illness or no improvement after 3 weeks.

  3. Box 3DRUG10+ days without improvement

    Consider a 14-day high-dose nasal corticosteroid after contraindication, technique and other-steroid review. It may improve symptoms, not illness duration. High-dose use here is off-label; no product substitution or dose card is inferred.

  4. Box 4SUPPORTConsider no or back-up antibiotic

    For 10+ days without improvement, consider no or back-up antibiotic after bacterial-likelihood and benefit/harm discussion. Back-up is for no improvement within 7 days or rapid/significant worsening.

  5. Box 5DRUGImmediate antibiotic branch: the NG79 adult table

    NG79 Table 1 adults): first choice - phenoxymethylpenicillin 500 mg four times a day for 5 days. If systemically very unwell, signs of more serious illness, or high complication risk - co-amoxiclav 500/125 mg three times a day for 5 days. Penicillin allergy (not pregnant) - doxycycline 200 mg on first day then 100 mg once a day for 4 days (5-day course), or clarithromycin 500 mg twice a day for 5 days; in pregnancy - erythromycin. Second choice (worsening on first choice for at least 2-3 days) - co-amoxiclav; allergy or worsening on second choice - consult a local microbiologist. If co-amoxiclav was used first choice, consult a local microbiologist for second choice.

  6. Box 6REVIEW/LIMITSelf-care and the MHRA decongestant rules

    Consider paracetamol/ibuprofen if suitable. MHRA Drug Safety Update 30 April 2026): xylometazoline/oxymetazoline nasal sprays or drops - do NOT use for more than 5 CONSECUTIVE DAYS; rebound congestion, rhinitis medicamentosa and tachyphylaxis are recognised with overuse; patients may mistake rebound congestion for the original congestion; do not exceed the daily dose and note the minimum dosing interval; if symptoms persist, worsen or do not improve after 5 days, alternative treatment may be required. COMBINATION RULE: do not use xylometazoline or oxymetazoline together or with other oral and nasal forms of medicines used to treat a blocked nose, such as pseudoephedrine, phenylephrine or ephedrine. Saline and decongestants (NG79 1.3.2): some people may wish to try nasal saline or nasal decongestants, although there is not enough evidence to show that they help to relieve nasal congestion. Reassess if symptoms worsen rapidly or significantly, taking account of alternative diagnoses such as a dental infection and any symptoms or signs suggesting a more serious illness or condition (NG79 1.1.3), and despite treatment also previous antibiotic use, which may lead to resistant organisms (NG79 1.1.7, close paraphrase).

Impetigo

Checked against the cited sources by automated comparison, November 2026. Steps labelled Direction-supported follow the cited sources' direction in this chart's own wording, not the sources' verbatim text. Not clinically validated.

  1. Box 1URGENTIdentify need for referral

    Refer to hospital for a suspected more serious condition such as cellulitis, or widespread impetigo with immunocompromise. Consider referral/advice for bullous impetigo (particularly babies aged ≤1 year), frequent recurrence, systemic illness or high complication risk. The treatment branches below are adult-focused.

  2. Box 2DRUGLocalised non-bullous: the named topical options

    If not systemically unwell/high risk, consider hydrogen peroxide 1% cream - apply two or three times a day for 5 days (NG153 Table 1). If hydrogen peroxide is unsuitable (for example, impetigo around the eyes) or ineffective: fusidic acid 2% cream - apply three times a day for 5 days (first-choice topical antibiotic). If fusidic acid resistance is suspected or confirmed: mupirocin 2% - apply three times a day for 5 days. Advise people with impetigo, and their parents or carers if appropriate, about good hygiene measures to reduce the spread of impetigo to other areas of the body and to other people (NG153 1.1.1).

  3. Box 3DRUGWidespread or oral branch: named oral options

    Widespread non-bullous: offer a short topical OR oral antibiotic course. ORAL (NG153 Table 1, close paraphrase): first choice flucloxacillin 500 mg four times a day for 5 days; penicillin allergy or flucloxacillin unsuitable (not pregnant) - clarithromycin 250 mg twice a day for 5 days (can be increased to 500 mg twice a day for severe infections); penicillin allergy in pregnancy - erythromycin 250 mg to 500 mg four times a day for 5 days. Administration (flucloxacillin SmPC section 4.2): take on an EMPTY STOMACH - at least 1 hour before or 2 hours after meals; swallow capsules with a FULL GLASS OF WATER (250 ml) to reduce the risk of oesophageal pain; patients should NOT LIE DOWN immediately after intake. Hypokalaemia monitoring (SmPC section 4.4): potentially life-threatening hypokalaemia can occur, especially in high doses, and can be resistant to potassium supplementation; regular measurements of potassium levels are recommended during therapy with higher doses - take care with hypokalaemia-inducing diuretics or other risk factors (e.g. malnutrition, renal tubule dysfunction).

  4. Box 4DRUGBullous or unwell/high risk

    Offer a short oral antibiotic course for all bullous impetigo, or non-bullous disease with systemic illness/high risk. Choose from the original adult table, guided by allergy and local resistance; flucloxacillin is first-choice oral treatment.

  5. Box 5REVIEW/LIMITIf treatment fails

    Reassess worsening or no improvement after the course. After topical-antibiotic failure, offer an oral antibiotic and consider a swab; after oral failure consider swab. Revisit herpes simplex, cellulitis and resistance rather than repeating blindly.

  6. Box 6REVIEW/LIMITFrequent recurrence / culture review

    Send skin swab for frequent recurrence; consider nasal swab/decolonisation with the full pathway. Review culture results and use a narrow-spectrum alternative if symptoms are not improving.
