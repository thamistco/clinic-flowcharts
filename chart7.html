NICE NG193 + NG215 · CKS · checked 18 Nov 2026

Pain management: chronic pain assessment and support

People aged 16 and over with chronic pain (primary, secondary, or both). NICE NG193 covers chronic primary pain in over-16s; antidepressant medicines advice is for ages 18+. Acute pain and pain from a known condition follow the guideline for that condition.

1

Rule out urgent and treatable causes

Chronic pain is not a diagnosis of exclusion from serious disease. Assess symptom-specific red flags (for example new neurology, systemic illness, unexplained weight loss, hot swollen joint) and arrange urgent or emergency assessment when indicated. Make a considered search for any injury or disease that may account for the pain, using clinical judgement with the person.

2

Person-centred assessment

Ask how pain affects daily life, work, sleep, physical and psychological wellbeing, relationships and social circumstances, and how life events affect the pain. Explore strengths and what helps. Think about chronic primary pain when there is no clear underlying cause, or the pain or its impact is out of proportion to any observable injury or disease. Primary and secondary pain can coexist.

3

Agree a care and support plan

Discuss priorities, abilities and goals, what the person is already doing that helps, and the evidence for benefits, risks and uncertainties of each option. Explain that symptoms fluctuate, a cause may not be found, and quality of life can improve even if pain persists. When communicating normal test results, avoid invalidating the person's experience.

4

Non-drug support for chronic primary pain

Offer a supervised group exercise programme and encourage physical activity. Consider acceptance and commitment therapy (ACT) or CBT for pain, and a single course of acupuncture or dry needling within the guideline's delivery limits. Do not offer TENS, ultrasound or interferential therapy, and do not offer biofeedback.

5

Medicines: help and harm

Consider an antidepressant (amitriptyline, citalopram, duloxetine, fluoxetine, paroxetine or sertraline; off-label, age 18+) after a full discussion of benefits and harms, explaining these may help pain, sleep, distress and quality of life without a depression diagnosis. Do not initiate opioids, gabapentinoids, benzodiazepines, NSAIDs or paracetamol for chronic primary pain. If the person already takes these, review together: explain the lack of evidence, agree safe continuation if there is benefit at a safe dose with few harms, or support reducing and stopping. For pain from a known condition, follow the guideline for that condition.

Review, flare-ups and referral

Reassess if symptoms change or flare; review the care plan and consider new causes. Consider referral to specialist pain services when primary-care options are exhausted, function is falling, or complex dependence-forming medicines are involved. When stopping antidepressants, opioids, gabapentinoids or benzodiazepines, plan gradual withdrawal with the person (see the opioid down-titration chart).

Headache: red flags, diagnosis and treatment

Young people aged 12 and over and adults with tension-type headache, migraine or cluster headache. CG150 covers diagnosis and management in primary care; some recommendations differ for ages 12–17. It does not cover headache from secondary causes, which follow their own pathways.

1

Check for red flags first

Evaluate and consider further investigation or referral for: worsening headache with fever; sudden-onset thunderclap headache reaching maximum intensity within 5 minutes; new neurological deficit, cognitive dysfunction, personality change or impaired consciousness; recent head trauma; headache triggered by cough, Valsalva, sneeze or exercise; orthostatic headache; symptoms of giant cell arteritis or acute narrow-angle glaucoma; or a substantial change in headache character. Consider investigation for new-onset headache with compromised immunity, past malignancy, or vomiting without obvious cause.

+

Read the full source, scope and exceptions. A check date is not a new evidence-review date.

  • CG150 1.1.1–1.1.2 red flags
  • NG12 suspected cancer: brain/CNS referral
2

Diagnose with a headache diary

Consider an 8-week diary recording frequency, duration and severity, associated symptoms, all prescribed and over-the-counter acute medicines, precipitants and relationship to menstruation. Diagnose tension-type headache, migraine or cluster headache from the feature table; if any migraine features are present in chronic headache, diagnose chronic migraine. Suspect aura when fully reversible neurological symptoms develop gradually over at least 5 minutes and last 5–60 minutes.

+

Original sources and qualifications

Read the full source, scope and exceptions. A check date is not a new evidence-review date.

3

Look for medication overuse

Be alert to medication overuse headache when headache developed or worsened during 3 months or more of: triptans, opioids, ergots or combination analgesics on 10+ days/month, or paracetamol, aspirin or NSAIDs on 15+ days/month. Do not refer people with a primary headache or medication overuse headache for neuroimaging solely for reassurance.

+

Original sources and qualifications

Read the full source, scope and exceptions. A check date is not a new evidence-review date.

  • CG150 1.2.7, 1.3.3
4

Acute treatment by type

Tension-type: consider aspirin, paracetamol or an NSAID (no aspirin under 16). Migraine: offer a triptan plus an NSAID, or a triptan plus paracetamol; monotherapy is an option, and consider adding an anti-emetic even without nausea; start with the lowest-cost triptan and try alternatives if ineffective. Cluster: offer oxygen and/or subcutaneous or nasal triptan. Do not offer opioids or ergots for any of these. In pregnancy, offer paracetamol for migraine and discuss risks before a triptan or NSAID.

+

Original sources and qualifications

Read the full source, scope and exceptions. A check date is not a new evidence-review date.

  • CG150 1.3.7–1.3.16, 1.3.32, 1.3.35–1.3.38
5

Prevention when attacks are frequent

For migraine, consider propranolol, topiramate or amitriptyline after discussing benefits, risks and suitability - including the self-harm risk of propranolol in depression and contraception/pregnancy cautions for topiramate. If these fail or are unsuitable, consider a course of up to 10 acupuncture sessions. Review the need for continuing prophylaxis 3–6 months after starting. Specialist options (for example CGRP inhibitors, botulinum toxin for chronic migraine) follow technology appraisal criteria.

+

Original sources and qualifications

Read the full source, scope and exceptions. A check date is not a new evidence-review date.

  • CG150 1.3.18–1.3.27 prophylaxis
6

Withdraw overused medicines and review

Explain that medication overuse headache is treated by withdrawing the overused medicine (abruptly for simple analgesics and triptans; opioids need a planned reduction - see the opioid down-titration chart). Advise that headache may worsen briefly. Consider prophylaxis for the underlying headache disorder alongside withdrawal, and review response. Warn anyone using acute treatments about the overuse risk from the start.

+

Original sources and qualifications

Read the full source, scope and exceptions. A check date is not a new evidence-review date.

  • CG150 1.3.6, 1.3.42–1.3.45
  • NG215 withdrawal planning

Palpitations: triage, assessment and referral

Adults presenting in primary care with palpitations. The CKS pathway covers emergency admission, assessment, cardiology referral and reassurance. It does not replace acute coronary syndrome or arrhythmia-specific pathways once a cause is confirmed.

1

Emergency admission features

Arrange emergency admission if palpitations come with: sustained ventricular tachycardia; SVT that persists after a Valsalva manoeuvre and one attempt at carotid sinus massage; haemodynamic compromise; known or suspected structural heart disease with serious features; syncope, severe dizziness or breathlessness; onset during exercise; high-grade AV block; or new-onset atrial fibrillation with rapid ventricular rate or decompensated heart failure.

+

Original sources and qualifications

Read the full source, scope and exceptions. A check date is not a new evidence-review date.

  • CKS palpitations: admission criteria
2

Focused history and examination

Ask about onset, offset, frequency, regularity and duration; associated chest pain, breathlessness, dizziness or syncope; triggers including caffeine, alcohol, nicotine, drugs and medicines; and family history of arrhythmia or sudden cardiac death. Examine pulse, blood pressure and cardiovascular system. Record a 12-lead ECG - ideally during symptoms, though a normal ECG between episodes does not exclude an arrhythmia.

+

Original sources and qualifications

Read the full source, scope and exceptions. A check date is not a new evidence-review date.

  • CKS palpitations: assessment
3

Blood tests and rhythm capture

Arrange full blood count, urea and electrolytes, thyroid function, liver function, HbA1c and lipids as indicated. If episodes are frequent but not captured on a resting ECG, consider ambulatory ECG monitoring matched to symptom frequency. Consider echocardiography when structural disease is suspected.

+

Original sources and qualifications

Read the full source, scope and exceptions. A check date is not a new evidence-review date.

  • CKS palpitations: investigations
4

Refer to cardiology when indicated

Refer for: atrial flutter; SVT that terminated (self or with manoeuvres); ECG evidence of pre-excitation (Wolff-Parkinson-White pattern); frequent ventricular ectopics (for example more than 30 per hour on monitoring) or suspected ventricular tachycardia; palpitations with a family history of premature sudden cardiac death or inherited cardiac condition; or exercise-induced symptoms. Use urgent or routine routes according to features.

+

Original sources and qualifications

Read the full source, scope and exceptions. A check date is not a new evidence-review date.

  • CKS palpitations: referral criteria
5

Reassure when low risk

Consider reassurance and no referral when episodes are isolated or infrequent, the ECG is normal, there are no red-flag features and no significant family history. Address triggers - caffeine, alcohol, nicotine, recreational drugs, medicines and stress - and treat reversible causes such as anaemia, thyroid disease or electrolyte disturbance.

+

Original sources and qualifications

Read the full source, scope and exceptions. A check date is not a new evidence-review date.

  • CKS palpitations: reassurance and primary care management
6

Safety-net, driving and work

Advise when to seek urgent help: syncope, sustained rapid heartbeat, chest pain or breathlessness. Give driving advice where relevant - DVLA rules apply to arrhythmias causing or likely to cause incapacity - and discuss occupational implications for safety-critical work. Review results and arrange follow-up if symptoms change or recur.

+

Original sources and qualifications

Read the full source, scope and exceptions. A check date is not a new evidence-review date.

  • CKS palpitations: follow-up and advice
  • DVLA: cardiovascular disorders and driving

Falls: assessment and prevention in older people

Adults aged 65 and over, and people aged 50–64 at higher risk of falls. NG249 covers assessment and prevention in the community; it replaces the falls part of CG161. Inpatient falls and fragility-fracture management follow their own pathways.

1

After a fall: exclude injury and acute illness

A new fall can signal injury (fracture, head injury - especially with anticoagulants) or an acute medical cause such as infection, syncope or hypoglycaemia. Assess and manage these first; the falls pathway addresses prevention once the person is safe. Consider safeguarding and capacity issues where relevant.

+

Read the full source, scope and exceptions. A check date is not a new evidence-review date.

2

Ask about falls routinely

Ask older people opportunistically - at routine appointments, home visits and medicines reviews - whether they have fallen in the past year, how often, in what context, and whether they feel unsteady or fear falling. Do not use falls risk-prediction tools to identify people at risk or to decide who gets an assessment.

+

Original sources and qualifications

Read the full source, scope and exceptions. A check date is not a new evidence-review date.

  • NG249 identifying risk - no prediction tools
3

Decide who needs a comprehensive assessment

Offer a comprehensive multifactorial falls assessment after a fall needing medical attention, after recurrent falls, or when there is high-risk context - for example frailty, fear of falling, new instability, or inability to get up after a fall. Cover gait, strength and balance; functional ability and fear of falling; vision; home hazards; cardiovascular and neurological examination; continence; cognition and mental health; osteoporosis and fracture risk; and a structured medication review including psychotropics and other falls-risk-increasing drugs.

+

Original sources and qualifications

Read the full source, scope and exceptions. A check date is not a new evidence-review date.

  • NG249 comprehensive assessment components
4

Exercise and strength-and-balance work

Offer a personalised, progressive strength and balance exercise programme to people at risk or who have fallen, delivered by appropriately trained professionals; community-based or home-based options can both work. Encourage ongoing physical activity after the programme ends.

+

5

Home, vision and medicines interventions

Offer a home-hazard assessment and intervention by suitably trained staff for people who have fallen or are at risk. Address visual problems (cataract surgery where indicated; take care with multifocal lenses during outdoor activity). Review medicines and reduce, stop or switch those linked to falls - particularly psychotropics - using shared decision making and withdrawal planning where needed.

Refer and follow up

Refer to the local falls prevention service where available; use local pathways for orthostatic hypotension, syncope or suspected cardiac causes, and consider bone-health assessment for fracture prevention. Agree an individualised plan, share it with the person, and review progress and adherence.

Adults, children and young people with tinnitus. NG155 covers assessment, investigation and management including when to refer urgently. It is deliberately referral-led: most assessment beyond otoscopy and most management sit with audiology services.

1

Same-day and urgent routes

Refer immediately (emergency) people with tinnitus accompanied by: risk of suicide or self-harm, or sudden neurological symptoms or signs (for example facial weakness). Refer to be seen within 24 hours tinnitus with sudden hearing loss, and refer urgently (suspected-cancer pathway where criteria are met) or promptly for objective pulsatile tinnitus. Unilateral or asymmetric tinnitus, or tinnitus with persistent otalgia, needs onward specialist assessment.

Primary care assessment

Ask about onset (sudden or gradual), sidedness, pulsatile or non-pulsatile character, associated hearing loss, vertigo, otalgia or discharge, impact on sleep, concentration and mood, and noise exposure, ototoxic medicines and wax. Examine the ears and perform otoscopy; check for wax or infection and treat those first. Assess distress and its effect on daily life.

Refer to audiology

Refer people with persistent or bothersome tinnitus to audiology for hearing assessment, tinnitus assessment and management planning - directly where local pathways allow. Refer regardless of measured hearing thresholds when the person wants help. Arrange imaging only through the specialist route (MRI for asymmetric symptoms is a secondary-care decision).

Information and sound-based support

At every stage, explain what tinnitus is, likely causes, that it often improves, and self-help options. In the tinnitus pathway, options include sound therapy (including amplification for hearing loss) and tinnitus counselling. Do not offer betahistine, melatonin or other medicines specifically to treat tinnitus - there is no effective drug treatment.

1

Emergency admission: serious causes

Arrange emergency admission for a suspected life-threatening or serious cause - for example peritonitis, perforated ulcer, intestinal obstruction or ischaemia, ruptured abdominal aortic aneurysm or dissection, appendicitis, acute pancreatitis, abdominal sepsis, ectopic pregnancy with instability, incarcerated hernia, testicular torsion, diabetic ketoacidosis, or severe dehydration from gastroenteritis - and for people severely unwell with Crohn's, ulcerative colitis, acute diverticulitis, pyelonephritis, hepatitis B or gallstones.

+

Original sources and qualifications

Read the full source, scope and exceptions. A check date is not a new evidence-review date.

  • CKS acute abdominal pain: admission list
2

Red flags that raise urgency

Medical: sepsis signs, jaundice, significant dehydration, polyuria/polydipsia (DKA), purpuric rash, respiratory symptoms. Surgical: recent significant abdominal trauma or surgery, irreducible hernia, peritonism (guarding, rebound, constant pain worse on movement), obstruction features (colicky pain, absolute constipation, bilious vomiting), testicular or labial pain, "red currant jelly" stool in children. Other: distension, bilious or blood-stained vomit, blood in stool, palpable mass, severe or increasing pain, unintentional weight loss.

+

Read the full source, scope and exceptions. A check date is not a new evidence-review date.

3

Assess: vitals, history, examination

Measure blood pressure, pulse, temperature and capillary refill. Take onset, duration, severity, progression, radiation and quality of pain; location; associated symptoms; previous episodes; bowel habit change; urinary features; trauma or bleeding; past medical/surgical history; medicines; travel; smoking, alcohol and substances; family history - and for women, menstrual, sexual, contraceptive and pregnancy history. Examine guided by pain location (inspection, auscultation, percussion, palpation; selective DRE, pelvic and scrotal examination). Presentations are often atypical in older people, the immunocompromised, people living with obesity, and pregnancy.

+

4

Investigate - without delaying admission

Consider FBC, U&E, LFT, blood glucose, CRP and urinalysis, plus a pregnancy test for all women of childbearing age. Add amylase/lipase (pancreatitis), STI testing (at-risk women), coagulation screen (vascular cause), lactate (mesenteric ischaemia), FIT (suspected malignancy), stool culture or disease markers as indicated. Do not delay admission to wait for results.

5

Refer or manage the suspected cause

Urgent referral: suspected cancer (suspected-cancer pathway per NG12), Crohn's disease, gallstones with jaundice or suspected biliary obstruction, severe sickle cell crisis, ulcerative colitis. Refer: cholecystitis, Fitz-Hugh Curtis syndrome, gallstones, hepatitis B, kidney stones, and endometriosis or fibroids beyond primary-care control. Manage in primary care per the relevant CKS topic: diverticulitis, constipation, dyspepsia/GORD, gastroenteritis, pelvic inflammatory disease, pyelonephritis, UTI, opioid withdrawal. Advise appropriate analgesia.

Antidepressant review timing

Review response between 2 and 4 weeks after starting treatment. SMI: six annual physical health checks

For everyone on the SMI register, complete all six checks in the preceding 12 months: smoking status, alcohol consumption, BMI (height and weight), blood pressure, total:HDL cholesterol (lipid profile) and HbA1c (blood glucose). The fuller NHS England check also covers substance misuse and medication side effects. Record results and act on what they show - the checks alone do not improve outcomes.

National / Local guidance

What differs: Nothing conflicts - the local scheme builds on the national QOF checks. The national framework defines the six checks and register rules; the Sussex LCS adds funded intervention and follow-up expectations.

Safety and source status: The Sussex LCS document states a service period ending 30 September 2025. Its clinical content remains a reasonable local benchmark, but confirm the scheme's current status with the ICB before relying on its funding or targets.

National guidance

QOF (2025/26): practices maintain an SMI register (schizophrenia, bipolar affective disorder and other psychoses, excluding remission) and record the six physical health checks - smoking, alcohol, BMI, blood pressure, lipids and HbA1c - with a national target of at least 60% of register patients having all six in the preceding 12 months. NICE NG222 governs depression treatment review; NG215 governs safe prescribing and withdrawal.
