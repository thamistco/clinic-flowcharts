    1

    Rule out urgent and treatable causes

    URGENT

    +

    Chronic pain is not a diagnosis of exclusion from serious disease. For people aged 16 and over with chronic pain (primary, secondary, or both), assess symptom-specific red flags (for example new neurology, unexplained weight loss, fever or sweats, a history of cancer, or pain that worsens at night or when lying down) and arrange urgent or emergency assessment when indicated. Make a considered search for any injury or disease that may account for the pain, using clinical judgement in discussion with the person.

    2

    Person-centred assessment

    ASSESS

    +

    Ask how pain affects daily life, work, sleep, physical and psychological wellbeing, relationships and social circumstances, and how life events affect the pain. Explore strengths and what helps. Think about chronic primary pain when there is no clear underlying cause, or the pain or its impact is out of proportion to any observable injury or disease. Primary and secondary pain can coexist.

    3

    Agree a care and support plan

    SUPPORT

    +

    Discuss priorities, abilities and goals, what the person is already doing that helps, and the evidence for benefits, risks and uncertainties of each option. Explain that symptoms fluctuate, a cause may not be found, and quality of life can improve even if pain persists. When communicating normal test results, avoid invalidating the person's experience.

    4

    Non-drug support for chronic primary pain

    SUPPORT

    +

    Offer a supervised group exercise programme and encourage physical activity. Consider acceptance and commitment therapy (ACT) or CBT for pain, and a single course of acupuncture or dry needling within the guideline's delivery limits. Do not offer TENS, ultrasound or interferential therapy, and do not offer biofeedback.

    5

    Medicines

    DRUG

    +

    help and harm: Consider an antidepressant (amitriptyline, citalopram, duloxetine, fluoxetine, paroxetine or sertraline; off-label, age 18+) after a full discussion of benefits and harms, explaining these may help pain, sleep, distress and quality of life without a depression diagnosis. Do not initiate opioids, gabapentinoids, benzodiazepines, NSAIDs or paracetamol for chronic primary pain. If the person already takes these, review together: explain the lack of evidence, agree safe continuation if there is benefit at a safe dose with few harms, or support reducing and stopping. For pain from a known condition, follow the guideline for that condition.

    6

    Review, flare-ups and referral

    REVIEW/LIMITREFER

    +

    Reassess if symptoms change or flare; review the care plan and consider new causes. Arrange specialist referral (urgency depending on clinical judgement) when red flags suggest serious underlying pathology, non-specialist management is failing, pain remains poorly controlled, or there is significant distress. When stopping antidepressants, opioids, gabapentinoids or benzodiazepines, plan gradual withdrawal with the person and do not stop abruptly unless exceptional medical circumstances apply (see the opioid down-titration chart).

    1

    Check for red flags first

    URGENT

    +

    Evaluate and consider further investigation or referral for: worsening headache with fever; sudden-onset thunderclap headache reaching maximum intensity within 5 minutes; new neurological deficit, cognitive dysfunction, personality change or impaired consciousness; recent head trauma; headache triggered by cough, Valsalva, sneeze or exercise; orthostatic headache; symptoms of giant cell arteritis or acute narrow-angle glaucoma; or a substantial change in headache character. Consider investigation for new-onset headache with compromised immunity, past malignancy, or vomiting without obvious cause.

    2

    Diagnose with a headache diary

    ASSESS

    +

    Consider an 8-week diary recording frequency, duration and severity, associated symptoms, all prescribed and over-the-counter acute medicines, precipitants and relationship to menstruation. Diagnose tension-type headache, migraine or cluster headache from the feature table; if any migraine features are present in chronic headache, diagnose chronic migraine. Suspect aura when fully reversible neurological symptoms develop gradually over at least 5 minutes and last 5–60 minutes.

    3

    Look for medication overuse

    ASSESS

    +

    Be alert to medication overuse headache when headache developed or worsened during 3 months or more of: triptans, opioids, ergots or combination analgesics on 10+ days/month, or paracetamol, aspirin or NSAIDs on 15+ days/month. Do not refer people with a primary headache or medication overuse headache for neuroimaging solely for reassurance.

    4

    Acute treatment by type

    SUPPORT

    +

    Tension-type: consider aspirin, paracetamol or an NSAID (no aspirin under 16). Migraine: offer a triptan plus an NSAID, or a triptan plus paracetamol; monotherapy is an option, and consider adding an anti-emetic even without nausea; start with the lowest-cost triptan and try alternatives if ineffective. Cluster: offer oxygen and/or subcutaneous or nasal triptan. Do not offer opioids or ergots for any of these. In pregnancy, offer paracetamol for migraine and discuss risks before a triptan or NSAID.

    5

    Prevention when attacks are frequent

    SUPPORT

    +

    For migraine, consider propranolol, topiramate or amitriptyline after discussing benefits, risks and suitability – including the self-harm risk of propranolol in depression. Topiramate: not for migraine prevention in pregnancy; anyone who could become pregnant requires the Pregnancy Prevention Programme (highly effective contraception during treatment and for at least 4 weeks after stopping; reduced efficacy of systemic hormonal contraceptives – add a barrier method and report bleeding-pattern changes; Patient Guide; repeat pregnancy testing as needed; if pregnancy occurs – stop straight away, contact the GP urgently, arrange prompt specialist reassessment; see the migraine chart for the full safeguard wording). If these fail or are unsuitable, consider a course of up to 10 acupuncture sessions. Review the need for continuing prophylaxis 3–6 months after starting. Specialist options (for example CGRP inhibitors, botulinum toxin for chronic migraine) follow technology appraisal criteria.

    6

    Withdraw overused medicines and review

    REVIEW/LIMIT

    +

    Explain that medication overuse headache is treated by withdrawing the overused medicine (abruptly for simple analgesics and triptans; opioids need a planned reduction – see the opioid down-titration chart). Advise that headache may worsen briefly. Consider prophylaxis for the underlying headache disorder alongside withdrawal, and review response. Warn anyone using acute treatments about the overuse risk from the start.

    If symptoms are happening now

    URGENT

    +

    Call 999 or go to A and E now if palpitations do not go away, OR if you also have chest pain, shortness of breath, or feeling faint or fainting; do not drive yourself (NHS heart-palpitations page, reviewed 17 March 2026).

    1

    Emergency admission features

    URGENT

    +

    Arrange emergency admission if palpitations come with: sustained ventricular tachycardia on ECG; persistent SVT on ECG (before deciding to admit, if trained and competent to do so, termination may be attempted with a Valsalva manoeuvre, then carotid sinus massage – CSM only if trained and competent, for up to 5 seconds on one side with the neck extended, only if there are no contraindications, with a defibrillator available and ideally an ECG recorded continuously during and after, per CKS); haemodynamic compromise; known or suspected structural heart disease with serious features; significant breathlessness, chest pain, syncope or near syncope; onset during exercise; high-grade or third-degree AV block; or newly diagnosed atrial fibrillation with a clinically significant tachycardia or decompensated heart failure.

    2

    Focused history and examination

    ASSESS

    +

    Ask about onset, offset, frequency, regularity and duration; associated chest pain, breathlessness, dizziness or syncope; triggers including caffeine, alcohol, nicotine, drugs and medicines; and family history of arrhythmia or sudden cardiac death. Examine pulse, blood pressure and cardiovascular system. Record a 12-lead ECG – ideally during symptoms, though a normal ECG between episodes does not exclude an arrhythmia.

    3

    Blood tests and rhythm capture

    ASSESS

    +

    Arrange blood tests including full blood count, urea and electrolytes, thyroid function, liver function, HbA1c and lipid profile to identify an underlying cause (CKS palpitations, revised February 2026). If symptoms are short-lived, arrange ambulatory ECG monitoring in primary care if available, or refer to cardiology; consider a patient-activated hand-held ECG device (such as KardiaMobile), smart watch or other wearable to record intermittent symptoms, depending on clinical judgement. Monitoring intensity is guided by symptom frequency, nature and severity (CKS-cited expert opinion: a 2-week continuous loop event monitor has the highest diagnostic yield-to-cost ratio). Arrange an echocardiogram in primary care if available, or refer to cardiology, if: a murmur is found on examination; heart failure is suspected; structural heart disease is suspected (for example, the ECG shows left bundle branch block, left ventricular hypertrophy, atrial enlargement, or Q waves); or ambulatory ECG monitoring shows a greater than 10% burden of ventricular ectopy (CKS palpitations, history-of-palpitations scenario).

    4

    Refer to cardiology when indicated

    ASSESSREFER

    +

    URGENT cardiology referral: syncope or near-syncope; palpitations precipitated by exercise; family history of sudden cardiac death under 40 and/or known or suspected hypertrophic cardiomyopathy; second- or third-degree AV block on ECG. ROUTINE referral: accompanying chest pain or lightheadedness; history or symptoms of structural heart disease, heart failure or hypertension; other resting ECG abnormality; recurrent sustained tachyarrhythmia, atrial fibrillation or flutter; symptoms clearly consistent with paroxysmal SVT (sudden onset and offset of a fast, regular heartbeat); ventricular extrasystoles when underlying heart disease is suspected, extrasystoles are frequent (over 30 an hour) or VT is suspected. For ECG-evidence items found during current palpitations (atrial flutter, SVT terminated by manoeuvres, pre-excitation/WPW pattern), CKS leaves urgency to clinical judgement. Direction-supported limb: the resting-ECG item above is supported in direction by the CKS assessment page; wording is this chart's summary.

    5

    Reassure when low risk

    SUPPORT

    +

    Consider reassurance and no referral when episodes are isolated or infrequent, the ECG is normal, there are no red-flag features and no significant family history. Address triggers – caffeine, alcohol, nicotine, recreational drugs, medicines and stress – and treat reversible causes such as anaemia, thyroid disease or electrolyte disturbance.

    6

    Safety-net, driving and work

    REVIEW/LIMITSAFETY-NET

    +

    Advise when to seek urgent help: if palpitations do not go away, OR you also have chest pain, shortness of breath, or feeling faint or fainting, call 999 or go to A and E now; do not drive yourself. Give driving advice where relevant – DVLA rules apply to arrhythmias causing or likely to cause incapacity – and discuss occupational implications for safety-critical work. Review results and arrange follow-up if symptoms change or recur.

    1

    After a fall: exclude injury and acute illness

    URGENT

    +

    A new fall can signal injury (fracture, head injury – especially with anticoagulants) or an acute medical cause such as infection, syncope or hypoglycaemia. Assess and manage these first; the falls pathway addresses prevention once the person is safe. Consider safeguarding and capacity issues where relevant.

    2

    Ask about falls routinely

    ASSESS

    +

    Ask older people opportunistically – at routine appointments, home visits and medicines reviews – whether they have fallen in the past year, how often, in what context, and whether they feel unsteady or fear falling. Do not use falls risk-prediction tools to identify people at risk or to decide who gets an assessment.

    3

    Decide who needs a comprehensive assessment

    ASSESS

    +

    +

    +

    Suicide risk: ask directly and act

    URGENT

    +

    NG222 safeguards: 1.2.8 always ask people with depression directly about suicidal ideation and intent; if there is a risk of self-harm or suicide, assess social support and awareness of help sources, arrange help appropriate to need, and advise seeking further help if the situation deteriorates. 1.2.11 advise the person and their family or carer to be vigilant for mood changes, agitation, negativity, hopelessness and suicidal ideation, and to contact their practitioner if concerned – particularly when starting or changing treatment and at times of increased personal stress. 1.2.12 if assessed at risk of suicide: do not withhold treatment for depression on the basis of suicide risk; take into account toxicity in overdose if an antidepressant is prescribed and if necessary limit the amount of medicine available; consider increasing support. 1.4.23 take into account toxicity in overdose for people at significant risk; do not routinely start TCAs, except lofepramine, as they carry the greatest risk in overdose. Agree and record a risk management and crisis plan.

    Antidepressant review timing (NG222)

    REVIEW/LIMIT

    +

    1.4.3 for all people – review how well treatment is working between 2 and 4 weeks after starting, and monitor suicidal ideation particularly in the early weeks. 1.4.11 first review usually within 2 weeks of starting (to check improvement and side effects), or 1 week after starting if the person is aged 18–25 or there is particular concern for suicide risk. 1.4.22 for people aged 18–25 or at increased suicide risk – assess mental state and mood before the prescription; review 1 week after STARTING OR INCREASING the dose for suicidality, ideally in person, or by video call, or by telephone if these options are not possible or not preferred; review again as often as needed, no later than 4 weeks after the starting appointment; set ongoing frequency by circumstances and any change in ideation or risk.

    Stopping antidepressants: what gradual means

    REVIEW/LIMIT

    +

    Continue treatment for at least 6 months after remission, reviewed regularly (NG222 1.4.11). On stopping (NG222 1.4.12-1.4.21): explain that dose is usually reduced in stages (tapering) and most people stop successfully; withdrawal symptoms can appear within a few days of stopping abruptly, missing doses or reducing, vary between people, and usually resolve in 1–2 weeks but can last several weeks or months; take into account the pharmacokinetic profile (short half-life needs a slower taper) and treatment duration; taper speed is led by the person; if severe withdrawal occurs, restart at the previous dose and slow the taper. Follow NG215 for the shared-decision stopping conversation.

    SMI: six annual physical health checks

    SUPPORT

    +

    For everyone on the SMI register (schizophrenia, bipolar affective disorder and other psychoses; the PHSMI denominator excludes patients recorded as in remission), the NHS England PHSMI core metric is all six component parts at any point in the preceding 12 months: smoking status, alcohol status, BMI, blood pressure, lipid profile, and blood glucose or HbA1c as clinically appropriate. The six-in-12-months standard (the PHSMI core metric) and the national 60% target (a Five Year Forward View for Mental Health commitment – 60% of people on the General Practice SMI register receiving a full and comprehensive physical health check, per the NHS England Physical Health Checks for People with SMI Statistical Press Notice 2023–24 Q4; the Long Term Plan successor measure is 390,000 people with SMI receiving a full annual physical health check in 2023–24) are national programme measures, NOT a QOF indicator. QOF 2026/27 (PRN02356, November 2026 update) has separate per-indicator rules: MH002 care plan; MH003 blood pressure; MH006 BMI; MH007 alcohol; MH011 lipid profile in the preceding 12 months for people currently prescribed antipsychotics and/or with pre-existing cardiovascular conditions and/or who smoke and/or are overweight (BMI at least 23 kg/m2, or at least 25 kg/m2 if ethnicity is recorded as White), otherwise the preceding 24 months; MH012 blood glucose or HbA1c in the preceding 12 months, excluding patients with a diagnosis of diabetes. There is no MH001 register indicator and no single all-six QOF indicator in 2026/27. The fuller NHS England check also covers substance misuse and medication side effects. Record results and act on what they show – the checks alone do not improve outcomes. Act on what the checks show: offer the corresponding follow-up interventions as results indicate, including alcohol intervention and weight-management follow-up (the PHSMI programme explicitly monitors uptake of the corresponding relevant follow-up interventions and access to national cancer screening programmes – NHS England Digital PHSMI page; QOF 2026/27 MH007 alcohol-consumption record and MH006 BMI record, previously.

    Antipsychotic physical-health monitoring is not the six checks

    REVIEW/LIMIT

    +

    The QOF/PHSMI six checks are a population screen; they do not discharge the antipsychotic monitoring schedule (CG178). 1.3.6.1 baseline before starting: weight (plotted), waist circumference, pulse and BP, fasting glucose or HbA1c, lipid profile and prolactin, movement-disorder assessment, nutritional/diet/activity assessment. 1.3.6.2 ECG before starting if specified in the SmPC, specific cardiovascular risk on examination, personal CVD history, or inpatient admission. 1.3.6.4 during treatment: weight weekly for the first 6 weeks, then at 12 weeks, 1 year and annually; waist annually; pulse/BP, fasting glucose/HbA1c and lipids at 12 weeks, 1 year and annually; plus response, side effects, movement disorders, adherence and overall physical health. 1.3.6.5 the secondary care team keeps responsibility for this monitoring for at least the first 12 months or until stabilised, whichever is longer; transfer to primary care only under shared care arrangements.

    Smoking cessation in psychosis: CG178 exceptions

    REVIEW/LIMIT

    +

    Offer help to stop smoking even if previous attempts were unsuccessful – but with the psychosis-specific exceptions (CG178): 1.1.2.3 be aware that reducing cigarette smoking can significantly change the metabolism of other drugs, particularly clozapine and olanzapine (levels can rise – plan monitoring). 1.1.2.4 do NOT offer bupropion to people with psychosis to help them stop smoking; warn people taking bupropion or varenicline about increased risk of adverse neuropsychiatric symptoms and monitor regularly, particularly in the first 2 to 3 weeks.

    REVIEW/LIMIT

    +

    Referral threshold and care-plan contents:

    REVIEW/LIMITREFER

    +

    NG222 1.16.8: refer people with more severe depression or chronic depressive symptoms to specialist mental health services for coordinated multidisciplinary care if the depression significantly impairs personal and social functioning AND they have not benefitted from previous treatments, AND EITHER multiple complicating problems (unemployment, poor housing, financial problems) OR significant coexisting mental and physical health conditions. 1.16.9: multidisciplinary care plans for that subgroup are developed together with the person, their GP and others involved (copy offered); set out roles and responsibilities; include 24-hour support information and how to contact it; include a crisis plan identifying triggers and management strategies; are updated on significant change; are reviewed at agreed intervals; and include medication management.
