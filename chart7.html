Pain management: chronic pain assessment and support

Checked against the cited sources by automated comparison, November 2026. Steps labelled Direction-supported follow the cited sources' direction in this chart's own wording, not the sources' verbatim text. Not clinically validated.

1

Rule out urgent and treatable causes

URGENT

+

Chronic pain is not a diagnosis of exclusion from serious disease. For people aged 16 and over with chronic pain (primary, secondary, or both), assess symptom-specific red flags (for example new neurology, unexplained weight loss, fever or sweats, a history of cancer, or pain that worsens at night or when lying down) and arrange urgent or emergency assessment when indicated. Make a considered search for any injury or disease that may account for the pain, using clinical judgement in discussion with the person.

2

Person-centred assessment

ASSESS

+

Ask how pain affects daily life, work, sleep, physical and psychological wellbeing, relationships and social circumstances, and how life events affect the pain. Explore strengths and what helps. Think about chronic primary pain when there is no clear underlying cause, or the pain or its impact is out of proportion to any observable injury or disease. Primary and secondary pain can coexist.

3

Agree a care and support plan

SUPPORT

+

Discuss priorities, abilities and goals, what the person is already doing that helps, and the evidence for benefits, risks and uncertainties of each option. Explain that symptoms fluctuate, a cause may not be found, and quality of life can improve even if pain persists. When communicating normal test results, avoid invalidating the person's experience.

4

Non-drug support for chronic primary pain

SUPPORT

+

Offer a supervised group exercise programme and encourage physical activity. Consider acceptance and commitment therapy (ACT) or CBT for pain, and a single course of acupuncture or dry needling within the guideline's delivery limits. Do not offer TENS, ultrasound or interferential therapy, and do not offer biofeedback.

5

Medicines

DRUG

+

help and harm: Consider an antidepressant (amitriptyline, citalopram, duloxetine, fluoxetine, paroxetine or sertraline; off-label, age 18+) after a full discussion of benefits and harms, explaining these may help pain, sleep, distress and quality of life without a depression diagnosis. Do not initiate opioids, gabapentinoids, benzodiazepines, NSAIDs or paracetamol for chronic primary pain. If the person already takes these, review together: explain the lack of evidence, agree safe continuation if there is benefit at a safe dose with few harms, or support reducing and stopping. For pain from a known condition, follow the guideline for that condition.

6

Review, flare-ups and referral

REVIEW/LIMIT

+

Reassess if symptoms change or flare; review the care plan and consider new causes. Arrange specialist referral (urgency depending on clinical judgement) when red flags suggest serious underlying pathology, non-specialist management is failing, pain remains poorly controlled, or there is significant distress. When stopping antidepressants, opioids, gabapentinoids or benzodiazepines, plan gradual withdrawal with the person and do not stop abruptly unless exceptional medical circumstances apply (see the opioid down-titration chart).

  • NICE NG215 recommendations, withdrawal planning 1.5 (published April 2022)
  • NICE CKS: Chronic pain - red flags (revised January 2025)
  • NICE CKS: Chronic pain - management including referral criteria (revised January 2025)

Headache: red flags, diagnosis and treatment

Checked against the cited sources by automated comparison, November 2026. Steps labelled Direction-supported follow the cited sources' direction in this chart's own wording, not the sources' verbatim text. Not clinically validated.

1

Check for red flags first

URGENT

+

Evaluate and consider further investigation or referral for: worsening headache with fever; sudden-onset thunderclap headache reaching maximum intensity within 5 minutes; new neurological deficit, cognitive dysfunction, personality change or impaired consciousness; recent head trauma; headache triggered by cough, Valsalva, sneeze or exercise; orthostatic headache; symptoms of giant cell arteritis or acute narrow-angle glaucoma; or a substantial change in headache character. Consider investigation for new-onset headache with compromised immunity, past malignancy, or vomiting without obvious cause.

2

Diagnose with a headache diary

ASSESS

+

Consider an 8-week diary recording frequency, duration and severity, associated symptoms, all prescribed and over-the-counter acute medicines, precipitants and relationship to menstruation. Diagnose tension-type headache, migraine or cluster headache from the feature table; if any migraine features are present in chronic headache, diagnose chronic migraine. Suspect aura when fully reversible neurological symptoms develop gradually over at least 5 minutes and last 5–60 minutes.

3

Look for medication overuse

ASSESS

+

Be alert to medication overuse headache when headache developed or worsened during 3 months or more of: triptans, opioids, ergots or combination analgesics on 10+ days/month, or paracetamol, aspirin or NSAIDs on 15+ days/month. Do not refer people with a primary headache or medication overuse headache for neuroimaging solely for reassurance.

4

Acute treatment by type

SUPPORT

+

Tension-type: consider aspirin, paracetamol or an NSAID (no aspirin under 16). Migraine: offer a triptan plus an NSAID, or a triptan plus paracetamol; monotherapy is an option, and consider adding an anti-emetic even without nausea; start with the lowest-cost triptan and try alternatives if ineffective. Cluster: offer oxygen and/or subcutaneous or nasal triptan. Do not offer opioids or ergots for any of these. In pregnancy, offer paracetamol for migraine and discuss risks before a triptan or NSAID.

5

Prevention when attacks are frequent

SUPPORT

+

For migraine, consider propranolol, topiramate or amitriptyline after discussing benefits, risks and suitability - including the self-harm risk of propranolol in depression. Topiramate: not for migraine prevention in pregnancy; anyone who could become pregnant requires the Pregnancy Prevention Programme (highly effective contraception during treatment and for at least 4 weeks after stopping; reduced efficacy of systemic hormonal contraceptives - add a barrier method and report bleeding-pattern changes; Patient Guide; repeat pregnancy testing as needed; if pregnancy occurs - stop straight away, contact the GP urgently, arrange prompt specialist reassessment; see the migraine chart for the full safeguard wording). If these fail or are unsuitable, consider a course of up to 10 acupuncture sessions. Review the need for continuing prophylaxis 3-6 months after starting. Specialist options (for example CGRP inhibitors, botulinum toxin for chronic migraine) follow technology appraisal criteria.

6

Withdraw overused medicines and review

REVIEW/LIMIT

+

Explain that medication overuse headache is treated by withdrawing the overused medicine (abruptly for simple analgesics and triptans; opioids need a planned reduction - see the opioid down-titration chart). Advise that headache may worsen briefly. Consider prophylaxis for the underlying headache disorder alongside withdrawal, and review response. Warn anyone using acute treatments about the overuse risk from the start.

  • Topamax (topiramate) SmPC (4.4 PPP; 4.5 contraceptive interaction; 4.6 pregnancy)
  • MHRA DSU June 2024: topiramate Pregnancy Prevention Programme

Palpitations: triage, assessment and referral

Checked against the cited sources by automated comparison, November 2026. Steps labelled Direction-supported follow the cited sources' direction in this chart's own wording, not the sources' verbatim text. Not clinically validated.

•

If symptoms are happening now

URGENT

+

Call 999 or go to A and E now if palpitations do not go away, OR if you also have chest pain, shortness of breath, or feeling faint or fainting; do not drive yourself (NHS heart-palpitations page, reviewed 17 March 2026).

1

Emergency admission features

URGENT

+

Arrange emergency admission if palpitations come with: sustained ventricular tachycardia on ECG; persistent SVT on ECG (before deciding to admit, if trained and competent to do so, termination may be attempted with a Valsalva manoeuvre, then carotid sinus massage - CSM only if trained and competent, for up to 5 seconds on one side with the neck extended, only if there are no contraindications, with a defibrillator available and ideally an ECG recorded continuously during and after, per CKS); haemodynamic compromise; known or suspected structural heart disease with serious features; significant breathlessness, chest pain, syncope or near syncope; onset during exercise; high-grade or third-degree AV block; or newly diagnosed atrial fibrillation with a clinically significant tachycardia or decompensated heart failure.

2

Focused history and examination

ASSESS

+

Ask about onset, offset, frequency, regularity and duration; associated chest pain, breathlessness, dizziness or syncope; triggers including caffeine, alcohol, nicotine, drugs and medicines; and family history of arrhythmia or sudden cardiac death. Examine pulse, blood pressure and cardiovascular system. Record a 12-lead ECG - ideally during symptoms, though a normal ECG between episodes does not exclude an arrhythmia.

3

Blood tests and rhythm capture

ASSESS

+

Arrange blood tests including full blood count, urea and electrolytes, thyroid function, liver function, HbA1c and lipid profile to identify an underlying cause (CKS palpitations, revised February 2026). If symptoms are short-lived, arrange ambulatory ECG monitoring in primary care if available, or refer to cardiology; consider a patient-activated hand-held ECG device (such as KardiaMobile), smart watch or other wearable to record intermittent symptoms, depending on clinical judgement. Monitoring intensity is guided by symptom frequency, nature and severity (CKS-cited expert opinion: a 2-week continuous loop event monitor has the highest diagnostic yield-to-cost ratio). Arrange an echocardiogram in primary care if available, or refer to cardiology, if: a murmur is found on examination; heart failure is suspected; structural heart disease is suspected (for example, the ECG shows left bundle branch block, left ventricular hypertrophy, atrial enlargement, or Q waves); or ambulatory ECG monitoring shows a greater than 10% burden of ventricular ectopy (CKS palpitations, history-of-palpitations scenario).

4

Refer to cardiology when indicated

ASSESS

+

URGENT cardiology referral: syncope or near-syncope; palpitations precipitated by exercise; family history of sudden cardiac death under 40 and/or known or suspected hypertrophic cardiomyopathy; second- or third-degree AV block on ECG. ROUTINE referral: accompanying chest pain or lightheadedness; history or symptoms of structural heart disease, heart failure or hypertension; other resting ECG abnormality; recurrent sustained tachyarrhythmia, atrial fibrillation or flutter; symptoms clearly consistent with paroxysmal SVT (sudden onset and offset of a fast, regular heartbeat); ventricular extrasystoles when underlying heart disease is suspected, extrasystoles are frequent (over 30 an hour) or VT is suspected. For ECG-evidence items found during current palpitations (atrial flutter, SVT terminated by manoeuvres, pre-excitation/WPW pattern), CKS leaves urgency to clinical judgement. Direction-supported limb: the resting-ECG item above is supported in direction by the CKS assessment page; wording is this chart's summary.

5

Reassure when low risk

SUPPORT

+

Consider reassurance and no referral when episodes are isolated or infrequent, the ECG is normal, there are no red-flag features and no significant family history. Address triggers - caffeine, alcohol, nicotine, recreational drugs, medicines and stress - and treat reversible causes such as anaemia, thyroid disease or electrolyte disturbance.

6

Safety-net, driving and work

REVIEW/LIMIT

+

Advise when to seek urgent help: if palpitations do not go away, OR you also have chest pain, shortness of breath, or feeling faint or fainting, call 999 or go to A and E now; do not drive yourself. Give driving advice where relevant - DVLA rules apply to arrhythmias causing or likely to cause incapacity - and discuss occupational implications for safety-critical work. Review results and arrange follow-up if symptoms change or recur.

  • NHS: heart palpitations (999/A and E and do-not-drive wording; reviewed 17 Mar 2026)
  • NICE CKS: palpitations, history scenario (revised Feb 2026; urgent vs routine split)
  • NICE CKS: palpitations, current scenario (revised Feb 2026; admission features and CSM caveats)

Falls: assessment and prevention in older people

Checked against the cited sources by automated comparison, November 2026. Steps labelled Direction-supported follow the cited sources' direction in this chart's own wording, not the sources' verbatim text. Not clinically validated.

1

After a fall: exclude injury and acute illness

URGENT

+

A new fall can signal injury (fracture, head injury - especially with anticoagulants) or an acute medical cause such as infection, syncope or hypoglycaemia. Assess and manage these first; the falls pathway addresses prevention once the person is safe. Consider safeguarding and capacity issues where relevant.

2

Ask about falls routinely

ASSESS

+

Ask older people opportunistically - at routine appointments, home visits and medicines reviews - whether they have fallen in the past year, how often, in what context, and whether they feel unsteady or fear falling. Do not use falls risk-prediction tools to identify people at risk or to decide who gets an assessment.

3

Decide who needs a comprehensive assessment

ASSESS

+

Offer a comprehensive falls assessment and comprehensive falls management to people who have fallen in the last year and meet any of these criteria (NG249 1.1.3): living with frailty; injured in a fall and needed medical (including surgical) treatment; experienced a loss of consciousness related to a fall; unable to get up independently after a fall; had 2 or more falls in the last year. Fallers without any of these criteria: assess gait and balance (1.1.4). The assessment covers gait, strength and balance; functional ability and fear of falling; vision; home hazards; cardiovascular and neurological examination; continence; cognition and mental health; osteoporosis and fracture risk; and a structured medication review including psychotropics and other falls-risk-increasing drugs.

4

Exercise and strength-and-balance work

SUPPORT

+

Offer a personalised, progressive strength and balance exercise programme to people at risk or who have fallen, delivered by appropriately trained professionals; community-based or home-based options can both work. Encourage ongoing physical activity after the programme ends.

5

Home, vision and medicines interventions

SUPPORT

+

Offer a home-hazard assessment and intervention using a validated tool for people who have fallen or are at risk, carried out by an occupational therapist or other appropriately trained professional (NG249 1.3.5-1.3.6). If visual impairment is caused by cataracts, refer them to an ophthalmologist (1.3.7). Review medicines and reduce, stop or switch those linked to falls - particularly psychotropics - using shared decision making and withdrawal planning where needed (1.3.2-1.3.3; NG215 for tapering).

6

Refer and follow up

REVIEW/LIMIT

+

Refer to the local falls prevention service where available; use local pathways for orthostatic hypotension, syncope or suspected cardiac causes, and consider bone-health assessment for fracture prevention. Agree an individualised plan, share it with the person, and review progress and adherence.

  • NICE NG249 recommendations (1.1.3-1.1.5)

Tinnitus: triage, referral and support

Checked against the cited sources by automated comparison, November 2026. Steps labelled Direction-supported follow the cited sources' direction in this chart's own wording, not the sources' verbatim text. Not clinically validated.

1

Same-day and urgent routes

URGENT

+

Refer immediately to a crisis mental health management team for assessment people who have tinnitus associated with a high risk of suicide; if needed, provide a safe place while waiting for the assessment (NG155 1.2.1). Refer immediately, in line with the NICE guideline on suspected neurological conditions, people with tinnitus associated with sudden onset of significant neurological symptoms or signs (for example, facial weakness), acute uncontrolled vestibular symptoms or suspected stroke (NG155 1.2.2). Refer to be seen within 24 hours tinnitus with sudden hearing loss (developing over 3 days or less, in the past 30 days). Refer to be seen within 2 weeks (NG155 1.2.5): tinnitus with distress affecting mental wellbeing (for example distress that prevents usual daily activities) even after receiving tinnitus support at first point of contact - refer in line with local pathways; or hearing loss that developed suddenly more than 30 days ago, or rapidly worsening hearing loss over 4 to 90 days - refer in line with the NICE guideline on hearing loss in adults. Consider referring people for tinnitus assessment and management in line with local pathways for persistent pulsatile tinnitus or persistent unilateral tinnitus (NG155 1.2.7 is consider-referring), and use the suspected-cancer pathway where NG12 criteria are met. CKS tinnitus) adds to the urgent-referral (seen within 2 weeks) list: persistent otalgia or otorrhoea that does not resolve with routine treatment (this limb is CKS content, not NG155).

2

Primary care assessment

ASSESS

+

Ask about onset (sudden or gradual), sidedness, pulsatile or non-pulsatile character, associated hearing loss, vertigo, otalgia or discharge, impact on sleep, concentration and mood, and noise exposure, ototoxic medicines and wax. Examine the ears and perform otoscopy; check for wax or infection and treat those first. Assess distress and its effect on daily life.

3

Refer to audiology

ASSESS

+

Offer an audiological assessment to people with tinnitus (NG155 1.4.1 - unqualified by hearing thresholds). Refer in line with local pathways for tinnitus assessment and management when tinnitus bothers the person despite having received tinnitus support at first point of contact, is persistent and objective, or is associated with unilateral or asymmetric hearing loss (1.2.6). Consider referral for persistent pulsatile tinnitus or persistent unilateral tinnitus (1.2.7). Arrange imaging only through the specialist route (MRI for asymmetric symptoms is a secondary-care decision).

4

Information and sound-based support

SUPPORT

+

At every stage, explain what tinnitus is, likely causes, that it often improves, and self-help options. In the tinnitus pathway, options include sound therapy (including amplification for hearing loss) and tinnitus counselling. Do not offer betahistine to treat tinnitus (NG155 1.5.5). Management is otherwise non-drug: CKS tinnitus lists reassurance, treating underlying causes such as impacted wax, sound therapy, psychological therapies and hearing aids, and advises reviewing medicines and, if appropriate, stopping a drug that may be causing tinnitus.

5

Psychological therapy for distress

SUPPORT

+

Consider CBT for tinnitus-related distress, delivered by appropriately trained professionals within the tinnitus service or via psychological therapy services. Screen for anxiety and depression and manage those in their own right (see the mental health review chart). Digital and group formats are options within the pathway.

6

Review and safety-net

REVIEW/LIMIT

+

Review people whose tinnitus persists, changes, or becomes unilateral, pulsatile or associated with other symptoms - these features change the route. Re-refer to audiology or ENT when symptoms progress despite support. Agree what the person should do if distress escalates, including crisis routes.

  • NICE NG155 recommendations (1.2.5)

Acute abdominal pain: triage and routing

Checked against the cited sources by automated comparison, November 2026. Steps labelled Direction-supported follow the cited sources' direction in this chart's own wording, not the sources' verbatim text. Not clinically validated.

1

Emergency admission: serious causes; torsion is time-critical

URGENT

+

Arrange emergency admission for people with a suspected life-threatening or serious cause of acute abdominal pain (CKS abdominal pain - acute, Scenario: Management, last revised January 2025), for example: abdominal sepsis; acute pancreatitis; aortic dissection; appendicitis; diabetic ketoacidosis; ectopic pregnancy with haemodynamic instability or significant concern about bleeding or pain; gastroenteritis where the person cannot retain fluids or shows shock or severe dehydration; hepatic abscess; hernial incarceration; intestinal obstruction; ischaemia; Mallory-Weiss tear; obstetric emergencies; perforated duodenal or gastric ulcer; peritonitis; ruptured abdominal aortic aneurysm; testicular torsion; typhlitis (neutropenic enterocolitis). Arrange urgent referral for suspected cancer, using a suspected cancer pathway referral or arranging investigations as appropriate (same CKS page). Arrange referral for suspected cholecystitis, Fitz-Hugh Curtis syndrome, gallstones, hepatitis B, or kidney stones; for endometriosis if symptoms are severe, persistent or recurrent; and for fibroids if symptoms cannot be managed in primary care (same CKS page). People with a new or pre-existing diagnosis who do not require referral are managed appropriately - the same page lists acute diverticulitis, constipation, Crohn's disease, dyspepsia, endometriosis, fibroids, gastroenteritis or infectious colitis, opioid withdrawal, pelvic inflammatory disease, pyelonephritis, ulcerative colitis and urinary tract infection. Suspected sickle cell crisis follows the CKS sickle-cell-disease topic (Scenario: Management - sickle cell crisis, last revised January 2025): arrange immediate admission for severe pain not controlled by simple analgesia or low-dose opioids, dehydration from severe vomiting or diarrhoea, severe sepsis or temperature over 38.5C (over 38C under age 2, below 36C, or hypotension), acute chest syndrome features, new neurological symptoms or signs, acute fall in haemoglobin, acute enlargement of spleen or liver, marked increase in jaundice, any change in vision, haematuria, or fulminant priapism; arrange urgent in-hospital assessment when pain is not controlled by home measures. Suspected testicular torsion (sudden-onset scrotal pain with torsion suspected clinically): arrange emergency hospital admission to urology or paediatric surgery, depending on clinical judgement (CKS Scrotal pain and swelling, Scenario: Testicular torsion, last revised April 2026). Previous severe, self-limiting episodes with no current scrotal pain or swelling: arrange a urology referral, the urgency depending on the frequency and duration of episodes (same CKS scenario, close paraphrase). Scope: the chart's stated lower bound is 1 month of age but the content is adult-oriented; no paediatric threshold is asserted (no paediatric primary source opened) - for infants and children use a paediatric assessment route with age-specific observations and fever/sepsis thresholds, and keep safeguarding (including non-accidental injury) in mind for unexplained or recurrent presentations.

2

Red flags that raise urgency

URGENT

+

Medical: sepsis signs, jaundice, significant dehydration, polyuria/polydipsia (DKA), purpuric rash, respiratory symptoms. Surgical: recent significant abdominal trauma or surgery, irreducible hernia, peritonism (guarding, rebound, constant pain worse on movement), obstruction features (colicky pain, absolute constipation, bilious vomiting), testicular or labial pain, "red currant jelly" stool in children. Other: distension, bilious or blood-stained vomit, blood in stool, palpable mass, severe or increasing pain, unintentional weight loss.

3

Assess: vitals, history, examination

ASSESS

+

Measure blood pressure, pulse, temperature and capillary refill. Take onset, duration, severity, progression, radiation and quality of pain; location; associated symptoms; previous episodes; bowel habit change; urinary features; trauma or bleeding; past medical/surgical history; medicines; travel; smoking, alcohol and substances; family history - and for women, menstrual, sexual, contraceptive and pregnancy history. Examine guided by pain location (inspection, auscultation, percussion, palpation; selective DRE, pelvic and scrotal examination). Presentations are often atypical in older people, the immunocompromised, people living with obesity, and pregnancy.

4

Investigate without delaying admission; pregnancy changes the route

ASSESS

+

Arrange investigations depending on the assessment and suspected cause, and DO NOT DELAY ADMISSION to wait for the results of investigations (CKS abdominal pain - acute, Investigations, January 2025 via cloud browser). Initial tests to consider: full blood count; urea and electrolytes; liver function test; blood glucose; C-reactive protein; urinalysis; pregnancy test for all women of childbearing age. Other primary-care investigations as indicated: amylase and lipase (suspected pancreatitis); chlamydia and gonorrhoea testing (women at risk of sexually transmitted infections); coagulation screen (suspected vascular cause); markers for specific diseases (for example coeliac disease or inflammatory bowel disease); quantitative faecal immunochemical testing (suspected malignancy); serum lactic acid (suspected acute mesenteric ischaemia); stool culture (suspected infectious cause). Keep the pregnancy test for women of childbearing age, and state what a positive or possible pregnancy changes: the differential (ectopic pregnancy must be considered, with or without bleeding), the investigation route (radiation choices), drug choices and the admission threshold all shift. Pregnant with stomach ache: urgent GP appointment or NHS 111 the same day (NHS stomach-ache page). Positive test with pain or tenderness: refer to the early pregnancy assessment service the same day. the early-pregnancy-assessment-service referral is current recommendation 1.4.7 (the draft-era 1.3.7 numbering is superseded). Pregnancy red flags to ask about: vaginal bleeding or discharge, shoulder-tip pain, dizziness or syncope in early pregnancy.

5

Refer or manage the suspected cause

SUPPORT

+

Urgent referral: suspected cancer (suspected-cancer pathway per NG12). Stable suspected ectopic pregnancy: exclude ectopic pregnancy even without risk factors - about a third have none (NG126 1.4.8); symptoms include shoulder-tip pain and dizziness or fainting (1.4.3) and examination signs include peritoneal signs (1.4.4); refer immediately to the early pregnancy assessment service (out-of-hours gynaecology if unavailable), to be seen within 24 hours when the clinical situation warrants (1.4.7, 1.3.4); unstable patients or significant pain/bleeding go directly to A and E (1.4.1). Refer: cholecystitis, Fitz-Hugh Curtis syndrome, gallstones, hepatitis B, kidney stones, endometriosis (severe, persistent or recurrent) and fibroids beyond primary-care control. Manage in primary care per the relevant CKS topic: acute diverticulitis, Crohn's disease, ulcerative colitis, constipation, dyspepsia/GORD, gastroenteritis, pelvic inflammatory disease, pyelonephritis, UTI, opioid withdrawal. Advise appropriate analgesia.

•

Refer with an explicit urgency tier

KEY

+

Tier every route, do not leave one undifferentiated list. SAME-DAY: suspected complicated acute diverticulitis - refer for same-day hospital assessment if the person has uncontrolled abdominal pain and any feature in NG147 table 1 (perforation or peritonitis, abscess, sepsis, obstruction) - NG147 1.3.2. URGENT 2-WEEK: suspected cancer via the NG12 suspected-cancer pathway. ROUTINE REFERRAL: cholecystitis, Fitz-Hugh Curtis syndrome, gallstones, hepatitis B, kidney stones, endometriosis (severe, persistent or recurrent) and fibroids beyond primary-care control - confirm the actual timescale against the owning CKS topic at referral, since several of these can deteriorate same-day. MANAGE IN PRIMARY CARE per the relevant CKS topic, UNCOMPLICATED ONLY: acute diverticulitis, Crohn's disease, ulcerative colitis, constipation, dyspepsia/GORD, gastroenteritis, pelvic inflammatory disease, pyelonephritis, UTI, opioid withdrawal - a flare or complication changes the tier.

6

Safety-net with named 999 and 111 triggers

REVIEW/LIMIT

+

For people managed in primary care without a confirmed diagnosis, agree explicit safety-netting with named destinations. CALL 999 OR GO TO A&E NOW (NHS stomach-ache page, reviewed 20 Oct 2026) if stomach ache starts suddenly and is severe; vomiting blood or vomit looks green or like ground coffee; a lot of blood from the bottom or black sticky poo; cannot pee at all; cannot poo or fart at all; a rash that does not fade when pressed (harder to see on brown or black skin); struggling to breathe. Do not drive to A&E. URGENT GP APPOINTMENT OR NHS 111 the same day for worsening severe or increasing pain, persistent vomiting, fever, syncope, or any pregnancy. Arrange review of results and symptoms within a timeframe matched to the presentation, and reassess the route if the picture changes - including out of hours.

Mental health review: depression follow-up and SMI physical health

Checked against the cited sources by automated comparison, November 2026. Steps labelled Direction-supported follow the cited sources' direction in this chart's own wording, not the sources' verbatim text. Not clinically validated.

1

Risk first: suicide, self-harm and crisis

URGENT

+

Assess risk at the review and always when presentation changes. Monitor suicidal ideation particularly in the early weeks of antidepressant treatment. Where risk is identified, agree and record a risk management and crisis plan; use urgent mental health / crisis routes where there is considerable immediate risk to self or others, or inability to maintain safety (NG222 1.2.9: considerable immediate risk -> refer urgently to specialist mental health services). Depression with psychotic symptoms without that immediate risk: offer referral to specialist mental health services, not an automatic urgent/crisis route (NG222 1.12.1,- 'Offer referral', no urgency qualifier); urgency follows the risk, not the psychosis alone.

•

Name the crisis routes

URGENT

+

Every risk-management or crisis plan must say HOW to reach help: immediate risk to life - 999; urgent mental-health help - NHS 111 and select the mental-health option (in Sussex this connects to the 24/7 mental health crisis line - Sussex Partnership NHS FT page, or the local crisis resolution and home treatment team; someone to talk to - Samaritans 116 123 (free, 24 hours). NG222 1.16.9 requires care plans to include information about 24-hour support services and how to contact them, and a crisis plan identifying triggers and strategies. (The Sussex crisis-line example is area-specific - local arrangements vary.)

•

Suicide risk: ask directly and act

URGENT

+

NG222 safeguards: 1.2.8 always ask people with depression directly about suicidal ideation and intent; if there is a risk of self-harm or suicide, assess social support and awareness of help sources, arrange help appropriate to need, and advise seeking further help if the situation deteriorates. 1.2.11 advise the person and their family or carer to be vigilant for mood changes, agitation, negativity, hopelessness and suicidal ideation, and to contact their practitioner if concerned - particularly when starting or changing treatment and at times of increased personal stress. 1.2.12 if assessed at risk of suicide: do not withhold treatment for depression on the basis of suicide risk; take into account toxicity in overdose if an antidepressant is prescribed and if necessary limit the amount of medicine available; consider increasing support. 1.4.23 take into account toxicity in overdose for people at significant risk; do not routinely start TCAs, except lofepramine, as they carry the greatest risk in overdose. Agree and record a risk management and crisis plan.

2

Review the whole picture

ASSESS

+

Assess need and how treatment is working; take into account physical health problems, coexisting mental health problems, previous treatment experiences and engagement factors. Use routine outcome monitoring where appropriate (for example PHQ-9), check treatment concordance, side effects and harms, and review any care plan at agreed intervals. Stopping antidepressants: what gradual means

REVIEW/LIMIT

+

Continue treatment for at least 6 months after remission, reviewed regularly (NG222 1.4.11). On stopping (NG222 1.4.12-1.4.21): explain that dose is usually reduced in stages (tapering) and most people stop successfully; withdrawal symptoms can appear within a few days of stopping abruptly, missing doses or reducing, vary between people, and usually resolve in 1-2 weeks but can last several weeks or months; take into account the pharmacokinetic profile (short half-life needs a slower taper) and treatment duration; taper speed is led by the person; if severe withdrawal occurs, restart at the previous dose and slow the taper. Follow NG215 for the shared-decision stopping conversation.

4

SMI: six annual physical health checks

SUPPORT

+

For everyone on the SMI register (schizophrenia, bipolar affective disorder and other psychoses; the PHSMI denominator excludes patients recorded as in remission), the NHS England PHSMI core metric is all six component parts at any point in the preceding 12 months: smoking status, alcohol status, BMI, blood pressure, lipid profile, and blood glucose or HbA1c as clinically appropriate. The six-in-12-months standard (the PHSMI core metric) and the national 60% target (a Five Year Forward View for Mental Health commitment - 60% of people on the General Practice SMI register receiving a full and comprehensive physical health check, per the NHS England Physical Health Checks for People with SMI Statistical Press Notice 2023-24 Q4; the Long Term Plan successor measure is 390,000 people with SMI receiving a full annual physical health check in 2023-24) are national programme measures, NOT a QOF indicator. QOF 2026/27 (PRN02356, November 2026 update) has separate per-indicator rules: MH002 care plan; MH003 blood pressure; MH006 BMI; MH007 alcohol; MH011 lipid profile in the preceding 12 months for people currently prescribed antipsychotics and/or with pre-existing cardiovascular conditions and/or who smoke and/or are overweight (BMI at least 23 kg/m2, or at least 25 kg/m2 if ethnicity is recorded as White), otherwise the preceding 24 months; MH012 blood glucose or HbA1c in the preceding 12 months, excluding patients with a diagnosis of diabetes. There is no MH001 register indicator and no single all-six QOF indicator in 2026/27. The fuller NHS England check also covers substance misuse and medication side effects. Record results and act on what they show - the checks alone do not improve outcomes. Act on what the checks show: offer the corresponding follow-up interventions as results indicate, including alcohol intervention and weight-management follow-up (the PHSMI programme explicitly monitors uptake of the corresponding relevant follow-up interventions and access to national cancer screening programmes - NHS England Digital PHSMI page; QOF 2026/27 MH007 alcohol-consumption record and MH006 BMI record, previously.

Antipsychotic physical-health monitoring is not the six checks

REVIEW/LIMIT

+

The QOF/PHSMI six checks are a population screen; they do not discharge the antipsychotic monitoring schedule (CG178). 1.3.6.1 baseline before starting: weight (plotted), waist circumference, pulse and BP, fasting glucose or HbA1c, lipid profile and prolactin, movement-disorder assessment, nutritional/diet/activity assessment. 1.3.6.2 ECG before starting if specified in the SmPC, specific cardiovascular risk on examination, personal CVD history, or inpatient admission. 1.3.6.4 during treatment: weight weekly for the first 6 weeks, then at 12 weeks, 1 year and annually; waist annually; pulse/BP, fasting glucose/HbA1c and lipids at 12 weeks, 1 year and annually; plus response, side effects, movement disorders, adherence and overall physical health. 1.3.6.5 the secondary care team keeps responsibility for this monitoring for at least the first 12 months or until stabilised, whichever is longer; transfer to primary care only under shared care arrangements.

Smoking cessation in psychosis: CG178 exceptions

REVIEW/LIMIT

+

Offer help to stop smoking even if previous attempts were unsuccessful - but with the psychosis-specific exceptions (CG178): 1.1.2.3 be aware that reducing cigarette smoking can significantly change the metabolism of other drugs, particularly clozapine and olanzapine (levels can rise - plan monitoring). 1.1.2.4 do NOT offer bupropion to people with psychosis to help them stop smoking; warn people taking bupropion or varenicline about increased risk of adverse neuropsychiatric symptoms and monitor regularly, particularly in the first 2 to 3 weeks.
