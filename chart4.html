    1

    Listen to concerns and goals

    ASSESS

    +

    Ask: what feels difficult, what helps, what are the person's strengths, and what would assessment change? Agree communication/sensory adjustments and who may contribute history. Respect consent and confidentiality. Direction-supported: the cited sources support this direction; the wording is this chart's summary, not the sources' own words.

    2

    Ask about lifelong patterns

    ASSESS

    +

    Explore social communication, reciprocal interaction, routines/repetitive interests, change and sensory differences; ask about early development and effects at home, education, work and relationships. Obtain examples, not stereotypes. Direction-supported: the cited sources support this direction; the wording is this chart's summary, not the sources' own words.

    3

    Adult assessment route

    SUPPORT

    +

    Consider AQ-10 only if no moderate/severe learning disability. Score ≥6 OR clinical suspicion, including informant history, supports comprehensive assessment. For moderate/severe learning disability use the separate CG142 brief-assessment route, not this cut-off.

    4

    Children: concerns across settings

    ASSESS

    +

    Ask child/parent concerns, duration, severity, development and functional impact; seek nursery/school observations. Masking, good eye contact, verbal ability or a negative tool do not rule autism out. Consider other explanations and coexisting needs. Direction-supported: the cited sources support this direction; the wording is this chart's summary, not the sources' own words.

    5

    Loss of skills needs a distinct route

    KEY

    +

    Language/social regression under 3: refer to autism team. Language regression over 3 or motor regression at any age: refer first to paediatrician/paediatric neurologist. Use clinical advice for boundary/atypical cases; do not leave regression on a routine checklist.

    6

    Send a useful referral

    SUPPORTREFER

    +

    Record concerns and observed examples, developmental/medical history and prior reports if available. East Sussex adults: healthcare-professional referral via local Assessment and Treatment Service using NDS form. East Sussex CAMHS child referrals use Single Point of Advice; younger autism routes involve community paediatrics. (Area-specific example: East Sussex – local routes and services vary.)

    7

    England Right to Choose

    SUPPORT

    +

    For a clinically appropriate first elective referral, discuss providers holding a qualifying NHS Standard Contract with any ICB or NHS England for the required service and age group (NHS England patient-choice guidance). Prior commissioner approval is not required when the legal right applies. Documented source conflict: the patient-facing ICB FAQ uses narrower wording (contract with NHS Surrey Heartlands or NHS England), while the local GP guide (updated 8 Sept 2026, Q5) matches the national rule; use the national legal criteria, and record the conflict rather than letting the FAQ veto a lawful referral.

    8

    Check the whole pathway first

    REVIEW/LIMIT

    +

    Ask what assessment, follow-up, support and any coexisting ADHD treatment are included; check waits, accessibility and onward care. ICB GP guide: once a GP makes a Right to Choose referral, the patient's care should transfer to that provider and the patient should not remain on the local NHS waiting list; it is recommended that the original provider is informed when making the second referral, so the patient can be removed from the original waiting list – avoiding duplication, clinical risk and artificially long lists. Do not cancel a referral without an agreed handover. Private self-pay is outside NHS Right to Choose. (Area-specific example: East Sussex – local routes and services vary.)

    9

    Support need not wait for diagnosis

    SUPPORT

    +

    Use school/SENCo and needs-based support; Amaze ND Family Support is for local families with under-25s before/after assessment. Adults can use NAS community/branches and local peer groups. Peer support is not diagnosis, clinical treatment or crisis care. (Area-specific example: East Sussex – local routes and services vary.)

    1

    Ask for concrete examples

    ASSESS

    +

    Explore inattention, impulsivity/hyperactivity, childhood onset, persistence, settings and impact on learning, work, home and relationships. Ask what helps and what support is wanted. ADHD may be under-recognised in girls/women.

    2

    Check other needs and explanations

    ASSESS

    +

    Record developmental and mental-health history, physical health, sleep/substance concerns and other neurodevelopmental conditions. Seek observer reports with consent. Scales and school reports add evidence; a score alone is not diagnosis.

    3

    Child impairment determines urgency

    ASSESS

    +

    Severe impairment: direct secondary-care referral. Persistent problems with at least moderate impairment: specialist assessment; NG87 also allows up to 10 weeks watchful waiting with ADHD-focused parent support when appropriate. Do not postpone severe cases for a parenting course.

    4

    Under 5: parent support first

    SUPPORT

    +

    NG87 recommends ADHD-focused group parent training as first-line for under-5s with ADHD. Persistent significant impairment after environmental modifications/training needs specialist ADHD advice. NG87 1.5.9: do not offer medication for ADHD for any child under 5 years without a second specialist opinion from an ADHD service with expertise in managing ADHD in young children (ideally a tertiary service). NG87 1.2.9: primary care practitioners should not make the initial diagnosis or start medication in children or young people with suspected ADHD.

    5

    Adult referral criteria

    SUPPORTREFER

    +

    For an adult without a childhood diagnosis, refer to an ADHD-trained specialist when typical manifestations began in childhood, persisted, are not better explained by another psychiatric diagnosis, and cause moderate/severe impairment. Coexisting conditions can still be present.

    6

    Local assessment routes

    SUPPORT

    +

    East Sussex adult NDS referrals go from healthcare professionals via the local Assessment and Treatment Service. Children: the East Sussex route is the Single Point of Advice (SPoA), a 0–18 triage hub run by Sussex Partnership with East Sussex County Council; younger children need age-appropriate paediatric/specialist advice. Confirm current age and service acceptance with the service – do not rely on neighbouring-region criteria. (Area-specific example: East Sussex – local routes and services vary.)

    7

    England Right to Choose

    SUPPORT

    +

    For a clinically appropriate first elective referral, discuss providers holding a qualifying NHS Standard Contract with any ICB or NHS England for the required service and age group (NHS England patient-choice guidance). Prior commissioner approval is not required when the legal right applies. Documented source conflict: the patient-facing ICB FAQ uses narrower wording (contract with NHS Surrey Heartlands or NHS England), while the local GP guide (updated 8 Sept 2026, Q5) matches the national rule; use the national legal criteria, and record the conflict rather than letting the FAQ veto a lawful referral. This is an NHS referral route, not a self-pay assessment, and a referral does not itself produce a diagnosis.

    8

    Before choosing: prescribing, monitoring and reviews

    REVIEW/LIMIT

    +

    Specialist titration/dose stabilisation precedes shared care. Ask who prescribes, monitors physical health and performs specialist reviews, and what happens if GP shared care is declined. Local protocol requires GP agreement and ongoing specialist responsibilities; transfer is not guaranteed. No dose is supplied here. NG87 1.7.29: after titration and dose stabilisation, prescribing and monitoring of ADHD medication should be carried out under Shared Care Protocol arrangements with primary care. Monitoring schedule (NG87 1.8.5, 1.8.9-1.8.11, close paraphrase): height every 6 months in children and young people; weight every 3 months in children 10 years and under, at 3 and 6 months after starting treatment in children over 10 years and young people, and every 6 months thereafter, or more often if concerns arise; weight every 6 months in adults; heart rate and blood pressure before and after each dose change and every 6 months; if sustained resting tachycardia over 120 bpm, arrhythmia, or systolic blood pressure above the 95th percentile (or a clinically significant increase) on 2 occasions, reduce the dose and refer to a paediatric hypertension specialist or adult physician. A healthcare professional with training and expertise in managing ADHD reviews ADHD medication at least once a year (NG87 1.10.1, source term retained). Local provider caveat (Sussex GP guide): at least one named RTC provider discharges the patient back to the GP once stabilised and does not provide annual reviews, while the local Shared Care Protocols stipulate remaining under specialist care with regular specialist review – check the chosen provider's model against the SCP before committing; if the GP declines shared care, prescribing responsibility remains with the specialist. (Area-specific example: East Sussex – local routes and services vary.)

    9

    Support while waiting and afterwards

    SUPPORT

    +

    Needs-based school/work adjustments and family support can start without waiting for diagnosis. Local Amaze supports families of children and young people under 25 at any stage of the neurodevelopmental pathway, with or without a diagnosis; ADHD UK offers national online peer support; ADHD Aware runs online peer groups and an in-person adult support group in Hastings (Amaze listing). Check current dates and eligibility with organisers. (Area-specific example: East Sussex – local routes and services vary.)

    1

    Start with distress and safety

    ASSESS

    +

    Ask the child and carer what happens before/during/after episodes, what the child may need, and what helps. In autistic children assess pain/illness, communication difficulties, anxiety/other conditions, sensory/social environment and changes in routine before choosing intervention. Urgent safety or health concerns need appropriate clinical help, not a routine group. Risk context: CG170 1.1.8 lists assessing risk – including self-harm, harm to others, self-neglect, breakdown of family or residential support, and exploitation or abuse by others – among the skills that professionals working with autistic children and young people should be trained in (a staff-training recommendation, not a direct assessment order). Ask about these risks and follow local safeguarding procedures where indicated.

    2

    Agree a joined-up support plan

    SUPPORT

    +

    Coordinate home, nursery/school and health support around the child. If behaviour persists, seek multidisciplinary review; psychosocial support informed by functional assessment is first-line when no underlying trigger is found. Specialist medicines are not a routine primary-care behaviour solution. Source check: the functional-assessment framing is CG170 1.4.7: offer a psychosocial intervention informed by a functional assessment of behaviour as first-line treatment when no coexisting or environmental trigger is identified); multidisciplinary review is CG170 1.4.5; antipsychotics only when psychosocial interventions are insufficient, specialist-initiated and monitored (1.4.10).

    3

    Early years: 0–5

    TREATMENT

    +

    Talk with health visitor, nursery/SENCo and GP about development, communication, sensory needs and family support. East Sussex Family Hubs list SEND stay-and-play 0–5 and Sunflowers 2–5; specific parent courses list EarlyBird 2–4. Ask about the next intake rather than assuming a place. (Area-specific example: East Sussex – local routes and services vary.)

    4

    School age: support now

    TREATMENT

    +

    Speak to class teacher/SENCo about needs, adjustments and an assess-plan-do-review approach, including learning, communication, sensory needs, attendance and emotional wellbeing. School SEN support can be provided without an EHCP; keep the child's views central. Direction-supported: the cited sources support this direction; the wording is this chart's summary, not the sources' own words.

    5

    Parent programmes: use the right band

    TREATMENT

    +

    Published East Sussex courses include ADHD parenting 4+, EarlyBird Plus 4+, Teen Life 10+ and disability parenting 2+. Some are waitlisted or current cohorts already started. Amaze offers training/navigation for local under-25 families before/after assessment; ask which offer fits the child. (Area-specific example: East Sussex – local routes and services vary.)

    6

    Teenagers and transition

    TREATMENT

    +

    Include the young person in decisions; discuss school/college, mental health and preparing for adult services. Family Hubs list SEND youth activities spanning 8–25 across different groups. Amaze Amazing Futures offers youth peer activities up to 25; age bands and places vary. Plan health-service transition, not an automatic handover. (Area-specific example: East Sussex – local routes and services vary.)

    7

    When to ask about an EHC assessment

    MONITORING

    +

    For needs beyond available SEN support, discuss an EHC needs assessment with the setting/SENCo. Parents, the young person over 16, or the setting may request one. Council decides whether to assess, then whether a plan is needed; normal statutory timetable is up to 20 weeks, subject to exceptions. Seek SENDIASS advice if disputed.

    8

    Clinical referral and family breaks

    TREATMENTREFER

    +

    Neurodevelopmental assessment and mental-health/CAMHS care have different purposes; coexisting mental-health needs should not be left waiting for a diagnosis. Use the local referral instructions and age bands. Ask separately about parent-carer assessment, young-carer support and short breaks; see the carers page. Direction-supported: the cited sources support this direction; the wording is this chart's summary, not the sources' own words.

    1

    Recognise the caring role

    ASSESS

    +

    Unpaid practical or emotional support can count as caring. Ask what care is given, overnight demands, health/sleep, work/education, relationships, finances and what the carer is willing/able to continue. Tell the GP about the caring role and ask about support.

    2

    Adult caring for an adult

    SUPPORT

    +

    Under the Care Act, ask the adult social-care team for a carer's assessment where there appears to be a need for support. Contact the council for the person cared for. It is separate from their needs assessment and does not depend on receiving Carer's Allowance. Agree goals, support needs and a written plan/outcome.

    3

    Parent caring for a disabled child

    SUPPORT

    +

    Ask children's services about a parent-carer needs assessment and the child's own assessment, rather than applying the adult Care Act route indiscriminately. Consider impact on parent wellbeing and the whole family. Care for the Carers and Amaze offer local parent information/support. (Area-specific example: East Sussex – local routes and services vary.)

    4

    Young carers: under 18

    SUPPORT

    +

    Ask the council for a young-carer assessment, including education, wellbeing, wishes and whether the tasks are appropriate. In East Sussex statutory young-carer assessment is through Imago/East Sussex Young Carers. Care for the Carers activities are a separate service, not the statutory assessment. (Area-specific example: East Sussex – local routes and services vary.)

    5

    Plan a break before crisis

    SUPPORT

    +

    Options include sitting/home support, day activities, overnight/residential respite or supported holidays. Ask about carer and cared-for-person assessments, care needs, availability and costs. Council-funded respite is assessed, not automatic or always free; ask about personal budgets/direct payments if eligible.

    6

    Children's short breaks

    SUPPORT

    +

    East Sussex lists SEND short breaks for children/young people and siblings, mainly 0–19; some offers extend to 25. Contact providers about suitability and spaces. Do not confuse provider grant funding with individual family entitlement. Family support can be sought separately from a diagnosis. (Area-specific example: East Sussex – local routes and services vary.)

    7

    Check money and benefits carefully

    REVIEW/LIMIT

    +

    Carer's Allowance generally requires age 16+, at least 35 caring hours weekly, a qualifying disability benefit for the cared-for person, and education/earnings/residence conditions. Check the live GOV.UK rules and benefit interactions; assessment and support are not limited to people who qualify. No rate or earnings figure is hard-coded here.

    8

    Local help and contingency planning

    SUPPORT

    +

    Care for the Carers offers information, peer activities and parent/young-adult support; its young-carers service covers ages 5–17 (CFTC). East Sussex Young Carers (ESYC, county council) helps young carers aged 5 to 18 (council page) and holds the statutory young-carer assessment route via Imago. Signpost by service: ESYC/Imago for statutory assessment and 5–18 provision; CFTC for peer/activity support 5–17 and young-adult carer support 17-25. Confirm the current band with the service at referral – the two published bands differ. Contingency planning (East Sussex County Council carers' checklist): the East Sussex carers card identifies the person as a carer in an emergency and allows an emergency respite plan to be set up (it also gives retail/leisure discounts); funding is available for replacement care if the carer needs to attend a healthcare appointment or training course. Agree who can step in and how to access urgent help; do not rely on a group or a discount card as guaranteed emergency cover. (Area-specific example: East Sussex – local routes and services vary.)

    Joint pain: patterns, red flags and referral

    1

    Hot swollen joint: exclude infection

    URGENT

    +

    A sudden severely painful, hot/swollen joint needs urgent assessment; fever may be absent. If septic arthritis is suspected, refer immediately via the local pathway. Do not label a first painful red joint as gout without considering infection and CPP crystal arthritis.

    2

    Other urgent features

    URGENT

    +

    Major injury with severe pain, deformity, inability to bear weight or altered sensation needs emergency assessment. New headache/scalp tenderness, jaw/tongue claudication or visual symptoms with a PMR-type presentation suggest GCA and need immediate medical attention. Direction-supported: the cited sources support this direction; the wording is this chart's summary, not the sources' own words.

    3

    Describe the pattern, not a label

    ASSESS

    +

    Record onset/duration, joints and symmetry, swelling/heat, morning/rest stiffness, activity effects, function and systemic symptoms. Ask about skin/nails, eye symptoms, bowel/genitourinary illness and family history when SpA is possible. Look for true synovitis, enthesitis and dactylitis.

    4

    Persistent synovitis: RA route

    URGENT

    +

    Refer any adult with unexplained persistent synovitis. Urgent referral if small hand/foot joints, more than one joint, or ≥3 months delay before seeking advice, even with normal inflammatory markers or negative RF/anti-CCP. Do not wait for tests to refer.

    5

    Investigations should fit the question

    ASSESS

    +

    With clinical synovitis/suspected RA: RF; consider anti-CCP if RF negative; hand/foot X-rays for persistent synovitis. These must not delay referral. For suspected gout, measure serum urate; consider aspiration if uncertain, and repeat low flare urate ≥2 weeks after settling. No blanket diagnostic blood panel.

    6

    OA: activity-related pattern

    TREATMENT

    +

    NG226 clinical OA diagnosis: age ≥45, activity-related joint pain and no morning stiffness or stiffness ≤30 minutes. Do not routinely image unless atypical features or another diagnosis is suspected. A hot swollen joint or prolonged stiffness should not be forced into an OA label.

    7

    Gout or CPP crystal arthritis

    ASSESS

    +

    Rapid severe red/swollen episodes, often overnight, support crystal disease but are not proof. Gout often involves first MTP; CPP attacks commonly involve knee/wrist. A serum urate alone does not distinguish all causes. Use aspiration/imaging when needed and keep infection on the differential. Direction-supported: the cited sources support this direction; the wording is this chart's summary, not the sources' own words.

    8

    Psoriatic / peripheral SpA

    URGENT

    +

    Ask about psoriasis/nails, dactylitis, enthesitis, uveitis and IBD. Urgently refer suspected new inflammatory arthritis via its appropriate route – NG65 1.1.8 (close paraphrase): urgently refer people with suspected new-onset inflammatory arthritis to a rheumatologist for a spondyloarthritis assessment, UNLESS rheumatoid arthritis, gout or acute calcium pyrophosphate (CPP) arthritis is suspected; if RA is suspected, use the NICE rheumatoid-arthritis referral route instead. Refer dactylitis; refer non-mechanical enthesitis if persistent, multisite OR linked to NG65 associated features. Open the full feature list. Painful red light-sensitive/blurred eye needs same-day ophthalmology.

    9

    Inflammatory back pain: exact SpA criteria

    ASSESS

    +

    For back pain starting before 45 and lasting >3 months, use the full NG65 additional-feature list: refer if ≥4; if exactly 3, test HLA-B27 and refer if positive. Do not improvise a shortened score. Negative tests or normal X-rays do not alone exclude SpA. Safety-net (NG65 1.1.6, close paraphrase): if the criteria are not met but clinical suspicion of axial spondyloarthritis remains, advise the person to seek repeat assessment if new signs, symptoms or risk factors listed in 1.1.5 develop – especially with current or past inflammatory bowel disease, psoriasis or uveitis.

    10

    PMR: shoulder/hip girdle pattern

    ASSESS

    +

    Consider in over-50s with bilateral shoulder/pelvic girdle pain and prolonged stiffness; check mimics, inflammatory markers and GCA. Before starting corticosteroids, arrange the CKS pre-steroid baseline panel to rule out other conditions: FBC, U&Es, LFTs, calcium, ALP, protein electrophoresis, TSH, CK, rheumatoid factor and dipstick urinalysis (CKS polymyalgia rheumatica diagnosis, last revised February 2024). If GCA is suspected, do not delay steroids for tests – CKS: 'immediate high doses of oral steroids are required to reduce the risk of serious complications, such as vision loss'. For confirmed PMR, the 2026 BSR guideline (Rheumatology 2026;65(10):keag502; supersedes BSR/BHPR 2009): consider discussing with rheumatology after any relapse (for example via Advice and Guidance) and refer after a second relapse (overarching principle 5); refer everyone after 18 months or more of glucocorticoid therapy; discuss with people recently diagnosed the option of referral to consider adding methotrexate (Recommendation 6). Starting dose if PMR is confirmed (Recommendation 1, verbatim): 'Treat newly diagnosed PMR with 12.5–15 mg prednisolone daily, increasing to a maximum of 25 mg daily if necessary for symptom control. After symptoms have been controlled for 2–4 weeks, begin to taper the dose.' Bone protection (Recommendation 2): 'Prescribe bisphosphonates for people with PMR starting glucocorticoid therapy unless contraindicated.' SOURCE CONFLICT (presented, not resolved): NICE NG259 1.7.3 (July 2026) bases treatment on risk assessment (for example 15 mg or more prednisolone daily for 3 months or more, or T-score -1.5 or less with 5 mg or more daily for over 3 months); BSR 2026 recommends bisphosphonates for all people with PMR starting glucocorticoids. The more protective option is the BSR recommendation; the prescriber decides and records the reason. Give steroid counselling, sick-day rules and a Steroid Emergency Card at initiation (BSR monitoring table). No diagnostic steroid trial is supplied here.

    11

    Reactive or post-viral patterns

    ASSESS

    +

    Recent gastrointestinal/genitourinary infection can precede reactive arthritis; post-viral arthritis can mimic RA. Use history, examination and targeted infection testing where indicated, not an assumed benign diagnosis. Persistent synovitis still needs appropriate referral; eye symptoms may be urgent. Direction-supported: the cited sources support this direction; the wording is this chart's summary, not the sources' own words.

    12

    Widespread pain / fibromyalgia

    ASSESS

    +

    Widespread pain, fatigue and unrefreshing sleep can suggest fibromyalgia; there is no single diagnostic test. Examine for alternative disease, especially synovitis, and assess symptoms/function. Fibromyalgia and inflammatory arthritis can coexist; do not dismiss new swelling because of an existing diagnosis.

    13

    Document uncertainty and review

    MONITORING

    +

    Record working differential, tests/referrals and who will review results, plus return advice for new hot swelling, systemic illness, visual symptoms or deterioration. Use the relevant condition chart for subsequent management; this page does not select a treatment or rule out every cause.

    Fibromyalgia and chronic primary pain

    1

    Listen and map the impact

    ASSESS

    +

    Acknowledge pain as real. Ask about widespread pain, fatigue, unrefreshing sleep, cognitive symptoms, daily function, mood, work, caring roles, strengths and goals. Allow enough time; a further appointment may be needed. Normal tests do not invalidate pain.

    2

    Look for other disease and urgent concerns

    URGENT

    +

    Do not attribute new focal neurological findings, systemic illness, unexplained weight loss, hot swollen joints or other concerning changes to fibromyalgia. Assess the symptom-specific red flags and arrange urgent/immediate assessment when indicated. Fibromyalgia can coexist with other disease. Direction-supported: the cited sources support this direction; the wording is this chart's summary, not the sources' own words.

    3

    Clinical diagnosis, not a blood-test label

    ASSESS

    +

    An adequately experienced clinician can make the diagnosis; specialist confirmation is not compulsory in a clear case. There is no confirmatory test and it is not simply a diagnosis of exclusion. Use history/examination, ideally face to face, and consider mimics and coexisting conditions.

    4

    Use the full RCP diagnostic worksheet

    ASSESS

    +

    RCP uses ACR 2016 criteria: WPI ≥7 with SSS ≥5, or WPI 4–6 with SSS ≥9; pain in at least 4 of 5 regions; symptoms at a similar level for at least 3 months. Use the full definitions and clinician judgement. No self-completed score alone or online auto-diagnosis.

    5

    Investigate mimics without endless retesting

    ASSESS

    +

    RCP lists FBC, ESR/CRP, CK, LFTs, TSH, blood glucose and U&Es to assess alternatives/coexisting disease. Further tests depend on clinical suspicion. Explain the purpose; avoid repeating investigations after diagnosis without new symptoms/signs. A normal panel does not itself diagnose fibromyalgia.

    6

    Explain and agree a shared plan

    TREATMENT

    +

    Explain the working diagnosis, uncertainty, likely fluctuation and what management can improve. Agree meaningful function/quality-of-life goals, written information and support. Treat coexisting disease in parallel. Do not imply symptoms are imaginary or promise a cure.

    7

    Tailored activity and psychological care

    TREATMENT

    +

    Offer a supervised group exercise programme for chronic primary pain, adapted to needs, preferences and abilities; support ongoing activity. Consider ACT or CBT for pain from appropriately trained staff. Review tolerance and goals rather than imposing a fixed exercise escalation.

    8

    Other non-drug options: preserve limits

    SUPPORT

    +

    NG193 allows consideration of one course of acupuncture/dry needling only within its stated limits – NG193 1.2.5 (close paraphrase): a single course for people aged 16 years and over, delivered in a community setting, by a band 7 (equivalent or lower) healthcare professional with appropriate training, made up of no more than 5 hours of healthcare professional time. Use the original recommendation. Do not offer TENS, ultrasound, interferential therapy or biofeedback for chronic primary pain.

    9

    Medicines: chronic primary pain is distinct

    SUPPORT

    +

    For age ≥18, NICE says consider one of its specified antidepressants – NG193 1.2.7 names amitriptyline, citalopram, duloxetine, fluoxetine, paroxetine or sertraline – after a full discussion of benefits and harms; in April 2021 this was an off-label use. NG193 1.2.9: explain these medicines may help with quality of life, pain, sleep and psychological distress even in the absence of a diagnosis of depression (both close paraphrase). NG193/CKS describe off-label use; check the current product licence, BNF, interactions and local policy. Seek specialist advice for age 16–17. No medicine or dose is selected here.

    10

    Do not start an analgesic ladder for primary pain

    MONITORING

    +

    For chronic primary pain, NG193 says not to initiate opioids, gabapentinoids, NSAIDs or paracetamol, among other listed medicines. This is indication-specific, not a ban on treating a separate condition. Check the full exclusions; do not import pregabalin-first US/EULAR prescribing into this NICE pathway.

    11

    Review existing medicines safely

    MONITORING

    +

    If already taking a medicine NG193 advises against starting, review benefit and harm together. Agree safe continuation if benefit and few harms, or supported reduction when appropriate. Discuss withdrawal; do not abruptly stop antidepressants or dependence-forming medicines. Track the agreed plan.

    12

    Refer for uncertainty, complexity or unmet need

    TREATMENTREFER

    +

    Seek relevant specialist advice when diagnosis is uncertain, findings suggest inflammatory/neurological disease, complexity exceeds skills, or pain/distress remains poorly controlled despite primary care. Clear fibromyalgia alone does not mandate rheumatology. Match the referral to the clinical question and local criteria.

    13

    Follow up and reassess changes

    MONITORING

    +

    Agree who reviews function, symptoms, mood, treatment benefit/harms, referrals and new concerns. Reassess a flare or changed presentation; investigate new symptoms as indicated rather than assuming every problem is fibromyalgia. Offer suitable patient information and peer support, not as a replacement for clinical care.

    Show what the chart says

    (1) First. Step 1, Listen and map the impact.

    Acknowledge pain as real. Ask about widespread pain, fatigue, unrefreshing sleep, cognitive symptoms, daily function, mood, work, caring roles, strengths and goals. Allow enough time; a further appointment may be needed. Normal tests do not invalidate pain.

    (2) Next. Step 6, Explain and agree a shared plan.

    Explain the working diagnosis, uncertainty, likely fluctuation and what management can improve. Agree meaningful function/quality-of-life goals, written information and support. Treat coexisting disease in parallel. Do not imply symptoms are imaginary or promise a cure.

    (3) Watch for and review. Step 13, Follow up and reassess changes.

    Agree who reviews function, symptoms, mood, treatment benefit/harms, referrals and new concerns. Reassess a flare or changed presentation; investigate new symptoms as indicated rather than assuming every problem is fibromyalgia. Offer suitable patient information and peer support, not as a replacement for clinical care.
