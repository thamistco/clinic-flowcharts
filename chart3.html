How to read these charts.

Every step has been accounted for and inspected in the current re-check; many steps have also been checked against current UK sources (November 2026) by automated comparison. Labels used in the step text:

Obesity: adult weight management

Checked against the cited sources by automated comparison, November 2026. Steps labelled Direction-supported follow the cited sources' direction in this chart's own wording, not the sources' verbatim text. Not clinically validated.

  1. Box 1ASSESSAsk permission; assess the whole person

    Ask permission to discuss weight and measure it. Address the presenting problem first. Use non-stigmatising language, personal health goals, weight history, medicines, eating-disorder risk and social/mental-health context.

  2. Box 2ASSESSMeasure risk and treat comorbidity

    Use BMI with judgement; add waist-to-height ratio if BMI <35. Use ethnicity-adjusted thresholds, not one cut-off for everyone. Identify and manage diabetes, BP, lipids and other comorbidities now, not only after weight loss.

  3. Box 3SUPPORTAgree sustainable support

    Agree realistic health goals and a suitable behavioural/diet/activity programme. Consider preferences, access, finances and previous attempts. Medicines follow starting and evaluating these approaches and continue alongside reduced-calorie diet and increased activity.

  4. Box 4DRUGTirzepatide: eligibility is not access

    NICE TA1026 eligibility: BMI >=35 plus >=1 weight-related comorbidity, with ethnic adjustment and commercial terms. England primary-care rollout is narrower and dated (NHS England interim commissioning guidance, Table 1 [2026] amendments): we are in funding-variation Year 2 (2026/27), which funds Cohorts I and II - Cohort I: >=4 qualifying comorbidities with BMI >=40; Cohort II: >=4 qualifying comorbidities with BMI 35-39.9; qualifying comorbidities are hypertension, dyslipidaemia, obstructive sleep apnoea, cardiovascular disease and type 2 diabetes; BMI thresholds usually reduced by 2.5 kg/m2 for people from South Asian, Chinese, other Asian, Middle Eastern, Black African or African-Caribbean backgrounds. Year 3 (2027/28 and 2028/29) is planned to add Cohort III (3 qualifying comorbidities, BMI >=40), at which point Cohorts I-III are funded. Specialist weight-management services use the full TA1026 criteria. Confirm the local ICB service and wraparound support.

  5. Box 5DRUGSemaglutide: two distinct NICE routes

    TA875 weight management: specialist multidisciplinary service, maximum 2 years, at least 1 comorbidity and BMI >=35, or BMI 30-34.9 meeting specialist-referral criteria (TA875 1.1). TA1152 cardiovascular prevention: up to a maintenance dose of 2.4 mg once weekly in adults with established CVD and BMI >=27 (TA1152 1.1). Licence-vs-funding ceiling (presented with exact scope, not interchangeable): the Wegovy SmPC (emc 13799 section 4.2) escalates 0.25 mg (weeks 1-4), 0.5 (5-8), 1 (9-12), 1.7 (13-16) to the 2.4 mg maintenance dose for ALL indications; 7.2 mg once weekly is only 'for weight management in patients with obesity, if needed', after a minimum of 4 weeks on 2.4 mg; administer either 1 injection of 7.2 mg or 3 injections of 2.4 mg one after each other, depending on the device (the single-dose 7.2 mg pen was MHRA-approved 14 Apr 2026; the January 2026 authorisation required the three-injection form). MHRA (14 Apr 2026,: the 7.2 mg dose does not apply to patients with BMI below 30 kg/m2 or to those using Wegovy to lower cardiovascular risk. 7.2 mg is therefore a licence maximum for BMI >=30 weight management only: it is not a TA875- or TA1152-funded dose, does not extend the TA875 2-year maximum, and does not apply to the CVD-prevention route (2.4 mg ceiling). Check the live SmPC/BNF for current administration and local commissioning before prescribing. Use lower BMI thresholds (usually reduced by 2.5 kg/m2) for people from South Asian, Chinese, other Asian, Middle Eastern, Black African or African-Caribbean family backgrounds (TA875 1.1).

  6. Box 6DRUGLiraglutide or orlistat: different criteria

    Liraglutide TA664: BMI ≥35 (32.5 in specified ethnic groups), non-diabetic hyperglycaemia AND high CVD risk; secondary-care specialist tier 3 service. Orlistat: BMI ≥30, or ≥28 with risk factors. Do not treat these as interchangeable entry criteria.

  7. Box 7SUPPORTConsider specialist/surgical assessment

    Offer bariatric assessment for BMI ≥40, or 35–39.9 with a significant condition improved by weight loss, and agreement to long-term follow-up. Apply ethnic adjustment and the separate expedited recent-onset type 2 diabetes criteria. Referral is not automatic surgery.

  8. Box 8REVIEW/LIMITReview response and stopping rules

    Tirzepatide: decide benefit/risk if <5% loss after 6 months at highest tolerated dose. Semaglutide: consider stopping if <5% after 6 months. Liraglutide: stop if <5% after 12 weeks at 3 mg. Review safety, adherence and support after stopping.

  9. Box 9KEYSafety before and during treatment

    Pre-treatment checks are a synthesis of the individual product SmPCs (not class-level rules): contraception and pregnancy plans (tirzepatide 4.5: reduced efficacy of oral contraceptives cannot be excluded - advise switching to a non-oral contraceptive method, or adding a barrier method, for 4 weeks after initiation and after each dose escalation; semaglutide 4.6: women of childbearing potential are recommended to use contraception); renal and hepatic status (both products: no dose adjustment but limited experience in severe impairment; semaglutide is not recommended in end-stage renal disease; gastrointestinal adverse reactions can cause dehydration and deterioration in renal function); interactions (product 4.5 sections, including hypoglycaemia risk with sulfonylurea or insulin). Persistent severe abdominal pain needs urgent assessment for pancreatitis; assess dehydration; cases of pulmonary aspiration have been reported with GLP-1 receptor agonists under general anaesthesia or deep sedation - tell the anaesthetic team before procedures. Product-specific SmPC combinations: the semaglutide (Wegovy, section 4.5) and liraglutide (Saxenda) SmPCs state these should not be used in combination with other GLP-1 receptor agonist products; the tirzepatide (Mounjaro) SmPC carries no combination statement. Direction-supported: item-level support from the named product SmPCs; wording is this chart's summary.

  10. REVIEW/LIMITPlanning pregnancy on weight-management medication (per product)

    Per-product SmPC pregnancy-planning actions (product-level synthesis, not a class interval;against each product SmPC): Wegovy (semaglutide 2.4 mg): women of childbearing potential are recommended to use contraception; do not use in pregnancy; discontinue at least 2 months before a planned pregnancy (long half-life); do not use while breastfeeding; report any pregnancy to the Novo Nordisk UK pregnancy registry (SmPC 4.6). Mounjaro (tirzepatide): not recommended in women of childbearing potential not using contraception; discontinue at least 1 month before a planned pregnancy (SmPC 4.6) - a distinct rule from the oral-contraceptive switch-to-non-oral or added-barrier cover for 4 weeks after initiation and after each dose escalation (SmPC 4.5). Saxenda (liraglutide 3 mg): do not use in pregnancy; discontinue if the patient wishes to become pregnant or pregnancy occurs - the SmPC states no interval; do not use while breastfeeding (SmPC 4.6). Xenical (orlistat): no clinical data on exposed pregnancies, so exercise caution when prescribing to pregnant women (SmPC 4.6); orlistat may indirectly reduce oral-contraceptive availability with severe diarrhoea - an additional contraceptive method is recommended in that case (SmPC 4.4/4.5); contraindicated while breastfeeding (SmPC 4.3/4.6).

Sources: NG246; TA875 1.1 and TA1152 1.1; emc product 13799 Wegovy SmPC section 4.2; MHRA/GOV.UK news 14 Apr 2026.

Insomnia: adult assessment and treatment

Checked against the cited sources by automated comparison, November 2026. Steps labelled Direction-supported follow the cited sources' direction in this chart's own wording, not the sources' verbatim text. Not clinically validated.

  1. Box 1ASSESSAssess sleep and daytime impact

    Adults 18 and over; not a paediatric sleep pathway, sleep apnoea treatment, or a sedative withdrawal protocol. Clarify duration, frequency, sleep opportunity and daytime impairment. Review mood, medicines, caffeine/alcohol and shift work. A 2-week sleep diary can help. Snoring/witnessed apnoea, parasomnias or restless legs may need a different pathway.

  2. Box 2ASSESSTreat contributors; keep driving safe

    Address stressors, physical and mental-health problems. Advise the person not to drive if they feel sleepy; the DVLA must be informed if excessive sleepiness is having, or is likely to have, an adverse effect on driving. Seek specialist assessment for another sleep disorder or diagnostic uncertainty.

  3. Box 3SUPPORTShort-term

    behavioural measures first: For under 3 months, address triggers and sleep habits. If sleep hygiene fails, daytime impairment is severe with significant distress and insomnia is not likely to resolve soon, offer CBT for insomnia. Hypnotics are not routine: a selected short course (3-7 days) may be considered for acute distress, with review and a clear stop plan - lowest effective dose, no longer than 2 weeks, no repeats without review, and not for older people or in pregnancy/breastfeeding.

  4. Box 4SUPPORTChronic insomnia

    offer CBTi: Offer CBTi as first-line treatment for chronic insomnia in adults of any age, face-to-face or digitally as appropriate. Sleep hygiene supports treatment but is not a stand-alone substitute for CBTi. Avoid long-term benzodiazepine or Z-drug prescribing.

  5. Box 5DRUGDaridorexant

    apply TA922 criteria: Consider only for symptoms at least 3 nights/week for at least 3 months with daytime functioning considerably affected, when CBTi has been tried but not worked or is unavailable/unsuitable. Keep treatment as short as possible; assess within 3 months of starting and stop for inadequate response; if continued, review regularly. BNF adult dose: 50 mg once nightly within 30 minutes before bedtime; 25 mg may be more appropriate for some. Use 25 mg with moderate hepatic impairment or with moderate CYP3A4 inhibitors (also ciclosporin or ciprofloxacin) per the live monograph; avoid in severe hepatic impairment. Contraindicated in narcolepsy. Review CNS depressants, alcohol, respiratory disease (severe COPD unstudied), mood/suicide risk, pregnancy and breastfeeding. Leave about 9 hours between dosing and driving/machinery. A forgotten bedtime dose should not be taken during the night.

  6. Box 6REVIEW/LIMITReview and refer when needed

    Arrange follow-up review, for example 2-4 weeks depending on the clinical situation. If symptoms have not improved, reassess - consider CBTi if not already offered, alternative diagnoses and referral. Seek specialist advice if primary-care treatment fails, for suspected other sleep disorders, or for occupational at-risk groups (for example professional drivers). If there has been no response to the first hypnotic, do not prescribe another.

  • NICE CKS: Insomnia - assessment and managing insomnia (revised June 2026)
  • NICE TA922: Daridorexant for treating long-term insomnia (published October 2023)
  • BNF: Daridorexant (accessed 19 November 2026)
  • eMC SmPC: Quviviq (daridorexant)

Atopic eczema: adult review

Checked against the cited sources by automated comparison, November 2026. Steps labelled Direction-supported follow the cited sources' direction in this chart's own wording, not the sources' verbatim text. Not clinically validated.

  1. Box 1URGENTRecognise urgent infection

    Adults 18 and over; atopic eczema only, not all itchy rashes. Suspected eczema herpeticum is a medical emergency requiring prompt emergency hospital admission. Consider severe systemic illness and other diagnoses. Weeping or crusting alone does not prove bacterial infection - not all flares are infective, even with weeping and crusts.

  2. Box 2ASSESSAssess severity and triggers

    Review extent, itch, sleep, daily life, treatment use and possible contact allergy or infection. Reconsider the diagnosis if the distribution or response is atypical. Match the severity-specific source to the presentation; do not use one steroid potency for every site.

  3. Box 3SUPPORTEmollients and practical self-care

    Prescribe generous amounts of emollients and advise frequent, liberal use, even when the skin is clear. Demonstrate application and avoid known irritants/soap triggers. Address treatment concerns. Do not start exclusion diets without specialist supervision. Choose a product the person can use consistently.

  4. Box 4DRUGTreat the flare by severity and site

    Mild eczema: consider a mild topical corticosteroid (for example hydrocortisone 1%) for inflamed areas, continued for 48 hours after the flare is controlled. Moderate: a moderately potent steroid; severe: a potent steroid on inflamed body sites. For delicate areas (face, flexures) start mild (moderate) or moderate (severe) potency and aim for a maximum of 5 days. Use the lowest effective potency, an agreed duration and review.

  5. Box 5DRUGInfection is not routine antibiotics

    Continue eczema treatment (emollients and topical corticosteroids) whether antibiotics are offered or not. In people not systemically unwell, do not routinely offer topical or oral antibiotics for secondary bacterial infection. If systemically unwell, offer an oral antibiotic following the full NG190 choice-of-antibiotic recommendations, with allergy and local-policy checks.

  6. Box 6REVIEW/LIMITReview control and referral need

    Review frequent flares, heavy steroid use and maintenance regimens. Refer urgently (within 2 weeks) to dermatology if eczema is severe and has not responded to optimum topical treatment after 1 week. Routine referral for uncertain diagnosis, unsatisfactory control (for example 1-2 flares per month), facial eczema not responding to appropriate treatment, suspected contact allergic dermatitis, recurrent secondary infection, or significant social or psychological impact.

  • NICE CKS: Atopic eczema - mild, moderate and severe scenarios (revised March 2025 to October 2026)
  • NICE NG190: Secondary bacterial infection of eczema - recommendations (published March 2021; minor update January 2022)
  • NICE NG190 update information

Iron deficiency anaemia: adult work-up

Checked against the cited sources by automated comparison, November 2026. Steps labelled Direction-supported follow the cited sources' direction in this chart's own wording, not the sources' verbatim text. Not clinically validated.

  1. Box 1URGENTAssess urgency and alternative causes

    Adults 18 and over, non-pregnant; this is iron deficiency anaemia, not a complete anaemia or transfusion protocol - B12/folate, haemolysis and marrow disorders need separate work-up. Assess symptoms and stability. Profound anaemia with signs of heart failure requires referral. Do not assume all anaemia is iron deficiency; seek advice if the type is uncertain, severe symptoms occur or specialist investigations are needed.

  2. Box 2ASSESSConfirm with FBC and iron studies

    Check FBC and ferritin in suspected iron deficiency. Ferritin below 30 micrograms/L confirms deficiency; inflammation, malignancy or liver disease can raise ferritin even with deficiency present. If inflammation may be masking the result, consider inflammation markers or other iron studies and seek haematology/biochemistry advice when unclear; consider B12/folate when indicated.

  3. Box 3ASSESSFind the cause, not only the Hb

    Review bleeding, diet, medicines and malabsorption. Screen for coeliac disease with serology, test urine for blood and offer FIT for suspected colorectal-cancer triage. Men and postmenopausal women generally need gastroenterology assessment unless they have overt non-gastrointestinal bleeding. Use the full age/sex-specific pathway.

  4. Box 4DRUGTreat while arranging investigations

    Offer one tablet once daily of oral ferrous sulfate, ferrous fumarate or ferrous gluconate; one tablet on alternate days can help intolerance. Correct an identified cause. Do not defer replacement while waiting for investigations, unless colonoscopy is imminent. Parenteral iron is a separate assessed/referral decision if oral iron is contraindicated, ineffective or not tolerated.

  5. Box 5REVIEW/LIMITCheck response and replenish stores

    Recheck FBC within the first 4 weeks. A rise of about 20 g/L over 3-4 weeks is expected. Review adherence, tolerance, ongoing loss and diagnosis if response is poor. If it responds, recheck FBC at 2-4 months to confirm normalisation. Once Hb and red cell indices normalise, continue iron for 3 months, then stop and monitor FBC periodically (for example at 3, 6, 12 and 24 months).

  6. Box 6URGENTEscalate unexplained or recurrent disease

    FIT of at least 10 micrograms Hb/g faeces meets the colorectal suspected-cancer referral threshold. A lower or absent FIT must not delay referral where strong clinical concern persists. Persistent/recurrent iron deficiency anaemia, oral intolerance or inadequate response needs further assessment.

  • NICE CKS: Anaemia - iron deficiency, management (revised July 2026)
  • NICE CKS: Anaemia - iron deficiency, investigations and confirmed deficiency (revised July 2026)
  • NICE NG12 suspected cancer, colorectal recommendations 1.3.1-1.3.3 (FIT; updated April 2026)

Osteoporosis: fracture risk assessment

Checked against the cited sources by automated comparison, November 2026. Steps labelled Direction-supported follow the cited sources' direction in this chart's own wording, not the sources' verbatim text. Not clinically validated.

  1. Box 1ASSESSIdentify who needs assessment

    Adult fragility-fracture risk and treatment assessment; not acute fracture care, a FRAX calculator or a full medicine-selection protocol. Assess people aged 50 and over and women after menopause with a previous fragility fracture or current/frequent systemic glucocorticoids. Consider assessment for all men 75+, all women 65+, and people 50-74 (and postmenopausal women under 65) with risk factors such as 2+ falls in the last year, a first-degree relative's hip fracture, BMI under 18.5, smoking, alcohol over 14 units/week or other NG259 table-1 factors. Under 50: separate criteria (previous hip/vertebral fragility fracture or 2+ major osteoporotic fractures).

  2. Box 2ASSESSUse an appropriate risk tool

    Combine a full clinical risk assessment with a 10-year major-osteoporotic-fracture risk from FRAX or QFracture at ages 40-90, and QFracture at ages 30-39. Use the same tool throughout care. Seek specialist advice for people under 30 or with a suspected rare bone disease.

  3. Box 3ASSESSArrange DXA when indicated

    At age 30 and over, offer DXA after a hip/vertebral fragility fracture, a single major osteoporotic fragility fracture in the last 2 years, or 2 or more fragility fractures. Consider DXA to guide treatment decisions if the 10-year major-fracture risk is 10% or more. Do a baseline DXA when starting treatment unless not tolerated, not technically feasible, or not needed for treatment or monitoring decisions.

  4. Box 4ASSESSLook for vertebral fractures

    Consider vertebral fracture assessment during DXA in men 70+ and women 60+, or in younger people with a previous major osteoporotic fracture, unexplained back or radiating rib pain, height loss over 4 cm or kyphosis, current/frequent systemic glucocorticoids, or exceptionally low BMD for age. Use the full exceptions and imaging pathway (no VFA after recent spinal imaging, with scoliosis, or above the reliable-reading weight threshold).

  5. Box 5SUPPORTShare the treatment decision

    Make a shared decision using the risk score, BMD, fracture number/timing/sites and clinical risk factors. NG259 criteria include a previous hip/vertebral fragility fracture, very high-dose systemic glucocorticoids (15 mg+ prednisolone-equivalent daily for 3 months+), a T-score of -2.5 or less, or -1.5 or less with defined risk combinations. Do not replace this with one universal FRAX threshold. Treatment can be considered without DXA when a scan is not tolerated, feasible or needed.

  6. Box 6REVIEW/LIMITPlan reassessment if untreated

    Advise people who decline or delay treatment when and how to re-access care, and to make contact if they change their mind or circumstances change. Reassess risk (same tool) when circumstances change; otherwise at 5 years, or within 2-3 years if close to the treatment criteria. Do not routinely repeat a DXA scan within 2 years unless exceptional circumstances.

  • NICE NG259: Osteoporosis - risk assessment, recommendations (published July 2026; replaces CG146)
  • NICE NG259 update information

Vaginal discharge: assessment and routing

Checked against the cited sources by automated comparison, November 2026. Steps labelled Direction-supported follow the cited sources' direction in this chart's own wording, not the sources' verbatim text. Not clinically validated.

  1. Box 1URGENTRecognise urgent pelvic/systemic illness

    Pelvic pain, fever, deep dyspareunia or abnormal bleeding can indicate upper-tract disease. Suspected PID with pregnancy/possible ectopic, severe systemic illness, adnexal mass or an unwell person with uncertain diagnosis needs urgent hospital assessment.

  2. Box 2ASSESSTake a sensitive history

    Ask about discharge, itch/pain, urinary symptoms, bleeding, pregnancy possibility, sexual exposures, medicines and prior treatments. Assess STI risk without assuming a cause from colour or smell alone. Increased STI risk (CKS): condomless sex with new or casual partners; younger than 25; a new sexual partner or more than one partner in the last 12 months; previous STI; Black ethnicity. Consider infective and non-infective causes.

  3. Box 3ASSESSExamine and choose tests

    Explain the examination, obtain consent and offer a chaperone. Select examination, pH, vaginal swab/culture or STI tests according to symptoms, risk and recurrence. pH alone cannot distinguish BV from trichomoniasis. Consider a pregnancy test. If at high STI risk: offer testing for chlamydia, gonorrhoea, trichomoniasis, HIV and syphilis, and strongly recommend GUM referral; testing can be done in primary care when GUM cannot be attended and appropriate expertise is available (CKS).

  4. Box 4SUPPORTSTI or PID: use sexual-health care

    Strongly recommend sexual-health/GUM assessment for STI testing, treatment and partner notification when indicated. Confirmed chlamydia, gonorrhoea or trichomoniasis follows its own current pathogen-specific pathway; do not reuse a BV/candida regimen. Suspected PID not needing admission: start empirical antibiotic treatment as soon as a working diagnosis is made - ideally with STI screening first, but do not delay treatment while awaiting tests or results (CKS PID topic); if the woman is unable or unwilling to attend GUM, follow the current CKS PID topic. Cervicitis: treat for chlamydia while awaiting swab results (CKS).

  5. Box 5SUPPORTTreat the identified cause

    Use the current specific pathway for BV, candidiasis, cervicitis or non-infective disease. If no cause or concerning findings, physiological discharge may need reassurance and avoidance of irritant products, rather than repeated empirical antimicrobials.

  6. Box 6REVIEW/LIMITSafety-net and reassess

    Reassess persistent/recurrent symptoms or uncertain diagnosis and consider sexual-health referral. Worsening pain, fever, bleeding or systemic illness needs prompt review. Cancer routes (NG12): aged 55 and over with unexplained vaginal discharge presenting for the first time, or with thrombocytosis, or reporting haematuria - consider urgent direct-access ultrasound for endometrial cancer (1.5.13). Unexplained postmenopausal bleeding not attributable to HRT: suspected cancer pathway referral at 55 and over (1.5.12), consider referral under 55 (1.5.14). Cervix appearance consistent with cervical cancer (1.5.16), unexplained vulval lump, ulceration or bleeding (1.5.17), or unexplained palpable vaginal mass (1.5.18): consider suspected cancer pathway referral.

Suspected dementia: assessment and support

Checked against the cited sources by automated comparison, November 2026. Steps labelled Direction-supported follow the cited sources' direction in this chart's own wording, not the sources' verbatim text. Not clinically validated.

  1. Box 1URGENTExclude delirium and rapid progression

    Investigate reversible causes including delirium, depression, sensory impairment and medicines. If delirium versus dementia is unclear, treat delirium first. Suspected rapidly progressive dementia needs a neurological service, not routine memory-clinic delay.

  2. Box 2ASSESSHistory and daily function

    Take cognitive, behavioural and psychological history and the impact on daily life. With appropriate involvement, obtain history from someone who knows the person well. Consider physical examination and blood/urine tests for reversible causes.

  3. Box 3ASSESSTest fairly; refer if still suspected

    Use a validated brief cognitive tool and account for sensory, language and educational factors. A normal score does not exclude dementia. Refer to a specialist diagnostic service when reversible causes have been investigated and suspicion remains.

  4. Box 4DRUGDiagnosis and medicine boundaries

    Specialist assessment establishes subtype. Alzheimer medicines depend on diagnosis/severity and relevant expertise; an initial prescription may be in primary care under those conditions. Do not treat all cognitive impairment alike or stop an AChE inhibitor for severity alone.

  5. Box 5SUPPORTDistress: assess causes first

    Check pain, delirium and environmental causes. Offer psychosocial/environmental measures first. Only offer antipsychotics for people living with dementia who are at risk of harming themselves or others, or experiencing agitation, hallucinations or delusions causing severe distress (1.7.3; off-label for almost all antipsychotics; follow MHRA advice). Discuss benefits and harms with the person and family/carers, with a decision aid (1.7.5). Lewy-body / Parkinson's disease dementia: antipsychotics can worsen motor features and in some cases cause severe antipsychotic sensitivity reactions - seek specialist advice (1.7.4). Use the lowest effective dose for the shortest possible time and reassess at least every 6 weeks (1.7.6). Stop treatment if the person is not getting a clear ongoing benefit, after discussion with them and their family or carers (1.7.7).

  6. Box 6REVIEW/LIMITOngoing person and carer support

    Ensure access to memory/multidisciplinary services, ask about emerging needs, and provide tailored carer education and support. Discuss carer assessment/respite rights and future planning. Check nutrition, sensory needs and other health problems.

  • NICE NG97 recommendations (1.7.3-1.7.7)

Croup: assessment and first actions

Checked against the cited sources by automated comparison, November 2026. Steps labelled Direction-supported follow the cited sources' direction in this chart's own wording, not the sources' verbatim text. Not clinically validated.

  1. Box 1URGENTAirway danger: arrange urgent help

    Moderate/severe croup or impending respiratory failure needs hospital admission. Fatigue, cyanosis, reduced consciousness or diminishing stridor in a tiring child can be ominous. Suspected epiglottitis: do not examine the throat or manipulate the neck. While awaiting admission (CKS): give controlled supplementary oxygen to all children with symptoms of severe illness or impending respiratory failure; administer a dose of oral dexamethasone (0.15 mg/kg) - if the child is too unwell to receive medication, inhaled budesonide (2 mg nebulised as a single dose) or intramuscular dexamethasone (0.6 mg/kg as a single dose) are possible alternatives. Do not delay urgent transfer to give a medicine.

  2. Box 2ASSESSRecognise the pattern; keep calm

    Typical barking cough and hoarse voice, often worse at night, follow viral symptoms. Keep the child comfortable with the carer; avoid agitation and forced repositioning. Consider foreign body and other diagnoses when atypical.

  3. Box 3ASSESSAssess severity and referral threshold

    Mild: no stridor/recession at rest. Stridor with recession at rest is at least moderate. Consider admission with poor intake/dehydration, high fever/toxic appearance, significant comorbidity, young age or limited access/support at home.

  4. Box 4DRUGMild croup: single oral steroid

    If hospital admission is not indicated (mild illness), give a single oral dexamethasone dose of 0.15 mg/kg (150 micrograms/kg) using the current weight, to be taken immediately (CKS croup management, last revised May 2022). Confirm the oral formulation, strength and volume against the current BNFC before prescribing. The card is for mild croup, not a rescue/nebulised-adrenaline or severe-croup repeat-dose protocol.

  5. Box 5SUPPORTSupport and observe

    Encourage fluids and continued breastfeeding; use appropriate fever/pain treatment. Check the child regularly, including overnight. Expected symptoms often settle within 48 hours, but deterioration needs reassessment rather than waiting out that period.

  6. Box 6REVIEW/LIMITGive explicit emergency advice

    Seek hospital care for persistent stridor/recession or agitation. Call 999 for blue/grey colour, unusual sleepiness/unresponsiveness, severe breathing difficulty, inability to speak, drooling or trouble swallowing. Agree follow-up based on clinical judgement.

Bronchiectasis: adult community review

Checked against the cited sources by automated comparison, November 2026. Steps labelled Direction-supported follow the cited sources' direction in this chart's own wording, not the sources' verbatim text. Not clinically validated.

  1. Box 1URGENTEscalate serious deterioration

    Arrange hospital admission for people who demonstrably need intravenous antibiotics - for example symptoms or signs suggesting cardiorespiratory failure or sepsis: cyanosis; confusion; marked breathlessness, rapid respiration or laboured breathing; peripheral oedema; temperature 38C or higher (CKS infective-exacerbation page). Seek specialist advice for repeated treatment failure, resistant bacteria or inability to take oral treatment (NG117 1.1.8).

  2. Box 2ASSESSSuspect and confirm properly

    Assess chronic productive cough/recurrent infection. Check sputum culture, CXR, spirometry, oxygen saturation and FBC as indicated. Refer suspected bronchiectasis to respiratory care for confirmation, usually thin-section CT, and investigation of the underlying cause.

  3. Box 3SUPPORTAirway clearance is core care

    Ensure a respiratory physiotherapist teaches an individual airway-clearance technique and checks it. Optimise activity, smoking cessation, vaccination and pulmonary rehabilitation when breathlessness limits daily life. Do not routinely add ICS without another indication.

  4. Box 4DRUGExacerbation: culture and antibiotics

    Send sputum before antibiotics if possible, without delaying needed treatment. Use previous susceptibility, local policy and severity to choose an oral regimen, usually 7–14 days; review when results return. Do not reuse the 5-day COPD course. Direction-supported limb: the do-not-delay-antibiotics point is supported in direction by NG117 and BTS guidance; wording is this chart's summary.

  5. Box 5DRUGFrequent exacerbations: specialist plan

    Refer >=3 exacerbations/year, fewer with substantial morbidity, chronic Pseudomonas/MRSA/NTM or declining lung function. Long-term antibiotics need specialist selection, not automatic macrolide repeats. Before a long-term oral macrolide (CKS): optimise other therapies first (airway clearance, pulmonary rehabilitation); ECG to assess QTc interval; baseline liver function tests; counsel on adverse effects (gastrointestinal upset, hearing and balance disturbance, cardiac effects, microbiological resistance); sputum microbiology including investigation for non-tuberculous mycobacteria (NTM) - avoid macrolide monotherapy if NTM is identified, and avoid macrolides for 2 weeks before NTM testing; determine the baseline exacerbation rate. After starting: LFTs after 1 month then every 6 months; ECG 1 month after starting - stop macrolide treatment if new QTc prolongation.

  6. Box 6REVIEW/LIMITAt least annual review

    Review symptoms, exacerbations, sputum culture, spirometry, SpO2, BMI, comorbidities and airway-clearance adherence. Check vaccination and smoking support. A patient self-management plan should be considered (BTS good practice point; a BTS example plan is available). High-risk patients remain with specialist care.
