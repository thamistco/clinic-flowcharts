    1

    Identify serious illness

    URGENT

    +

    Adults aged 18 and over; excludes children and pregnancy; not an airway-emergency protocol. Refer to hospital for acute sore throat with severe systemic infection or severe suppurative complications such as quinsy, parapharyngeal or retropharyngeal abscess, or Lemierre syndrome. Offer an immediate antibiotic with advice to people who are systemically very unwell, have symptoms and signs of a more serious illness or condition, or are at high risk of complications.

    2

    Assess likelihood of benefit

    ASSESS

    +

    Use FeverPAIN or Centor criteria. Acute sore throat is self-limiting and often viral; symptoms can last around one week, and most people get better within this time without antibiotics, regardless of cause.

    3

    Low score

    SUPPORT

    +

    no antibiotic: FeverPAIN 0–1 or Centor 0–2: do not offer an antibiotic. Advise on the usual course (around one week), that an antibiotic is not needed, and to seek medical help if symptoms worsen rapidly or significantly, do not start to improve after one week, or the person becomes systemically very unwell.

    4

    Intermediate

    DRUG

    +

    discuss options: FeverPAIN 2–3: consider no antibiotic prescription with advice, or a back-up prescription, taking account of the small average benefit (about 16 hours shorter symptoms), most people feeling better after a week either way, the unlikely complications, and possible adverse effects (particularly diarrhoea and nausea).

    5

    Higher score

    DRUG

    +

    immediate or back-up: FeverPAIN 4–5 or Centor 3–4: consider an immediate antibiotic, or a back-up prescription with safety-netting. First choice for adults: phenoxymethylpenicillin 500 mg four times a day or 1,000 mg twice daily for 5–10 days (5 days may be enough for symptomatic cure; 10 days may improve microbiological cure). Penicillin allergy or intolerance, not pregnant (NG84 Table 1): clarithromycin 250 mg to 500 mg twice a day for 5 days. Penicillin allergy in pregnancy: erythromycin 250 mg to 500 mg four times a day or 500 mg to 1,000 mg twice a day for 5 days. Check interactions and organ function in the live BNF.

    6

    Relieve symptoms; safety-net

    REVIEW/LIMITSAFETY-NET

    +

    Consider paracetamol for pain or fever, or ibuprofen if preferred and suitable; advise adequate fluids. Back-up prescription: use if symptoms do not start to improve within 3–5 days or worsen rapidly or significantly at any time. Reassess if symptoms worsen rapidly or significantly, considering alternative diagnoses such as scarlet fever or glandular fever.

    1

    Exclude a more serious infection

    URGENT

    +

    Hospital referral for signs of serious illness such as sepsis. Fever or suspected pyelonephritis/complicated UTI needs its own pathway, not this lower-UTI regimen.

    2

    Immediate or back-up treatment

    ASSESS

    +

    Consider immediate or back-up antibiotic based on severity, complication risk, previous culture/antibiotics and preference.

    3

    Choose using current factors

    DRUG

    +

    Use local resistance and previous cultures. First choices (NG109 Table 1, non-pregnant women 16 and over): nitrofurantoin (if eGFR 45 ml/minute or more) 100 mg modified-release twice a day (or, if unavailable, 50 mg four times a day) for 3 days; or trimethoprim (if low risk of resistance) 200 mg twice a day for 3 days. Follow the MHRA advice on monitoring for pulmonary and hepatic adverse reactions to nitrofurantoin. Open the NICE adult table for alternatives. NG109 Table 1 also allows nitrofurantoin with caution at eGFR 30–44 mL/min only for suspected or proven multidrug-resistant organisms when potential benefit outweighs risk. The current UK SmPC (emc product 15332, sections) shows an internal tension rather than a simple licence-stricter-than-NICE position: section 4.3 contraindicates nitrofurantoin at eGFR below 45 mL/min, while the same SmPC's section 4.4 states it may be used with caution as short-course therapy only for the treatment of uncomplicated lower urinary tract infection in individual cases with an eGFR between 30–44 ml/min to treat resistant pathogens, when the benefits are expected to outweigh the risks – mirroring the NG109 footnote. The prescriber reconciles the two SmPC sections for the individual case; check the dispensed product's current SmPC and do not infer suitability from this summary.

    4

    Give back-up instructions

    SUPPORT

    +

    If supplied: use if symptoms have not started improving within 48 hours, or worsen at any time. Explain adverse effects and when to seek help.

    5

    Support symptom control

    SUPPORT

    +

    Paracetamol for pain, or suitable ibuprofen; enough fluids to avoid dehydration. No evidence found for cranberry products or urine-alkalinising agents to treat the infection.

    6

    Reassess if not improving

    REVIEW/LIMIT

    +

    Reassess if worsening or no improvement within 48 hours of antibiotics. Culture urine if not done; review results/treatment and consider alternative diagnoses or pyelonephritis.

    1

    Consider alternative diagnoses

    URGENT

    +

    Exclude specific causes such as cancer, infection, trauma and inflammatory disease, particularly with new or changed symptoms; use the relevant pathway if suspected.

    2

    Assess and stratify

    ASSESS

    +

    Consider risk stratification at first contact. Do not routinely image in non-specialist settings; specialist imaging only if likely to change management.

    3

    Activity and exercise

    SUPPORT

    +

    Tailor self-management advice, encourage normal activities and consider a suitable group exercise programme. Facilitate return to work/daily activities.

    4

    Low back pain: medicines

    DRUG

    +

    Consider oral NSAIDs after GI, liver and cardio-renal risk assessment; gastroprotection/monitoring as appropriate, lowest effective dose and shortest period. Do not offer paracetamol alone. Consider weak opioids (with or without paracetamol) for managing acute low back pain only if an NSAID is contraindicated, not tolerated or has been ineffective; do not routinely offer opioids for acute low back pain, and do not offer opioids for chronic low back pain. Do not offer SSRIs, SNRIs or tricyclic antidepressants, and do not offer gabapentinoids or other antiepileptics, for managing low back pain.

    5

    Sciatica: avoid harmful medicines

    DRUG

    +

    Do not offer gabapentinoids, other antiepileptics, oral corticosteroids or benzodiazepines. No opioids for chronic sciatica; review existing use/withdrawal safely. NSAID benefit limited; review harms.

    6

    Persistent sciatica

    REVIEW/LIMIT

    +

    Consider spinal decompression when non-surgical care has not improved pain/function and imaging matches symptoms. Full specialist criteria apply.

    •

    Ask about cauda equina features

    ASSESS

    +

    Bladder dysfunction (difficulty starting micturition, impaired sensation of urinary flow, retention, overflow incontinence); loss of rectal-fullness sensation or faecal incontinence; saddle (perianal, perineal or genital) sensory change; sexual dysfunction; severe or progressive bilateral leg weakness; gait disturbance; and bilateral radicular pain or unilateral pain progressing to bilateral (a warning that cauda equina may occur, not diagnostic alone).

    •

    Emergency referral

    URGENTREFER

    +

    Suspected cauda equina syndrome, suspected deteriorating spinal cord pathology (for example gait disturbance or multilevel weakness), or suspected spinal infection: emergency referral – CKS Sciatica (revised August 2026) routes these usually via an Emergency Department with access to emergency MRI scanning, not routine MSK triage. Source exactness flag: CKS also carries 'suspicion of tumour or infection' in its urgent-referral list, so suspected spinal infection appears in both the emergency and the urgent source lists; the split is presented unresolved. Local cauda equina pathways vary – confirm your local route.

    •

    Urgent referral

    URGENTREFER

    +

    Urgent (2-week) referral per CKS Sciatica (revised August 2026): suspected spinal tumour or infection; major motor loss (less than 3/5 on MRC grading); or bilateral radicular progression without CES features – urgent referral to a spinal surgery service, or urgent MRI. For suspected infection see also the emergency box: CKS lists it in both routes and the split is presented unresolved.

    •

    History of cancer

    URGENT

    +

    History of cancer with new-onset spinal pain is itself a CKS red flag: assess for possible bone metastases and refer urgently (within 2 weeks – urgent referral to a spinal surgery service or urgent MRI per CKS, or the NG12 suspected-cancer route where that fits the picture); this is a separate referral question from the cauda equina routes.

    1

    Clinical diagnosis

    ASSESS

    +

    Age 45+, activity-related joint pain and no morning stiffness or stiffness ≤30 minutes: diagnose clinically without imaging. Do not routinely image unless atypical features/alternative diagnosis.

    2

    Exercise is core treatment

    SUPPORT

    +

    Offer tailored strengthening/aerobic exercise; consider supervised sessions. Explain that initial discomfort can occur and regular exercise benefits pain/function.

    3

    Weight management

    SUPPORT

    +

    For overweight/obesity, explain weight-loss benefits and support the chosen goal; any loss may help, and 10% is likely to benefit more than 5%.

    4

    Medicines support activity

    DRUG

    +

    Use medicines alongside non-drug care at the lowest effective dose for the shortest time. Offer topical NSAID for knee OA; consider for other joints. If topical treatment ineffective/unsuitable, consider oral NSAID after risk review and offer gastroprotection.

    5

    Avoid low-value treatments

    DRUG

    +

    Do not routinely offer paracetamol/weak opioids except under limited source criteria. No strong opioids or glucosamine. Corticosteroid injections may give short-term relief if appropriate.

    6

    Review and joint replacement

    REVIEW/LIMIT

    +

    Agree follow-up; review if treatment is not working. Consider joint-replacement referral if joint symptoms substantially impact quality of life and non-surgical care is ineffective or unsuitable. Do not exclude solely because of age, sex or gender, smoking, comorbidities, overweight or obesity/BMI (NG226 1.6.1 and 1.6.3). Local 2026 checklist (East Sussex MSK Hip and Knee OA Checklist 2026): X-ray within the last 18 months (referrals without it may be returned), BMI <35 ideally or reasonable documented attempts to reduce BMI, at least 6 months conservative treatment, a decision aid and shared-decision conversation, and smoking-cessation advice if appropriate. These local items are commissioning/access gates, not NICE referral criteria – a commissioning barrier must not delay an urgent clinical pathway; confirm the current checklist version and requirements on the day of referral. (Area-specific example: East Sussex – local routes and services vary.)

    Colchicine dose card – product-specific

    DRUG

    +

BNF / emc 6415 regimen: 500 micrograms 2–4 times daily until symptoms relieved; maximum 6 mg per course; do not repeat a course within 3 days (72 hours).     SUPPORT

    +

    DRUG

    +

    REVIEW/LIMIT

If not confirmed, BDR with spirometry: diagnose if FEV1 increases 12% or more AND 200 ml or more from pre-bronchodilator, or 10% or more of predicted normal FEV1 (1.2.2). If spirometry is unavailable or delayed: measure PEF TWICE DAILY FOR 2 WEEKS – diagnose if PEF variability (amplitude percentage mean) is 20% or more (1.2.3). If still unconfirmed but clinically suspected: refer for consideration of a bronchial challenge test; diagnose if bronchial hyper-responsiveness is present (1.2.4).

    4

    Diagnosis: children 5–16 – fourth-step decision rules

    ASSESS

    +

    FeNO first: diagnose if 35 ppb or more (1.2.5). Otherwise BDR with spirometry: diagnose if FEV1 rises 12% or more from baseline, or 10% or more of predicted normal FEV1 (1.2.6). If spirometry delayed: PEF twice daily for 2 weeks, diagnose if variability 20% or more (1.2.7). If still unconfirmed but suspected: EITHER skin-prick testing to house dust mite OR total IgE plus blood eosinophil count (1.2.8) – EXCLUDE asthma if no house-dust-mite sensitisation on skin-prick or total serum IgE not raised; DIAGNOSE if sensitisation or raised total IgE AND eosinophil count more than 0.5 x 10^9 per litre. If still in doubt: refer to a paediatric specialist for a second opinion, including consideration of a bronchial challenge test (1.2.9).

    5

    Diagnosis: under 5

    ASSESS

    +

    Use a monitored treatment trial and regular review because objective testing is often impractical. Try objective tests at age 5; retry every 6–12 months if unable. Refer any preschool child with 1 hospital admission or ≥2 emergency-department admissions for wheeze in 12 months.

    6

    Before stepping up at any age

    SUPPORT

    +

    Check diagnosis, adherence, technique, triggers, smoking/vaping, environment and comorbidities. Do not prescribe SABA without an ICS prescription at any age (NG245; MHRA Drug Safety Update, 24 April 2025: excessive SABA use may mask progression of the underlying disease and increase the risk of severe, life-threatening exacerbations; review and adjust treatment in anyone using as-needed SABA more than twice weekly; urgently review anyone whose SABA prescription requests are rising or who has not collected their anti-inflammatory maintenance treatment; AIR and MART are the recommended alternatives for people over 12 years with poorly controlled asthma). Match device to ability and check technique. After starting/changing medicines, review in 8–12 weeks.

    7

    Treatment: 12+ start and early steps

    DRUG

    +

    New diagnosis: low-dose ICS/formoterol as needed (AIR) (NG245 1.7.1). If highly symptomatic (for example regular nocturnal waking) or presenting with a severe exacerbation, start low-dose MART alongside acute treatment (1.7.2). If not controlled on AIR (CKS example: needing AIR therapy 3 times a week or more), low-dose MART (1.7.3); if still uncontrolled, moderate-dose MART (1.7.4). LICENSING: NG245 1.7.1 states that in November 2025 only certain budesonide/formoterol inhalers were licensed for as-needed AIR therapy in mild asthma, so any other ICS/formoterol inhaler is off-label for AIR (the evidence is for a dry powder inhaler). The BNF lists mild asthma reliever therapy for DuoResp Spiromax 160/4.5, Fobumix 160/4.5 Easyhaler, Symbicort 200/6 Turbohaler and WockAIR 160/4.5 only; the SmPCs on the emc for Symbicort Turbohaler 200/6 (updated 22 March 2023), DuoResp Spiromax 160/4.5 (5 April 2024), Fobumix Easyhaler 160/4.5 (15 August 2025) and WockAIR 160/4.5 (4 April 2024) each say in section 4.1 that the product is also indicated as reliever therapy for adults and adolescents aged 12 and over with mild asthma. The BNF entry for beclometasone/formoterol (Bibecfo, Fostair, Fostair NEXThaler, Luforbec, Proxor, Vivaire) lists maintenance therapy and, for the 100/6 strengths only, MART, in adults only, with no as-needed-only reliever indication and no child dose; the SmPCs on the emc for Fostair 100/6 pMDI (updated 11 March 2026), Luforbec 100/6 (8 July 2024), Bibecfo/Vivaire 100/6 (17 August 2026), Proxor 100/6 (18 February 2025) and Fostair NEXThaler 100/6 (27 August 2020) all agree: regular treatment of asthma, as maintenance therapy or maintenance and reliever therapy, in adults aged 18 and over, with safety and efficacy under 18 not established (Fostair NEXThaler also says not to use at ages 5 to 11 because of safety concerns). None has a reliever-only indication. So beclometasone/formoterol is off-label for as-needed-only AIR use. ICS/LABA inhalers with a non-formoterol LABA (such as salmeterol) are not licensed for AIR or MART (CKS asthma, last revised March 2026). Position taken in this chart: Fostair and Luforbec (and the other beclometasone/formoterol inhalers) are for maintenance therapy and MART in adults only and are not licensed for as-needed-only use, as stated in the SmPCs and the BNF. The CKS prescribing page lists Fostair and Luforbec among inhalers licensed for AIR and MART, which the SmPCs do not support.

    8

    Treatment: 12+ later steps

    DRUG

    +

    On moderate-dose MART with good adherence, check FeNO/eosinophils: if raised, refer. If neither raised, consider an 8–12-week LTRA or LAMA trial, then assess the other as directed. Uncontrolled despite both trials: specialist. People already on other regimens (NG245 1.7.7-1.7.10): change SABA-only treatment to low-dose ICS/formoterol used as needed (AIR) (1.7.7); consider low-dose MART if asthma is not controlled on regular low-dose ICS, or low-dose ICS/LABA, with or without an LTRA, plus SABA as needed (1.7.8); consider moderate-dose MART if not controlled on the moderate-dose equivalents, with or without an LTRA or LAMA (1.7.9); when moving to MART, decide whether to continue supplementary therapy according to the benefit it gave when first introduced (1.7.10). Not controlled on a high-dose ICS: refer to a specialist in asthma care (1.7.11). CKS adds that people with an existing diagnosis who are stable on their current therapy do not have to switch.

    9

    Treatment: 5–11 start and MART

    DRUG

    +

    Start twice-daily paediatric low-dose ICS plus SABA as needed. If uncontrolled and able to manage MART, consider paediatric low-dose MART, then moderate-dose MART. Check the exact product and age: NG245 notes one budesonide/formoterol 100/6 DPI licensed for MART at ages 6–11, none at age 5; other use is off-label (NG245 1.8.2, minor change November 2025; the BNF lists Symbicort 100/6 Turbohaler for MART at ages 6–11). The Symbicort Turbohaler 100/6 SmPC (emc, updated 18 September 2025), section 4.2 B, gives a maintenance and reliever dose for children aged 6 to 11 (maintenance 1 inhalation daily, or 1 twice daily for some, plus 1 additional inhalation as needed, no more than 4 on any single occasion), whereas the Symbicort Turbohaler 200/6 SmPC (updated 22 March 2023) says MART is not recommended under 12, as does the Fobumix Easyhaler 80/4.5 SmPC (emc 8707) even though that product is licensed for regular treatment from age 6. Both statements, with sources: CKS asthma (basis for recommendation, last revised March 2026) says MART is currently not licensed in the UK below the age of 12 years; the Symbicort Turbohaler 100/6 SmPC (emc, updated 18 September 2025) gives a MART dose at ages 6 to 11, as do NG245 1.8.2 and the BNF. Check the product SmPC before prescribing.

    10

    Treatment: 5–11 non-MART route

    DRUG

    +

    If unable to manage MART, consider an 8–12-week LTRA trial with low-dose ICS plus SABA, stopping if ineffective. If uncontrolled, offer low-dose ICS/LABA plus SABA, then moderate-dose. Refer if uncontrolled on moderate-dose MART or ICS/LABA.

    11

    Treatment: under 5 trial and review

    DRUG

    +

    Consider 8–12 weeks of twice-daily paediatric low-dose ICS plus SABA for interval symptoms or severe episodes. If no response, check technique/adherence, environment and alternative diagnoses, then refer. If resolved, consider stopping and review again after 3 months.

    12

    Treatment: under 5 persistent symptoms

    DRUG

    +

    If recurrent, restart regular ICS and titrate low to moderate dose as indicated. If uncontrolled on moderate dose, consider an 8–12-week LTRA trial; stop if ineffective or not tolerated and refer. Explain montelukast neuropsychiatric warnings.

    13

    Action plan: risk stratification and ICS increase

    REVIEW/LIMIT

    +

    Actively identify people at risk of poor outcomes and tailor care (NG245 1.15.1): non-adherence; SABA over-use (more than 2 inhalers per year); needing 2 or more courses of oral corticosteroids per year; 2 or more emergency-department visits or any hospital admission for asthma. For adults (17 and over) using an ICS in a single inhaler, within a self-management programme: offer an increased ICS dose for 7 days when control deteriorates, with the action plan stating how and when, and what to do if symptoms do not improve – consider quadrupling the regular ICS dose; do not exceed the maximum licensed daily dose (1.14.4). Review cadence (NG245 1.16.1): in primary care, people with asthma should be reviewed at least annually and after any exacerbation by a healthcare professional with appropriate training in asthma management; the review should incorporate a written personalised action plan. Step-down (1.10.1/1.10.3, close paraphrase): at annual review, discuss with the person (or their family or carer, if appropriate) the potential risks and benefits of decreasing maintenance therapy when asthma has been well controlled on current therapy, and agree how the effects of decreasing will be monitored and reviewed, including self-monitoring and follow-up with a healthcare professional.

    •

    Occupational asthma and pregnancy

    SUPPORT

    +

    OCCUPATIONAL (NG245 1.4.1-1.4.2): in adult-onset asthma, poorly controlled established asthma, or reappearance of childhood asthma, ask – Are symptoms the same, better or worse on days away from work? Are symptoms the same, better or worse when on holiday (time away from work, longer than usual breaks, at weekends or between shifts)? Record all answers for later review. Refer suspected occupational asthma to an occupational asthma specialist. PREGNANCY (1.12.1-1.12.3, close paraphrase): asthma review during early pregnancy and in the postpartum period; emphasise the importance and safety of maintaining good control and of continuing asthma medicines to avoid problems for mother and baby; advise using short- and long-acting beta2 agonists, inhaled corticosteroids and oral theophyllines as normal during pregnancy; advise pregnant smokers on the dangers and give stop-smoking support.

    DRUG

    Contraception, pregnancy and breastfeeding before GLP-1 or tirzepatide

    DRUG

    +

    Before any GLP-1 receptor agonist or tirzepatide (recommended as initial therapy in this section), cover contraception and pregnancy explicitly – NG28's own initial-medicines preamble directs prescribers to the SmPC for pregnancy/breastfeeding precautions, and the chart now carries the MHRA position (GOV.UK GLP-1 guidance): use effective contraception during GLP-1 treatment and for a wash-out before trying to conceive – semaglutide (Wegovy, Ozempic, Rybelsus) at least 2 months; tirzepatide (Mounjaro) at least 1 month; liraglutide 0 months (stop just before trying).     Sick-day rules: pause list, DKA triggers, urgent help

    URGENT

    +

    Completes the sick-day pathway (TREND 2025 4th edition HCP guidance). PAUSE during dehydrating illness: the SGLT2 inhibitor if acutely ill, unable to eat, nauseated or vomiting with abdominal discomfort, dehydrated, or diagnosis uncertain (ABCD recommendation); metformin temporarily stopped in dehydration (severe diarrhoea or vomiting, fever, reduced fluid intake) – contact a healthcare professional; diuretics, ACE inhibitors/ARBs and NSAIDs temporarily stopped in dehydration (kidney-function risk). EUGLYCAEMIC DKA: on an SGLT2 inhibitor, DKA can occur with glucose 14 mmol/L or less – teach the signs (nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, excessive thirst, frequent urination, difficulty breathing, confusion, unusual fatigue or sleepiness) and the need for urgent attention even when glucose is normal or only slightly raised. DKA diagnosis thresholds: blood ketones 3 mmol/L or more (or more than 2+ on urine strips), glucose above 11 mmol/L, bicarbonate below 15 or venous pH below 7.3. GLP-1 receptor agonists and tirzepatide – acute pancreatitis (per-product SmPC warnings, both): semaglutide – Ozempic SmPC section 4.4 (emc product 9750); tirzepatide – Mounjaro SmPC section 4.4 (emc product 15484). Both state: patients should be informed of the symptoms of acute pancreatitis, including persistent, severe abdominal pain, and advised to seek immediate medical attention if they occur; if pancreatitis is suspected, the medicine should be discontinued; if the diagnosis of pancreatitis is confirmed, it should not be restarted. Semaglutide – NAION (MHRA Drug Safety Update, 5 February 2026): very rarely associated with non-arteritic anterior ischaemic optic neuropathy; advise people to attend eye casualty or A&E urgently with sudden loss of vision (including partial) or rapidly worsening eyesight, refer urgently to ophthalmology, and discontinue semaglutide if NAION is confirmed. Privately prescribed GLP-1 medicines may not appear in the record – ask (MHRA, 29 January and 5 February 2026). Fournier's gangrene on SGLT2i: pain, tenderness, erythema or swelling in the genital or perineal area with fever or malaise – discontinue and seek urgent care. SEEK URGENT MEDICAL HELP if: pregnant; SGLT2i plus DKA signs even with normal glucose; persistent vomiting with inability to keep fluids down; blood ketones 1.6 mmol/L or higher and unable to self-manage; unable to keep glucose above 3.5 mmol/L; or not improving despite this advice. Revise sick-day rules at every annual review and give written information (trenddiabetes.online).

    Confirm diagnosis: baseline work-up and severity grading

    ASSESS

    +

    Suspect in over-35s with risk factors and breathlessness, chronic cough/sputum, winter bronchitis or wheeze. Also ask about (NG115 1.1.2, close paraphrase): weight loss, reduced exercise tolerance, waking at night with breathlessness, ankle swelling, fatigue, occupational hazards, chest pain and haemoptysis – the last two are uncommon in COPD and raise the possibility of alternative diagnoses. Confirm with post-bronchodilator spirometry (FEV1/FVC below 0.7); use the MRC dyspnoea scale to grade breathlessness (1.1.3). SOURCE CONFLICT, both shown: NICE NG115 (1.1.5 to 1.1.7 and the severity table, last updated 26 July 2019) uses a post-bronchodilator FEV1/FVC ratio below 0.7, as the severity grading below does; the All Wales note records that GOLD uses the same fixed ratio. The published All Wales COPD guideline (Respiratory Health Implementation Group, updated October 2025, review date September 2026; supporting note 3 on page 5 and Step 2 on page 3 of the PDF) advises the lower limit of normal (LLN) for the post-bronchodilator FEV1/FVC ratio instead of the fixed 0.7 ratio. SEVERITY GRADING (NG115 severity table): post-bronchodilator FEV1/FVC below 0.7 with FEV1 80% predicted or more = mild, 50–79% = moderate, 30–49% = severe, below 30% = very severe. Offer respiratory review and spirometry when imaging shows incidental emphysema or chronic airways disease.

    SUPPORT

    +

    DRUG

    +

    4

    Triple therapy: the clinical-review gate comes first

    DRUG

    +

    GATE BEFORE TRIPLE (NG115 1.2.13): before starting LAMA+LABA+ICS, conduct a clinical review to ensure that non-pharmacological COPD management is optimised and tobacco-dependence treatment has been used or offered if the person smokes; that acute episodes of worsening symptoms are caused by COPD exacerbations and not by another physical or mental health condition; and that day-to-day symptoms adversely impacting quality of life are caused by COPD and not another condition.     REVIEW/LIMIT

    +

    REVIEW/LIMIT

    +

    Eosinophil-guided add-ons (specialist): eligibility split by product

    REVIEW/LIMIT

    +

    SUPPORT

    +

    DRUG

    +

    DRUG

    +

    DRUG

    +

    +

    DRUG

    +

    DRUG

    +

    5

    Sequence and monitoring

    REVIEW/LIMIT

    +

    Individualise order/doses for status, frailty and preference. (Note, not NG106 wording: 1.7.5 does not say to stop the medicine automatically – do not automatically discontinue on these results alone.) Beta-blocker safety: assess rhythm, rate and conduction with a 12-lead ECG before prescribing (1.7.10); do not offer with second- or third-degree heart block without a pacemaker or with bradycardia (HR <50 bpm) (1.7.11); do not withhold solely for age, peripheral vascular disease, erectile dysfunction, diabetes, interstitial pulmonary disease or COPD (1.7.9); measure BP before and after each dose increment (1.7.7) and assess heart rate and clinical status after each increment (1.7.12); consider repeat ECG after each increment in people with symptoms and bradycardia (1.7.13).

    Iron status in HFrEF

    REVIEW/LIMIT

    +

Consider iron sucrose, ferric carboxymaltose or ferric derisomaltose if haemoglobin is below 150 g/L and iron deficiency is defined as TSAT below 20% or ferritin below 100 ng/mL (1.4.6). If iron-deficiency anaemia is found, do not assume it is caused by heart failure; think about investigating alternative causes (1.4.7).

    1

    Identify / confirm abnormalities

    ASSESS

    +

    At-risk adults: eGFR + ACR. New eGFR <60: repeat within 2 weeks. ACR 3 to <70 mg/mmol: early-morning confirmation; >=70 no repeat required. A single result does not establish chronicity.

    2

    Agree monitoring / risk

    SUPPORT

    +

    Agree eGFR/ACR monitoring for risk/preferences; CKD not always progressive. Classify by combined GFR (G1-G5) and ACR (A1-A3) categories (1.2.1; Table 1 risk grid). Minimum eGFR checks per year (1.3.4, Table 2): G1-G2: 0–1 (A1), 1 (A2), 1 or more (A3); G3a: 1, 1, 2; G3b: 1–2, 2, 2 or more; G4: 2, 2, 3; G5: 4 (A1), 4 or more (A2), 4 or more (A3). Use KFRE where indicated. Table 1 risk grid: G1/G2 A1 = low (no CKD without other kidney-damage markers); G3a A1 or G1/G2 A2 = moderate; G1/G2 A3, G3a A2 or G3b A1 = high; G3a A3, G3b A2-A3, G4 and G5 = very high.

    3

    Know referral criteria

    KEYREFER

    +

    Refer if 5-year KFRE >5%; ACR >=70 unless diabetes explains it and is appropriately treated; ACR >30 with haematuria; sustained eGFR fall >=25% with category change within 12 months, or >=15 mL/min/1.73 m2 per year; BP above target despite >=4 antihypertensives at therapeutic doses; rare/genetic CKD or suspected renal artery stenosis. Take wishes/comorbidities into account.

    4

    CKD BP target

    SUPPORT

    +

    ACR <70: clinic systolic <140 (120–139), diastolic <90. ACR >=70: systolic <130 (120–129), diastolic <80 mmHg.

    5

    Renal-protective treatment

    DRUG

    +

    Hypertension + ACR >30: offer ACE inhibitor/ARB at highest tolerated licensed dose. SGLT2 inhibitor as add-on to optimised standard care (highest tolerated licensed ACE/ARB unless contraindicated) if eGFR 20 to <45 mL/min/1.73 m2, or 45–90 with either ACR >=22.6 mg/mmol or type 2 diabetes (1.6.9; dapagliflozin TA1075, empagliflozin TA942). Finerenone as add-on to optimised standard care (highest tolerated licensed ACE/ARB and an SGLT2 inhibitor unless unsuitable) for stages 3–4 CKD with ACR >=3 mg/mmol associated with type 2 diabetes and eGFR >=25 (1.6.10; TA877).

    6

    Statin for CVD prevention

    DRUG

    +

    Offer atorvastatin 20 mg for primary or secondary prevention of CVD. No risk-scoring tool: eGFR <60 and/or albuminuria already marks high risk. If lipid target not met and eGFR >=30: increase the atorvastatin dose; eGFR <30: agree higher doses with a renal specialist. Renal replacement therapy is outside NG238 scope.

    7

    Potassium / renal monitoring

    REVIEW/LIMIT

    +

    Before renin-angiotensin antagonists check potassium/eGFR; repeat 1–2 weeks after start/increase. Do not routinely start if K >5.0 mmol/L. After starting or increasing: do not modify the dose if eGFR decrease from baseline is <25% or creatinine increase is <30%; repeat the test in 1–2 weeks (1.6.19, 1.6.20). If eGFR change >=25% or creatinine change >=30%: investigate other causes (volume depletion, concurrent medicines such as NSAIDs); if no other cause found, stop or reduce to a previously tolerated lower dose and add an alternative antihypertensive if needed (1.6.21). Stop if serum potassium reaches 6.0 mmol/L or more and other medicines known to promote hyperkalaemia have been discontinued (1.6.18).

    2

    Traffic-light thresholds the assessment depends on

    ASSESS

    +

    Record temperature, heart rate, respiratory rate and capillary refill time as part of routine assessment (1.2.8). THRESHOLDS (NG143): tachycardia by APLS criteria (Table 1) – more than 160/min under 12 months, more than 150/min at 12–24 months, more than 140/min at 2–5 years. RED signs include: respiratory rate over 60 breaths per minute, grunting, moderate or severe chest indrawing, reduced skin turgor, bulging fontanelle, non-blanching rash, neck stiffness, status epilepticus, focal neurological signs. AMBER includes oxygen saturation of 95% or less when breathing air, capillary refill 3 seconds or longer, tachycardia per Table 1, crackles on auscultation. Use ALL red/amber/green features; account for the individual child; temperature alone is insufficient over 6 months.

    3

    Febrile infants under 3 months: what happens next

    ASSESS

    +

Age 3–6 months + 39C or higher: at least intermediate-risk (1.2.13). Under specialist care (1.5.2-1.5.6, close paraphrase): observe and record temperature, heart rate, respiratory rate; investigate with FBC, blood culture, CRP, urine testing for UTI (CXR only if respiratory signs; stool culture if diarrhoea); LUMBAR PUNCTURE unless contraindicated for infants under 1 month, all unwell infants 1–3 months, and infants 1–3 months with WBC below 5 or above 15 x10^9/L – without delay and whenever possible before antibiotics; PARENTERAL ANTIBIOTICS for all infants under 1 month with fever, all unwell infants 1–3 months, and infants 1–3 months with those WBC extremes. This chart is an under-5 overview, not the full sepsis, meningitis or neonatal-fever protocol; apply the complete current guidance for those presentations. The 1–3 month group is squarely in this chart's population. Fever lasting 5 days or longer: assess for Kawasaki disease (NG143).

    URGENT

    +

    SUPPORT

    +

    Review and referral intervals (CKS):

    REVIEW/LIMITREFER

    +

    Explain that common shoulder problems tend to be self-limiting, but the rehabilitation period can be at least 6 months. Consider referral to a specialised musculoskeletal clinic (for example for physiotherapy or corticosteroid injection) – but if orthopaedic referral is indicated, do not delay it. Urgent features bypass these intervals.
