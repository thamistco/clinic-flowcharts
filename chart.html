Acute sore throat

Checked against the cited sources by automated comparison, November 2026. Steps labelled Direction-supported follow the cited sources' direction in this chart's own wording, not the sources' verbatim text. Not clinically validated.

  1. Box 1URGENTIdentify serious illness

    Adults aged 18 and over; excludes children and pregnancy; not an airway-emergency protocol. Refer to hospital for acute sore throat with severe systemic infection or severe suppurative complications such as quinsy, parapharyngeal or retropharyngeal abscess, or Lemierre syndrome. Offer an immediate antibiotic with advice to people who are systemically very unwell, have symptoms and signs of a more serious illness or condition, or are at high risk of complications.

  2. Box 2ASSESSAssess likelihood of benefit

    Use FeverPAIN or Centor criteria. Acute sore throat is self-limiting and often viral; symptoms can last around one week, and most people get better within this time without antibiotics, regardless of cause.

  3. Box 3SUPPORTLow score

    no antibiotic: FeverPAIN 0-1 or Centor 0-2: do not offer an antibiotic. Advise on the usual course (around one week), that an antibiotic is not needed, and to seek medical help if symptoms worsen rapidly or significantly, do not start to improve after one week, or the person becomes systemically very unwell.

  4. Box 4DRUGIntermediate

    discuss options: FeverPAIN 2-3: consider no antibiotic prescription with advice, or a back-up prescription, taking account of the small average benefit (about 16 hours shorter symptoms), most people feeling better after a week either way, the unlikely complications, and possible adverse effects (particularly diarrhoea and nausea).

  5. Box 5DRUGHigher score

    immediate or back-up: FeverPAIN 4-5 or Centor 3-4: consider an immediate antibiotic, or a back-up prescription with safety-netting. First choice for adults: phenoxymethylpenicillin 500 mg four times a day or 1,000 mg twice daily for 5-10 days (5 days may be enough for symptomatic cure; 10 days may improve microbiological cure). Penicillin allergy/intolerance and pregnancy alternatives, and full interaction/organ-function checks, need the live source and BNF.

  6. Box 6REVIEW/LIMITRelieve symptoms; safety-net

    Consider paracetamol for pain or fever, or ibuprofen if preferred and suitable; advise adequate fluids. Back-up prescription: use if symptoms do not start to improve within 3-5 days or worsen rapidly or significantly at any time. Reassess if symptoms worsen rapidly or significantly, considering alternative diagnoses such as scarlet fever or glandular fever.

Lower UTI in non-pregnant women

Checked against the cited sources by automated comparison, November 2026. Steps labelled Direction-supported follow the cited sources' direction in this chart's own wording, not the sources' verbatim text. Not clinically validated.

  1. Box 1URGENTExclude a more serious infection

    Hospital referral for signs of serious illness such as sepsis. Fever or suspected pyelonephritis/complicated UTI needs its own pathway, not this lower-UTI regimen.

  2. Box 2ASSESSImmediate or back-up treatment

    Consider immediate or back-up antibiotic based on severity, complication risk, previous culture/antibiotics and preference.

  3. Box 3DRUGChoose using current factors

    Use local resistance and previous cultures. First choices: nitrofurantoin if eGFR ≥45 mL/min, or trimethoprim if resistance risk low. Open the NICE adult table for alternatives. NG109 Table 1 also allows nitrofurantoin with caution at eGFR 30-44 mL/min only for suspected or proven multidrug-resistant organisms when potential benefit outweighs risk. The current UK SmPC (emc product 15332, sections) shows an internal tension rather than a simple licence-stricter-than-NICE position: section 4.3 contraindicates nitrofurantoin at eGFR below 45 mL/min, while the same SmPC's section 4.4 states it may be used with caution as short-course therapy only for the treatment of uncomplicated lower urinary tract infection in individual cases with an eGFR between 30-44 ml/min to treat resistant pathogens, when the benefits are expected to outweigh the risks - mirroring the NG109 footnote. The prescriber reconciles the two SmPC sections for the individual case; check the dispensed product's current SmPC and do not infer suitability from this summary.

  4. Box 4SUPPORTGive back-up instructions

    If supplied: use if symptoms have not started improving within 48 hours, or worsen at any time. Explain adverse effects and when to seek help.

  5. Box 5SUPPORTSupport symptom control

    Paracetamol for pain, or suitable ibuprofen; enough fluids to avoid dehydration. No evidence found for cranberry products or urine-alkalinising agents to treat the infection.

  6. Box 6REVIEW/LIMITReassess if not improving

    Reassess if worsening or no improvement within 48 hours of antibiotics. Culture urine if not done; review results/treatment and consider alternative diagnoses or pyelonephritis.

Sources: NG109 lower UTI recommendations and Table 1; emc product 15332 nitrofurantoin SmPC; BNF nitrofurantoin monograph.

Low back pain and sciatica

Checked against the cited sources by automated comparison, November 2026. Steps labelled Direction-supported follow the cited sources' direction in this chart's own wording, not the sources' verbatim text. Not clinically validated.

  1. Box 1URGENTConsider alternative diagnoses

    Exclude specific causes such as cancer, infection, trauma and inflammatory disease, particularly with new or changed symptoms; use the relevant pathway if suspected.

  2. Box 2ASSESSAssess and stratify

    Consider risk stratification at first contact. Do not routinely image in non-specialist settings; specialist imaging only if likely to change management.

  3. Box 3SUPPORTActivity and exercise

    Tailor self-management advice, encourage normal activities and consider a suitable group exercise programme. Facilitate return to work/daily activities.

  4. Box 4DRUGLow back pain: medicines

    Consider oral NSAIDs after GI, liver and cardio-renal risk assessment; gastroprotection/monitoring as appropriate, lowest effective dose and shortest period. Do not offer paracetamol alone. Consider weak opioids (with or without paracetamol) for managing acute low back pain only if an NSAID is contraindicated, not tolerated or has been ineffective; do not routinely offer opioids for acute low back pain, and do not offer opioids for chronic low back pain. Do not offer SSRIs, SNRIs or tricyclic antidepressants, and do not offer gabapentinoids or other antiepileptics, for managing low back pain.

  5. Box 5DRUGSciatica: avoid harmful medicines

    Do not offer gabapentinoids, other antiepileptics, oral corticosteroids or benzodiazepines. No opioids for chronic sciatica; review existing use/withdrawal safely. NSAID benefit limited; review harms.

  6. Box 6REVIEW/LIMITPersistent sciatica

    Consider spinal decompression when non-surgical care has not improved pain/function and imaging matches symptoms. Full specialist criteria apply.

  7. ASSESSAsk about cauda equina features

    Bladder dysfunction (difficulty starting micturition, impaired sensation of urinary flow, retention, overflow incontinence); loss of rectal-fullness sensation or faecal incontinence; saddle (perianal, perineal or genital) sensory change; sexual dysfunction; severe or progressive bilateral leg weakness; gait disturbance; and bilateral radicular pain or unilateral pain progressing to bilateral (a warning that cauda equina may occur, not diagnostic alone).

  8. URGENTEmergency referral

    Suspected cauda equina syndrome, suspected deteriorating spinal cord pathology (for example gait disturbance or multilevel weakness), or suspected spinal infection: emergency referral - CKS Sciatica (revised August 2026) routes these usually via an Emergency Department with access to emergency MRI scanning, not routine MSK triage. Source exactness flag: CKS also carries 'suspicion of tumour or infection' in its urgent-referral list, so suspected spinal infection appears in both the emergency and the urgent source lists; the split is presented unresolved. Local cauda equina pathways vary - confirm your local route.

  9. URGENTUrgent referral

    Urgent (2-week) referral per CKS Sciatica (revised August 2026): suspected spinal tumour or infection; major motor loss (less than 3/5 on MRC grading); or bilateral radicular progression without CES features - urgent referral to a spinal surgery service, or urgent MRI. For suspected infection see also the emergency box: CKS lists it in both routes and the split is presented unresolved.

  10. URGENTHistory of cancer

    History of cancer with new-onset spinal pain is itself a CKS red flag: assess for possible bone metastases and refer urgently (within 2 weeks - urgent referral to a spinal surgery service or urgent MRI per CKS, or the NG12 suspected-cancer route where that fits the picture); this is a separate referral question from the cauda equina routes.

Osteoarthritis

Checked against the cited sources by automated comparison, November 2026. Steps labelled Direction-supported follow the cited sources' direction in this chart's own wording, not the sources' verbatim text. Not clinically validated.

  1. Box 1ASSESSClinical diagnosis

    Age 45+, activity-related joint pain and no morning stiffness or stiffness ≤30 minutes: diagnose clinically without imaging. Do not routinely image unless atypical features/alternative diagnosis.

  2. Box 2SUPPORTExercise is core treatment

    Offer tailored strengthening/aerobic exercise; consider supervised sessions. Explain that initial discomfort can occur and regular exercise benefits pain/function.

  3. Box 3SUPPORTWeight management

    For overweight/obesity, explain weight-loss benefits and support the chosen goal; any loss may help, and 10% is likely to benefit more than 5%.

  4. Box 4DRUGMedicines support activity

    Use medicines alongside non-drug care at the lowest effective dose for the shortest time. Offer topical NSAID for knee OA; consider for other joints. If topical treatment ineffective/unsuitable, consider oral NSAID after risk review and offer gastroprotection.

  5. Box 5DRUGAvoid low-value treatments

    Do not routinely offer paracetamol/weak opioids except under limited source criteria. No strong opioids or glucosamine. Corticosteroid injections may give short-term relief if appropriate.

  6. Box 6REVIEW/LIMITReview and joint replacement

    Agree follow-up; review if treatment is not working. Consider joint-replacement referral if joint symptoms substantially impact quality of life and non-surgical care is ineffective or unsuitable. Do not exclude solely because of age, sex or gender, smoking, comorbidities, overweight or obesity/BMI (NG226 1.6.1 and 1.6.3). Local 2026 checklist (East Sussex MSK Hip and Knee OA Checklist 2026): X-ray within the last 18 months (referrals without it may be returned), BMI <35 ideally or reasonable documented attempts to reduce BMI, at least 6 months conservative treatment, a decision aid and shared-decision conversation, and smoking-cessation advice if appropriate. These local items are commissioning/access gates, not NICE referral criteria - a commissioning barrier must not delay an urgent clinical pathway; confirm the current checklist version and requirements on the day of referral. (Area-specific example: East Sussex - local routes and services vary.)

Sources: NG226 recommendations; East Sussex MSK Hip-and-knee-OA-checklist-2026 PDF.

Gout

Checked against the cited sources by automated comparison, November 2026. Steps labelled Direction-supported follow the cited sources' direction in this chart's own wording, not the sources' verbatim text. Not clinically validated.

  1. Box 1URGENTExclude septic arthritis

    Assess for septic arthritis and other inflammatory/crystal arthritis. Suspected septic arthritis: refer immediately under the local pathway.

  2. Box 2ASSESSConfirm diagnosis

    Measure urate. If <360 micromol/L in a flare but gout remains suspected, repeat >=2 weeks after settling. Consider aspiration/microscopy if uncertain; imaging if aspiration cannot be done/uncertainty remains.

  3. Box 3DRUGTreat the flare

    Offer NSAID, colchicine or short oral corticosteroid course after considering comorbidities, co-prescriptions and preference. Kidney function branch: ideally avoid NSAIDs when eGFR is below 30 - if one is prescribed, monitor the person closely (CKS NSAIDs - prescribing issues); reduce the colchicine dose or lengthen the interval when eGFR is 10-50 and avoid colchicine in severe renal impairment (BNF). A short oral corticosteroid course (off-label) is the remaining first-line NG219 option. Consider an intra-articular or intramuscular corticosteroid injection (off-label) only when NSAIDs and colchicine are contraindicated, not tolerated or ineffective (NG219). Consider PPI with NSAID; ice packs can help.

  4. DRUGColchicine dose card - product-specific

    PRODUCT-SPECIFIC REGIMENS - follow the SmPC of the product actually dispensed. BNF / emc 6415 regimen: 500 micrograms 2-4 times daily until symptoms relieved; maximum 6 mg per course; do not repeat a course within 3 days (72 hours). Tiofarma (emc 14362) regimen: 1 mg to start, 500 micrograms after 1 hour, no further tablets for 12 hours, then if needed maximum 500 micrograms every 8 hours until symptoms relieved; maximum 6 mg per course; 72-hour gap. Both products: stop immediately if diarrhoea or vomiting occur - these may be the first signs of intoxication; emc 6415 additionally lists nausea and abdominal pain as stop-now symptoms. Contraindicated in pregnancy, breastfeeding, and women of childbearing potential unless using effective contraception; severe renal or hepatic impairment; blood dyscrasias; haemodialysis. Females: effective contraception during treatment and for at least 3 months after. Males: do not father a child during treatment and for at least 6 months after (Tiofarma SmPC 4.4/4.6). Renal: BNF - reduce dose or increase interval if eGFR 10-50 mL/min/1.73 m2, avoid in severe impairment (eGFR below 10 contraindicated for chronic prophylaxis). SmPCs give no numeric threshold: 6415 - caution mild, reduce dose or increase interval moderate, severe contraindicated; Tiofarma - 0.5 mg per day in mild/moderate, severe contraindicated. Check CYP3A4/P-glycoprotein interactions before use; some combinations require avoidance. Older adults may need adjustment. This is a flare regimen, not ULT prophylaxis.

  5. Box 4SUPPORTDiscuss ULT

    Offer treat-to-target ULT for multiple/troublesome flares, CKD 3-5, diuretics, tophi or chronic gouty arthritis. Discuss with others after a flare. Start at least 2-4 weeks after settling; frequent flares may justify starting during a flare.

  6. Box 5DRUGTitrate to target

    Allopurinol or febuxostat first-line; allopurinol first-line with major CVD. Start low; monthly urate guides increases. Kidney function branch (BNF): allopurinol maximum initial dose 100 mg daily in renal impairment, increased only if response inadequate - in severe impairment give below 100 mg daily or lengthen the interval; use febuxostat with caution when creatinine clearance is below 30 mL/minute. Aim <360 micromol/L; consider <300 with tophi/chronic arthritis or frequent flares despite <360.

  7. Box 6REVIEW/LIMITPrevent flares and review

    Discuss prophylaxis when starting/titrating ULT; offer colchicine if chosen while target reached. Consider annual urate after target. Refer for uncertainty, contraindicated/ineffective treatment, CKD 3b-5 or transplant.

Dyspepsia / GORD

Checked against the cited sources by automated comparison, November 2026. Steps labelled Direction-supported follow the cited sources' direction in this chart's own wording, not the sources' verbatim text. Not clinically validated.

  1. Box 1URGENTAlarm features and bleeding first

    Significant acute GI bleeding: refer immediately, the same day. Review medicines; consider cardiac/biliary disease. SUSPECTED-CANCER TRIGGERS (NG12 oesophageal/stomach,with mapped citations): refer on the suspected cancer pathway for dysphagia (NG12 1.2.1 oesophageal, 1.2.7 stomach), or age 55 and over with weight loss PLUS any of upper abdominal pain, reflux or dyspepsia (1.2.1 oesophageal, 1.2.7 stomach, [2015, amended 2025]). Consider a suspected cancer pathway referral for an upper abdominal mass consistent with stomach cancer (1.2.6). Consider NON-URGENT direct-access upper GI endoscopy at 55 or over with: treatment-resistant dyspepsia; upper abdominal pain with low haemoglobin; raised platelet count with any of nausea, vomiting, weight loss, reflux, dyspepsia or upper abdominal pain; or nausea/vomiting with any of weight loss, reflux, dyspepsia or upper abdominal pain (1.2.3 oesophageal, 1.2.9 stomach, identical criteria). Endoscopy for haematemesis: 1.2.8 (stomach). Do not let a PPI trial mask alarm features.

  2. Box 2ASSESSLifestyle changes

    Offer healthy-eating, weight-loss and smoking-cessation advice. Avoid personal triggers; discuss measures for meal-related/nocturnal symptoms.

  3. Box 3DRUGUninvestigated dyspepsia

    Uninvestigated dyspepsia: offer empirical full-dose PPI for 4 weeks; also offer H. pylori test-and-treat. Leave a 2-week PPI washout before breath/stool-antigen testing. These are options within a planned sequence, not simultaneous testing during PPI use.

  4. Box 4DRUGGORD: treat then step down

    Full-dose PPI for 4 or 8 weeks; if symptoms recur, lowest effective dose. Offer H2-receptor antagonist if inadequate PPI response. Severe oesophagitis has a different long-term route.

  5. Box 5SUPPORTH. pylori: use the original regimen

    Use breath/stool-antigen testing or locally validated lab serology, not office serology. Eradication regimens and doses are not condensed here: use the full current guidance and local antimicrobial policy; discuss adherence.

  6. Box 6REVIEW/LIMITReview persistent symptoms: all three referral triggers

    Step down to lowest effective treatment; offer annual review. CG184 1.11.1) - consider referral to a specialist service for people: (1) of any age with gastro-oesophageal symptoms that are non-responsive to treatment or unexplained; (2) with suspected GORD who are thinking about surgery; (3) with H. pylori that has not responded to second-line eradication therapy. 'Unexplained' is defined in the NICE suspected-cancer guideline as symptoms or signs that have not led to a diagnosis after initial assessment in primary care.

Depression

Checked against the cited sources by automated comparison, November 2026. Steps labelled Direction-supported follow the cited sources' direction in this chart's own wording, not the sources' verbatim text. Not clinically validated.

  1. Box 1URGENTAsk directly, warn, involve family, act on risk

    Ask about suicidal ideation and intent. Considerable immediate risk to self or others: urgent specialist mental-health referral (NG222 1.2.9). Assess support and arrange help appropriate to need. NG222 safeguards: warn that suicidal thoughts, self-harm and suicide can be more prevalent in the EARLY STAGES of antidepressant treatment, and ensure a risk-management strategy is in place (NG222 1.4.22, scoped: this recommendation applies to people aged 18 to 25 years OR those thought to be at increased risk of suicide). Advise the person AND their family or carer to be vigilant for mood changes, agitation, negativity and hopelessness, and suicidal ideation, and to contact their practitioner if concerned - particularly when starting or changing treatment and at times of increased personal stress (1.2.11). If assessed at risk of suicide: do not withhold treatment for depression because of suicide risk; take into account toxicity in overdose and if necessary limit the amount of medicine available; consider increasing the level of support (1.2.12). Advise seeking further help if the situation deteriorates (1.2.8). Name crisis routes in the plan: 999 for immediate risk to life, NHS 111 mental-health option, Samaritans 116 123 - confirm the local crisis-team route before republication.

  2. Box 2ASSESSAssess needs and severity: name the measure and threshold

    Assess symptoms, duration, functioning, history, comorbidities and preferences rather than symptom count alone. NG222): 1.2.4 - consider using a validated measure (for example, for symptoms, functions and/or disability) when assessing suspected depression to inform and evaluate treatment. SEVERITY INDICATOR: the guideline used a score of 16 on the PHQ-9 scale as its indicator - scores less than 16 defined as less severe depression, scores of 16 or more as more severe depression (NG222 definitions). Risk, safety plan, and safeguarding override severity scoring at every step. When considering treatments: carry out an assessment of need and develop a treatment plan, taking into account any physical health problems and any coexisting mental health problems (NG222 1.4.1).

  3. Box 3SUPPORTLess severe: least intrusive first

    Consider guided self-help first. Do not routinely offer antidepressants first-line unless informed preference. If declining treatment/improving, reassess normally in 2-4 weeks.

  4. Box 4SUPPORTMore severe: shared choice

    Match treatment to needs/preferences. All options in NICE table 2 can be first-line; discuss choices rather than a universal medicine/therapy default.

  5. Box 5DRUGIf medication chosen

    Agree benefits, harms, dose, withdrawal and follow-up. Check overdose toxicity, interactions and comorbidities. Sertraline and mirtazapine cards are examples for shared selection, not a fixed preferred-drug ladder.

  6. REVIEW/LIMITStopping, older people, relapse prevention and St John's Wort

    STOPPING (NG222 1.4.13-1.4.16): warn that abrupt stopping, missed doses or under-dosing can cause withdrawal symptoms (dizziness/unsteadiness, electric-shock sensations, irritability/anxiety/low mood, restlessness, sleep problems, sweating, nausea, palpitations, headaches, aches) - usually appearing within a few days and resolving in 1-2 weeks, but sometimes lasting weeks to months and sometimes severe, particularly if stopped suddenly. When stopping: take into account the half-life (short-half-life antidepressants taper more slowly) and duration of treatment; use proportional dose reductions; take particular care with paroxetine and venlafaxine; monitor during dose reduction for both withdrawal symptoms and the return of symptoms of depression, basing frequency on clinical and support needs (NG222 1.4.17, close paraphrase); the paroxetine/venlafaxine particular-care point and the fluoxetine alternate-day option are 1.4.18. See the mental-health-review route's withdrawal-vs-relapse wording for the full rules. OLDER PEOPLE (1.4.24): take into account general physical health, comorbidities and possible interactions with other medicines; carefully monitor for side effects. RELAPSE PREVENTION (1.8.1, close paraphrase): continuation of treatment after full or partial remission may reduce relapse risk - reach a shared decision. ST JOHN'S WORT (1.4.38, close paraphrase): advise on different potencies of preparations and potential serious interactions with other medicines (including hormonal contraceptives, anticoagulants and anticonvulsants) and that it should not be prescribed or advised for depression.

  7. Box 6REVIEW/LIMITReview early; monitor risk

    Antidepressant review usually within 2 weeks; within 1 week if age 18-25 or suicide concern. Higher risk/18-25: also review 1 week after dose increase. Review response in 2-4 weeks and monitor harms/suicidality.

Sources: NICE NG222 recommendations.

Asthma

Checked against the cited sources by automated comparison, November 2026. Steps labelled Direction-supported follow the cited sources' direction in this chart's own wording, not the sources' verbatim text. Not clinically validated.

  1. Box 1URGENTAcute illness comes first

    Treat an acutely unwell or highly symptomatic person immediately; obtain supporting tests if available without delaying treatment. If tests must wait, arrange them once acute symptoms are controlled. This chart is not an acute-attack treatment protocol.

  2. Box 2ASSESSAll ages: history before labels

    Record symptom pattern, variation, triggers, atopy and alternate diagnoses. For test-capable people, do not confirm asthma without compatible history and objective support; code suspected until confirmed. ICS can make FeNO/spirometry appear normal.

  3. Box 3ASSESSDiagnosis: adults over 16 - all four steps usable

    Check blood eosinophils or FeNO first: DIAGNOSE asthma if the eosinophil count is above the laboratory reference range or FeNO is 50 ppb or more (NG245 1.2.1). If not confirmed, BDR with spirometry: diagnose if FEV1 increases 12% or more AND 200 ml or more from pre-bronchodilator, or 10% or more of predicted normal FEV1 (1.2.2). If spirometry is unavailable or delayed: measure PEF TWICE DAILY FOR 2 WEEKS - diagnose if PEF variability (amplitude percentage mean) is 20% or more (1.2.3). If still unconfirmed but clinically suspected: refer for consideration of a bronchial challenge test; diagnose if bronchial hyper-responsiveness is present (1.2.4).

  4. Box 4ASSESSDiagnosis: children 5-16 - fourth-step decision rules

    FeNO first: diagnose if 35 ppb or more (1.2.5). Otherwise BDR with spirometry: diagnose if FEV1 rises 12% or more from baseline, or 10% or more of predicted normal FEV1 (1.2.6). If spirometry delayed: PEF twice daily for 2 weeks, diagnose if variability 20% or more (1.2.7). If still unconfirmed but suspected: EITHER skin-prick testing to house dust mite OR total IgE plus blood eosinophil count (1.2.8) - EXCLUDE asthma if no house-dust-mite sensitisation on skin-prick or total serum IgE not raised; DIAGNOSE if sensitisation or raised total IgE AND eosinophil count more than 0.5 x 10^9 per litre. If still in doubt: refer to a paediatric specialist for a second opinion, including consideration of a bronchial challenge test (1.2.9).

  5. Box 5ASSESSDiagnosis: under 5

    Use a monitored treatment trial and regular review because objective testing is often impractical. Try objective tests at age 5; retry every 6–12 months if unable. Refer any preschool child with 1 hospital admission or ≥2 emergency-department admissions for wheeze in 12 months.

  6. Box 6SUPPORTBefore stepping up at any age

    Check diagnosis, adherence, technique, triggers, smoking/vaping, environment and comorbidities. Do not prescribe SABA without an ICS prescription at any age. Match device to ability and check technique. After starting/changing medicines, review in 8–12 weeks.

  7. Box 7DRUGTreatment: 12+ start and early steps

    New diagnosis: low-dose ICS/formoterol as needed (AIR). If highly symptomatic or presenting with a severe exacerbation, start low-dose MART alongside acute treatment. If not controlled on AIR, low-dose MART; if still uncontrolled, moderate-dose MART.

  8. Box 8DRUGTreatment: 12+ later steps

    On moderate-dose MART with good adherence, check FeNO/eosinophils: if raised, refer. If neither raised, consider an 8–12-week LTRA or LAMA trial, then assess the other as directed. Uncontrolled despite both trials: specialist. Existing inhalers have a separate switching pathway.

  9. Box 9DRUGTreatment: 5–11 start and MART

    Start twice-daily paediatric low-dose ICS plus SABA as needed. If uncontrolled and able to manage MART, consider paediatric low-dose MART, then moderate-dose MART. Check the exact product and age: NG245 notes one budesonide/formoterol 100/6 DPI licensed for MART at ages 6–11, none at age 5; other use is off-label.

  10. Box 10DRUGTreatment: 5–11 non-MART route

    If unable to manage MART, consider an 8–12-week LTRA trial with low-dose ICS plus SABA, stopping if ineffective. If uncontrolled, offer low-dose ICS/LABA plus SABA, then moderate-dose. Refer if uncontrolled on moderate-dose MART or ICS/LABA.

  11. Box 11DRUGTreatment: under 5 trial and review

    Consider 8–12 weeks of twice-daily paediatric low-dose ICS plus SABA for interval symptoms or severe episodes. If no response, check technique/adherence, environment and alternative diagnoses, then refer. If resolved, consider stopping and review again after 3 months.

  12. Box 12DRUGTreatment: under 5 persistent symptoms

    If recurrent, restart regular ICS and titrate low to moderate dose as indicated. If uncontrolled on moderate dose, consider an 8–12-week LTRA trial; stop if ineffective or not tolerated and refer. Explain montelukast neuropsychiatric warnings.

  13. Box 13REVIEW/LIMITAction plan: risk stratification and ICS increase

    Actively identify people at risk of poor outcomes and tailor care (NG245 1.15.1): non-adherence; SABA over-use (more than 2 inhalers per year); needing 2 or more courses of oral corticosteroids per year; 2 or more emergency-department visits or any hospital admission for asthma. For adults (17 and over) using an ICS in a single inhaler, within a self-management programme: offer an increased ICS dose for 7 days when control deteriorates, with the action plan stating how and when, and what to do if symptoms do not improve - consider quadrupling the regular ICS dose; do not exceed the maximum licensed daily dose (1.14.4). Review cadence (NG245 1.16.1): in primary care, people with asthma should be reviewed at least annually and after any exacerbation by a healthcare professional with appropriate training in asthma management; the review should incorporate a written personalised action plan. Step-down (1.10.1/1.10.3, close paraphrase): at annual review, discuss with the person (or their family or carer, if appropriate) the potential risks and benefits of decreasing maintenance therapy when asthma has been well controlled on current therapy, and agree how the effects of decreasing will be monitored and reviewed, including self-monitoring and follow-up with a healthcare professional.

  14. SUPPORTOccupational asthma and pregnancy

    OCCUPATIONAL (NG245 1.4.1-1.4.2): in adult-onset asthma, poorly controlled established asthma, or reappearance of childhood asthma, ask - Are symptoms the same, better or worse on days away from work? Are symptoms the same, better or worse when on holiday (time away from work, longer than usual breaks, at weekends or between shifts)? Record all answers for later review. Refer suspected occupational asthma to an occupational asthma specialist. PREGNANCY (1.12.1-1.12.3, close paraphrase): asthma review during early pregnancy and in the postpartum period; emphasise the importance and safety of maintaining good control and of continuing asthma medicines to avoid problems for mother and baby; advise using short- and long-acting beta2 agonists, inhaled corticosteroids and oral theophyllines as normal during pregnancy; advise pregnant smokers on the dangers and give stop-smoking support.

Sources: NICE NG245 recommendations.

Checked against the cited sources by automated comparison, November 2026. Steps labelled Direction-supported follow the cited sources' direction in this chart's own wording, not the sources' verbatim text. Not clinically validated.

  1. Box 1ASSESSIndividualise targets: default HbA1c tiers

    Agree goals around needs, comorbidities, preferences and burden. DEFAULT TIERS (NG28 1.5.7-1.5.8): managed by healthy living and diet, or diet plus an initial medication regimen NOT associated with hypoglycaemia - support them to aim for 48 mmol/mol (6.5%); on a medicine associated with hypoglycaemia - aim for 53 mmol/mol (7.0%); if HbA1c rises to 58 mmol/mol (7.5%) or higher on the initial regimen - reinforce diet, healthy-living and adherence advice, support the person to aim for 53 mmol/mol (7.0%) AND intensify medicines. 1.5.9: consider relaxing the target case-by-case, particularly for older or frail adults or where comorbidity or reduced life expectancy makes tight control less beneficial. Agree the individual target with the person (1.5.5); support healthy living/adherence.

  2. Box 2DRUGNo relevant comorbidity / HF

    No relevant comorbidity, or heart failure: offer modified-release metformin + SGLT-2 inhibitor. If metformin contraindicated/not tolerated: SGLT-2 monotherapy. Other comorbidity, renal function and frailty can change this branch.

  3. Box 3DRUGStart initial medicines by comorbidity branch

    Use specific branches: ASCVD includes subcutaneous semaglutide with initial treatment; early onset considers GLP-1 receptor agonist or tirzepatide. Obesity has its own initial-treatment recommendation. Full combined-comorbidity criteria apply. Semaglutide dose scopes (not interchangeable): NICE NG28 1.15.1/1.15.2) recommend subcutaneous semaglutide (Ozempic) up to 1 mg once a week for adults with type 2 diabetes and atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, for its cardiovascular, renal and glycaemic benefits; the identified evidence covered doses only up to 1 mg once a week. The product licence (Ozempic SmPC section 4.2, emc 9750): the starting dose is 0.25 mg once weekly; after 4 weeks the dose should be increased to 0.5 mg; after at least 4 weeks on 0.5 mg it can be increased to 1 mg; after at least 4 weeks on 1 mg it can be increased to 2 mg once weekly to further improve glycaemic control; 0.25 mg is not a maintenance dose and weekly doses higher than 2 mg are not recommended. Doses above 1 mg are licensed for glycaemic control but are NOT the NICE-recommended initial ASCVD therapy; do not treat the two numbers as interchangeable.

  4. Box 4DRUGCKD / frailty change treatment

    eGFR 20 to <30: offer dapagliflozin or empagliflozin + DPP-4 inhibitor. eGFR <20: consider DPP-4 inhibitor. Frailty: offer MR metformin; SGLT-2 only if frailty does not create adverse-event risk. Full contraindication/alternative criteria apply.

  5. Box 5DRUGContraception, pregnancy and breastfeeding before GLP-1 or tirzepatide

    Before any GLP-1 receptor agonist or tirzepatide (recommended as initial therapy in this section), cover contraception and pregnancy explicitly - NG28's own initial-medicines preamble directs prescribers to the SmPC for pregnancy/breastfeeding precautions, and the chart now carries the MHRA position (GOV.UK GLP-1 guidance): use effective contraception during GLP-1 treatment and for a wash-out before trying to conceive - semaglutide (Wegovy, Ozempic, Rybelsus) at least 2 months; tirzepatide (Mounjaro) at least 1 month; liraglutide 0 months (stop just before trying). Do not use in pregnancy, when trying to conceive, or when breastfeeding; if pregnancy occurs, talk to a healthcare professional straight away. Tirzepatide may reduce oral-contraceptive effectiveness in people who are overweight or obese: add a barrier method (e.g. condom) for four weeks after starting AND for four weeks after any dose increase, or switch to a non-oral method (coil or implant). Before starting an SGLT-2 inhibitor, check whether the person may be at increased risk of diabetic ketoacidosis, for example if they have had a previous episode of DKA, are unwell with intercurrent illness, are at risk of dehydration or volume depletion, or are following a very low carbohydrate or ketogenic diet (NG28 1.21.1). Review treatment changes jointly with the person; this summary is not a basis to intensify treatment on its own.

  6. URGENTSick-day rules: pause list, DKA triggers, urgent help

    Completes the sick-day pathway (TREND 2025 4th edition HCP guidance). PAUSE during dehydrating illness: the SGLT2 inhibitor if acutely ill, unable to eat, nauseated or vomiting with abdominal discomfort, dehydrated, or diagnosis uncertain (ABCD recommendation); metformin temporarily stopped in dehydration (severe diarrhoea or vomiting, fever, reduced fluid intake) - contact a healthcare professional; diuretics, ACE inhibitors/ARBs and NSAIDs temporarily stopped in dehydration (kidney-function risk). EUGLYCAEMIC DKA: on an SGLT2 inhibitor, DKA can occur with glucose 14 mmol/L or less - teach the signs (nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, excessive thirst, frequent urination, difficulty breathing, confusion, unusual fatigue or sleepiness) and the need for urgent attention even when glucose is normal or only slightly raised. DKA diagnosis thresholds: blood ketones 3 mmol/L or more (or more than 2+ on urine strips), glucose above 11 mmol/L, bicarbonate below 15 or venous pH below 7.3. GLP-1 receptor agonists and tirzepatide - acute pancreatitis (per-product SmPC warnings, both): semaglutide - Ozempic SmPC section 4.4 (emc product 9750); tirzepatide - Mounjaro SmPC section 4.4 (emc product 15484). Both state: patients should be informed of the symptoms of acute pancreatitis, including persistent, severe abdominal pain, and advised to seek immediate medical attention if they occur; if pancreatitis is suspected, the medicine should be discontinued; if the diagnosis of pancreatitis is confirmed, it should not be restarted. Fournier's gangrene on SGLT2i: pain, tenderness, erythema or swelling in the genital or perineal area with fever or malaise - discontinue and seek urgent care. SEEK URGENT MEDICAL HELP if: pregnant; SGLT2i plus DKA signs even with normal glucose; persistent vomiting with inability to keep fluids down; blood ketones 1.6 mmol/L or higher and unable to self-manage; unable to keep glucose above 3.5 mmol/L; or not improving despite this advice. Revise sick-day rules at every annual review and give written information (trenddiabetes.online).

  7. URGENTSick-day: seek urgent help if

    Pregnant; on an SGLT2 inhibitor with DKA symptoms even when glucose is normal; vomiting persists or fluids cannot be kept down; ketones are 1.6 or higher and self-management is not possible; glucose cannot be kept above 3.5 mmol/L; or the person is deteriorating.

  8. ASSESSSick-day: ketone check

    If acutely unwell with capillary glucose over 12 mmol/L, check blood ketones: 0.6-1.5 mmol/L is DKA risk (fluids, correct glucose, retest in 2 hours); 1.6-2.9 is high risk (contact the diabetes team); 3.0 or higher needs urgent medical attention and may need A and E.

  9. REVIEW/LIMITSick-day: surgery or acute serious illness

    Interrupt the SGLT2 inhibitor if hospitalised for major surgery or acute serious medical illness, measure blood (not urine) ketones, and restart only when ketones are normal and the condition has stabilised (MHRA).

  10. REVIEW/LIMITSick-day: fluids, carbohydrate and recovery

    While unwell: maintain carbohydrate intake and drink regularly (TREND suggests about 3 litres/day for most adults, but this is NOT a universal target - follow any fluid restriction for heart failure, kidney disease or hyponatraemia).

  11. SUPPORTSick-day rules: when acutely unwell

    When acutely unwell (vomiting, diarrhoea, fever, poor intake, dehydration): pause the SGLT2 inhibitor; stop metformin while dehydrated or at renal risk; reduce the sulfonylurea if eating less; stop the DPP-4 inhibitor or GLP-1 receptor agonist and seek urgent help if pancreatitis is suspected; temporarily stop diuretics, ACE inhibitors/ARBs and NSAIDs if dehydrated.

  12. Box 6REVIEW/LIMITReviewing medicines: the NG28 1.24 hard rules and 1.22 review process

    HbA1c every 3-6 months until stable, then 6-monthly. HARD RULES (NG28): 1.24.3 - STOP GLP-1 receptor agonists or tirzepatide if the person becomes underweight (BMI of less than 18.5 kg/m2). 1.24.4 - STOP GLP-1 receptor agonists or tirzepatide if they do not help the person reach their individualised glycaemic targets AND they are not being taken for their cardiovascular benefits. 1.24.6 - DO NOT OFFER both a GLP-1 receptor agonist or tirzepatide AND a DPP-4 inhibitor together to treat type 2 diabetes. 1.24.2 - consider CONTINUING SGLT-2 inhibitors for their cardiovascular or renal benefits, even if they do not help the person reach their individualised glycaemic targets. REVIEW PROCESS: 1.22.1 - make a shared decision about changes with the person; 1.22.2 - optimise the current regimen before changing treatments (adverse effects, prescribed doses/formulations, adherence, revisit diet and healthy-living advice); 1.20.2 - when starting initial therapy with metformin and one or more other medicines, introduce one at a time starting with metformin and checking tolerability; if using an SGLT-2 inhibitor, start this as soon as metformin is at the maximum tolerated dose; 1.9.3 - with more than one comorbidity, make a shared decision about which comorbidity to prioritise. KETO DIETS: 1.21.2 - address modifiable DKA risks before starting an SGLT-2 inhibitor (people on a very-low-carbohydrate or ketogenic diet may need to delay until they have changed their diet); 1.21.3 - advise people on an SGLT-2 inhibitor that such a diet increases DKA risk and to speak with their healthcare professional first. Use current further-medication, insulin and complication chapters when goals unmet; no insulin dose generated.

Stable COPD

Checked against the cited sources by automated comparison, November 2026. Steps labelled Direction-supported follow the cited sources' direction in this chart's own wording, not the sources' verbatim text. Not clinically validated.

  1. Box 1ASSESSConfirm diagnosis: baseline work-up and severity grading

    Suspect in over-35s with risk factors and breathlessness, chronic cough/sputum, winter bronchitis or wheeze. Also ask about (NG115 1.1.2, close paraphrase): weight loss, reduced exercise tolerance, waking at night with breathlessness, ankle swelling, fatigue, occupational hazards, chest pain and haemoptysis - the last two are uncommon in COPD and raise the possibility of alternative diagnoses. Confirm with post-bronchodilator spirometry (FEV1/FVC below 0.7); use the MRC dyspnoea scale to grade breathlessness (1.1.3). BASELINE (1.1.15, close paraphrase): a chest radiograph to exclude other pathologies, a full blood count to identify anaemia or polycythaemia, and BMI calculated. SEVERITY GRADING (NG115 severity table): post-bronchodilator FEV1/FVC below 0.7 with FEV1 80% predicted or more = mild, 50-79% = moderate, 30-49% = severe, below 30% = very severe. Think about COPD in younger symptomatic people even when the ratio is 0.7 or above; consider alternative diagnoses in older people without typical symptoms despite a low ratio. Offer respiratory review and spirometry when imaging shows incidental emphysema or chronic airways disease.

  2. Box 2SUPPORTOptimise core care

    Before escalation: tobacco-dependence treatment, vaccinations, short-acting bronchodilator, and offer of pulmonary rehabilitation to people who view themselves as functionally disabled by COPD. Assess symptoms/exacerbations, not lung function alone.

  3. Box 3DRUGChoose dual route

    Persistent breathlessness/exacerbations: offer LAMA+LABA without asthmatic/steroid-responsive features. Consider LABA+ICS with those features. Both require core-care criteria; not interchangeable defaults.

  4. Box 4DRUGTriple therapy: the clinical-review gate comes first

    GATE BEFORE TRIPLE (NG115 1.2.13): before starting LAMA+LABA+ICS, conduct a clinical review to ensure that non-pharmacological COPD management is optimised and tobacco-dependence treatment has been used or offered if the person smokes; that acute episodes of worsening symptoms are caused by COPD exacerbations and not by another physical or mental health condition; and that day-to-day symptoms adversely impacting quality of life are caused by COPD and not another condition. Then: on LABA+ICS, offer triple for quality-of-life-limiting symptoms, 1 severe (hospitalised) exacerbation or 2 moderate exacerbations within a year (1.2.14); on LAMA+LABA, consider triple for severe or recurrent moderate exacerbations (1.2.15). 1.2.12: if symptoms are controlled on long-acting bronchodilators outside the dual-therapy recommendations, explain they can continue current treatment until both parties agree to change.

  5. Box 5REVIEW/LIMITSymptom-only trial: 3 months

    On LAMA+LABA with troublesome symptoms: consider 3-month triple trial. No improvement: stop ICS and return to LAMA+LABA; if improved, continue. Document ICS reason; review at least annually.

  6. Box 6REVIEW/LIMITDevices and adverse effects: MHRA ICS warning and inhaler rules

    Train/observe technique; minimise inhaler types. Discuss ICS harms (NG115 1.2.8): be aware of, and be prepared to discuss, the risk of side effects (including pneumonia) in people taking inhaled corticosteroids for COPD - FOLLOW THE MHRA SAFETY ADVICE on the risk of PSYCHOLOGICAL AND BEHAVIOURAL side effects associated with inhaled corticosteroids. 1.2.19: when prescribing long-acting medicines, ensure people receive inhalers they have been trained to use (for example, by specifying the brand and inhaler in prescriptions). 1.2.20: in most cases bronchodilator therapy is best administered using a hand-held inhaler (including a spacer if appropriate). 1.2.21-1.2.23: carbon footprint of inhalers. Product-specific doses need full BNF/device checks.

  7. Box 7REVIEW/LIMITEosinophil-guided add-ons (specialist): eligibility split by product

    Per NICE TA1142 and TA1166: for uncontrolled COPD (1 or more severe or 2 or more moderate exacerbations in the previous 12 months) with blood eosinophils at least 0.3 x10^9/L (300 cells/microlitre or more): dupilumab (TA1142 1.1) is an add-on option in adults on triple therapy (ICS + LABA + LAMA), or on double therapy (LABA + LAMA) if an inhaled corticosteroid is not appropriate; mepolizumab (TA1166 1.1) is an add-on option only in adults on triple therapy including an ICS. Specialist-initiated; the primary care role is recognition and referral.

Atrial fibrillation

Checked against the cited sources by automated comparison, November 2026. Steps labelled Direction-supported follow the cited sources' direction in this chart's own wording, not the sources' verbatim text. Not clinically validated.

  1. Box 1ASSESSConfirm with ECG

    Suspected AF: palpate pulse; if irregular, perform 12-lead ECG. Suspected paroxysmal AF not captured: choose ambulatory monitoring by frequency.

  2. URGENTPresenting acutely: emergency cardioversion and the 48-hour rules

    NG196 1.8.1: carry out emergency electrical cardioversion, without delaying to achieve anticoagulation, in people with life-threatening haemodynamic instability caused by new-onset atrial fibrillation. Presenting acutely without life-threatening instability (1.8.2): offer either rate or rhythm control if the onset is less than 48 hours; offer rate control if onset is more than 48 hours or is uncertain. Suspected concomitant acute decompensated heart failure (1.8.3): seek senior specialist input on the use of beta-blockers and do not use calcium-channel blockers. New-onset AF that will be treated with a rhythm-control strategy (1.8.4): consider either pharmacological or electrical cardioversion depending on clinical circumstances and resources.

  3. ASSESSEchocardiography

    Transthoracic echocardiography indications per NG196 1.3.1; do not perform echo routinely once anticoagulation is agreed SOLELY for the purpose of further stroke-risk stratification (1.3.2).

  4. Box 2SUPPORTStroke / bleeding risk

    Use CHA2DS2-VASc and ORBIT when considering or reviewing anticoagulation. Address modifiable bleeding-risk factors (NG196 1.2.3): uncontrolled hypertension, poor INR control on a vitamin K antagonist, concurrent antiplatelets/SSRIs/NSAIDs, harmful alcohol, reversible anaemia. Discuss risk and preference; no score calculated here.

  5. Box 3DRUGDiscuss anticoagulation

    Offer DOAC at CHA2DS2-VASc 2 or more; consider for men at score 1, accounting for bleeding risk/appraisal criteria. Do not offer anticoagulation to people under 65 with no risk factors other than sex (score 0 for men, 1 for women) (1.6.7). Check contraindications and BNF before selection/dosing.

  6. Box 4DRUGAvoid blanket exclusions

    Do not withhold solely for age/falls risk. Do not use aspirin monotherapy solely for AF stroke prevention. Full low-risk/VKA alternatives remain in source.

  7. Box 5DRUGRate control: exceptions apply

    Usually rate control first, except new-onset, reversible cause, AF-related HF and suitable rhythm/ablation routes (1.7.1). Initial monotherapy: a standard beta-blocker (not sotalol) or a rate-limiting CCB (diltiazem or verapamil), chosen on symptoms, heart rate, comorbidities and preferences (1.7.2); in AF with concomitant heart failure, follow the NG106 recommendations on beta-blockers and avoiding rate-limiting CCBs (1.7.3). Consider digoxin monotherapy for non-paroxysmal AF if the person does no or very little physical exercise, or other rate-limiting options are ruled out by comorbidities or preferences (1.7.4). If monotherapy does not control symptoms thought to be from poor rate control, consider combination therapy with any two of beta-blocker, diltiazem or digoxin (1.7.5). Do not offer amiodarone for long-term rate control (1.7.6). If symptoms continue after rate control, consider pharmacological and/or electrical rhythm control (1.7.7).

  8. Box 6REVIEW/LIMITReview risk and treatment

    Review stroke/bleeding risk and anticoagulation need/quality at least annually, or earlier when clinically relevant. For people not anticoagulated: annual review and documentation (1.6.16). Review takes into account MHRA advice on DOACs about bleeding risk and renal-function monitoring in renal impairment (1.6.17). Uncontrolled symptoms or rhythm-control needs require full NICE/specialist review, not dose escalation from this summary. Acute presentations, including life-threatening haemodynamic instability: see the acute-presentations box (NG196 1.8.1-1.8.4) - this route covers them; no exclusion applies.

    Mildly reduced EF: consider four classes. Preserved EF: consider MRA + SGLT2 inhibitor under current appraisal criteria. Do not assume identical treatment across phenotypes.

    In HFrEF, assess iron status and check for anaemia with transferrin saturation (TSAT), serum ferritin and haemoglobin (1.4.5). Consider iron sucrose, ferric carboxymaltose or ferric derisomaltose if haemoglobin is below 150 g/L and iron deficiency is defined as TSAT below 20% or ferritin below 100 ng/mL (1.4.6). If iron-deficiency anaemia is found, do not assume it is caused by heart failure; think about investigating alternative causes (1.4.7).

  7. Box 6REVIEW/LIMITCongestion / coordinated care

    Diuretics for congestion/fluid retention, titrated to lowest required dose. Offer a personalised, exercise-based cardiac rehabilitation programme. MDT and primary care share the plan/monitoring.

  8. REVIEW/LIMITReview and recall

    The primary care team leads a full review of heart failure care at all times, recalling the person at least every 6 months and updating the clinical record, with changes understood, agreed and shared with the specialist heart failure MDT, and access to specialist services arranged if needed (1.1.4). Monitoring frequency follows clinical status and stability: days to every 2 weeks if condition or medication has changed; stable people with proven heart failure at least every 6 months (1.8.3). Minimum monitoring content: functional capacity, fluid status, cardiac rhythm (minimum pulse examination), cognitive and nutritional status, medication review, renal function, iron status and haemoglobin (1.8.1).

Table 1 risk grid: G1/G2 A1 = low (no CKD without other kidney-damage markers); G3a A1 or G1/G2 A2 = moderate; G1/G2 A3, G3a A2 or G3b A1 = high; G3a A3, G3b A2-A3, G4 and G5 = very high.

  3. Box 3KEYKnow referral criteria

    Refer if 5-year KFRE >5%; ACR >=70 unless diabetes explains it and is appropriately treated; ACR >30 with haematuria; sustained eGFR fall >=25% with category change within 12 months, or >=15 mL/min/1.73 m2 per year; BP above target despite >=4 antihypertensives at therapeutic doses; rare/genetic CKD or suspected renal artery stenosis. Take wishes/comorbidities into account.

  4. Box 4SUPPORTCKD BP target

    ACR <70: clinic systolic <140 (120-139), diastolic <90. ACR >=70: systolic <130 (120-129), diastolic <80 mmHg.

  5. Box 5DRUGRenal-protective treatment

    Hypertension + ACR >30: offer ACE inhibitor/ARB at highest tolerated licensed dose. SGLT2 inhibitor as add-on to optimised standard care (highest tolerated licensed ACE/ARB unless contraindicated) if eGFR 20 to <45 mL/min/1.73 m2, or 45-90 with either ACR >=22.6 mg/mmol or type 2 diabetes (1.6.9; dapagliflozin TA1075, empagliflozin TA942). Finerenone as add-on to optimised standard care (highest tolerated licensed ACE/ARB and an SGLT2 inhibitor unless unsuitable) for stages 3-4 CKD with ACR >=3 mg/mmol associated with type 2 diabetes and eGFR >=25 (1.6.10; TA877).

  6. Box 6DRUGStatin for CVD prevention

    Offer atorvastatin 20 mg for primary or secondary prevention of CVD. No risk-scoring tool: eGFR <60 and/or albuminuria already marks high risk. If lipid target not met and eGFR >=30: increase the atorvastatin dose; eGFR <30: agree higher doses with a renal specialist. Renal replacement therapy is outside NG238 scope.

  7. Box 7REVIEW/LIMITPotassium / renal monitoring

    Before renin-angiotensin antagonists check potassium/eGFR; repeat 1-2 weeks after start/increase. Do not routinely start if K >5.0 mmol/L. After starting or increasing: do not modify the dose if eGFR decrease from baseline is <25% or creatinine increase is <30%; repeat the test in 1-2 weeks (1.6.19, 1.6.20). If eGFR change >=25% or creatinine change >=30%: investigate other causes (volume depletion, concurrent medicines such as NSAIDs); if no other cause found, stop or reduce to a previously tolerated lower dose and add an alternative antihypertensive if needed (1.6.21). Stop if serum potassium reaches 6.0 mmol/L or more and other medicines known to promote hyperkalaemia have been discontinued (1.6.18).

  3. Box 3SUPPORTChoose place of care

    CRB65 ≥2: consider hospital. Score 1: primary care with safety-netting or virtual/SDEC/hospital-at-home/hospital care. Score 0: consider primary care + safety-netting; clinical judgement still applies.

  4. Box 4DRUGStart antibiotics promptly: timing, IV review and full adult table

    Start antibiotic treatment as soon as possible after establishing a diagnosis of CAP, and within 4 hours of presentation to hospital (1.5.1). Oral first line if the person can take oral medicines and severity does not require IV (1.5.4). If IV antibiotics are given: review by 48 hours and, if possible, consider switching to oral to complete the course (1.5.5). Review the choice when microbiology results are available, using a narrower-spectrum antibiotic if appropriate (1.5.6). ADULT TABLE 1 (close paraphrase): LOW severity - amoxicillin 500 mg three times a day for 5 days (higher doses can be used, see BNF); penicillin allergy or atypicals suspected - doxycycline 200 mg day 1 then 100 mg daily for 4 days (5-day course), or clarithromycin 500 mg twice daily for 5 days; erythromycin 500 mg four times daily for 5 days in pregnancy. MODERATE - amoxicillin as above WITH clarithromycin (or erythromycin in pregnancy) if atypicals suspected; penicillin allergy - doxycycline or clarithromycin as above. HIGH - co-amoxiclav 500/125 mg three times daily orally or 1.2 g three times daily IV WITH clarithromycin (or erythromycin in pregnancy), for 5 days; penicillin allergy - levofloxacin 500 mg twice daily (see MHRA January 2024 fluoroquinolone restrictions; consult local microbiologist if unsuitable).

  5. Box 5REVIEW/LIMITCourse / stability review

    Adults: stop after 5 days unless microbiology requires longer or instability persists. Review cultures; narrow/change as appropriate.

  6. Box 6REVIEW/LIMITSafety-net recovery: the timeline and selective 6-week X-ray

    EXPECTED RECOVERY (NG250 1.10.1): symptoms should steadily improve; most adults can expect - 1 week: fever should have resolved; 4 weeks: chest pain and sputum production should have substantially reduced; 6 weeks: cough and breathlessness should have substantially reduced; 3 months: most symptoms should have resolved but fatigue may still be present; 6 months: they will feel back to normal. FOLLOW-UP X-RAY: 1.12.1 - do NOT routinely offer follow-up chest X-rays after inpatient pneumonia; 1.12.2 - CONSIDER follow-up chest X-rays at 6 weeks following discharge for people with risk factors for lung cancer or other underlying respiratory disease (for example, people who smoke or are over 50 years), persisting or deteriorating symptoms, or unexplained weight loss; 1.12.3 - shared decision taking into account recent imaging, comorbidities/frailty, prognosis and preferences. Seek advice if rapid or significant worsening.

Fever in children under 5

  1. Box 1URGENTImmediate threats

    Airway/breathing/circulation compromise or reduced consciousness: assess immediately; emergency referral by appropriate transport, usually 999 ambulance.

  2. Box 2ASSESSTraffic-light thresholds the assessment depends on

    Record temperature, heart rate, respiratory rate and capillary refill time as part of routine assessment (1.2.8). THRESHOLDS (NG143): tachycardia by APLS criteria (Table 1) - more than 160/min under 12 months, more than 150/min at 12-24 months, more than 140/min at 2-5 years. Capillary refill 3 seconds or longer = AMBER (1.2.9). RED signs include: respiratory rate over 60 breaths per minute, grunting, moderate or severe chest indrawing, reduced skin turgor, bulging fontanelle, non-blanching rash, neck stiffness, status epilepticus, focal neurological signs. AMBER includes oxygen saturation of 95% or less when breathing air, capillary refill 3 seconds or longer, tachycardia per Table 1, crackles on auscultation. Pneumonia tachypnoea thresholds: over 60/min age 0-5 months, over 50/min age 6-12 months, over 40/min over 12 months. Oximeter caveat (close paraphrase): be aware some pulse oximeters can under- or overestimate oxygen saturation, especially if borderline; overestimation has been reported in people with dark skin. 1.2.28: ask about recent travel abroad and consider imported infections. Use ALL red/amber/green features; account for the individual child; temperature alone is insufficient over 6 months.

  3. Box 3ASSESSFebrile infants under 3 months: what happens next

    Under 3 months + 38C or higher: HIGH RISK (red) group for serious illness (1.2.12; note some vaccinations induce fever in under-3-months) - EVERY febrile infant under 3 months needs urgent paediatric assessment, even if well-looking. Age 3-6 months + 39C or higher: at least intermediate-risk (1.2.13). Under specialist care (1.5.2-1.5.6, close paraphrase): observe and record temperature, heart rate, respiratory rate; investigate with FBC, blood culture, CRP, urine testing for UTI (CXR only if respiratory signs; stool culture if diarrhoea); LUMBAR PUNCTURE unless contraindicated for infants under 1 month, all unwell infants 1-3 months, and infants 1-3 months with WBC below 5 or above 15 x10^9/L - without delay and whenever possible before antibiotics; PARENTERAL ANTIBIOTICS for all infants under 1 month with fever, all unwell infants 1-3 months, and infants 1-3 months with those WBC extremes. This chart is an under-5 overview, not the full sepsis, meningitis or neonatal-fever protocol; apply the complete current guidance for those presentations. The 1-3 month group is squarely in this chart's population. Fever lasting 5 days or longer: assess for Kawasaki disease (NG143).

  4. URGENTThink sepsis; meningococcal and meningitis recognition

    Think 'Could this be sepsis?' (NG143 1.2.2). Consider meningococcal disease (1.2.18: fever with non-blanching rash, especially if ill-looking, purpura over 2 mm, capillary refill 3 seconds or longer, or neck stiffness) and bacterial meningitis (1.2.19).

  5. Box 4URGENTRed / amber: act on risk

    Red without immediate threat: urgent paediatric referral (NG143 1.4.3); the most severe presentations go by 999 ambulance (1.4.2). Amber without diagnosis: safety net or specialist assessment, explicit warnings/access and/or follow-up.

  6. Box 5SUPPORTGreen and home care

    All green and no red/amber: home care with advice. Paracetamol or ibuprofen for distress, not temperature alone; not simultaneously. No drug dose selected.

  7. URGENTAntibiotic rules and the pre-hospital antibiotics question

    (NG143 1.4.9): DO NOT prescribe oral antibiotics to children with fever without apparent source. Pre-hospital parenteral antibiotics (NG143 1.4.10 vs NG240 1.2.4-1.2.6): UNRESOLVED source difference - both attributed positions shown below; no recommendation is made here. NG143 1.2.2: think 'Could this be sepsis?' and refer to NICE's guideline on suspected sepsis in under 16s if a child presents with fever and symptoms or signs that indicate possible sepsis. Unresolved source difference - pre-hospital parenteral antibiotics for suspected meningococcal disease: NG143 1.4.10 (2007) says give benzylpenicillin or a third-generation cephalosporin at the earliest opportunity and cross-refers to the meningococcal guideline; NG240 1.2.4-1.2.6 (2024) applies to STRONGLY suspected disease: give IV/IM ceftriaxone or benzylpenicillin only if a clinically significant transfer delay is likely (strongly suspected meningitis), or as soon as possible unless it delays transfer (strongly suspected meningococcal disease), and never with severe antibiotic allergy to EITHER ceftriaxone OR benzylpenicillin. The passages may read together as the 2024 guideline qualifying the 2007 one. Any drug, dose and route must be checked in the BNFC at the time; no paediatric dose is drafted.

  8. Box 6REVIEW/LIMITWhen to seek help again

    Fit, non-blanching rash, worsening child, greater parental worry, fever 5 days or more, or difficulty caring: seek further advice. Suspected meningococcal disease: transfer to hospital as an emergency (NG240 1.2.1; the most severe presentations go by 999 ambulance, NG143 1.4.2).
