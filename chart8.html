Haemorrhoids: assessment and management

Adults presenting in primary care with haemorrhoids. The CKS pathway covers conservative and drug management, red flags and when to refer. Suspected anorectal cancer routes through NICE NG12 and is not a haemorrhoid pathway.

1

Admission and suspected cancer first

Arrange emergency admission for a thrombosed or strangulated haemorrhoid presenting within 72 hours of onset, an incarcerated irreducible haemorrhoid, or signs of sepsis. For any rectal bleeding, mass or bowel change that could be cancer, assess against NG12 and use the suspected-cancer pathway when criteria are met. In pregnancy, severe symptoms may warrant urgent specialist advice.

2

Confirm the diagnosis and grade

Ask about bleeding pattern, pain, itching, swelling, prolapse, soiling, bowel habit and straining. Examine the perianal area and perform digital rectal examination; internal haemorrhoids usually need proctoscopy to confirm and grade (1-4). Consider other causes of perianal symptoms: anal fissure, abscess or fistula, skin tags, warts, inflammatory bowel disease.

3

Conservative measures for everyone

Advise: avoid straining and prolonged time on the toilet; keep stools soft with adequate fibre and fluids; anal hygiene (gentle cleaning, avoid excessive wiping); respond promptly to the urge to defecate. Treat constipation where present. Review medicines that constipate (for example opioids) and consider alternatives.

4

Symptom relief: what to offer and what to avoid

Offer simple analgesia such as paracetamol for pain. Avoid opioid analgesics (constipation) and avoid NSAIDs if there is bleeding. Note that many topical anaesthetic or corticosteroid preparations are affected by NHS England OTC prescribing restrictions - check current local formulary policy before prescribing; a short course of a topical corticosteroid with anaesthetic may be considered for severe symptoms per local policy.

5

Refer non-responders for procedures

Refer to secondary care if symptoms persist despite primary-care management, for grade 3-4 haemorrhoids, or for recurrent troublesome bleeding once cancer is excluded. Secondary-care options include rubber band ligation, sclerotherapy, and surgery for larger or persistent disease. Continue conservative measures meanwhile and safety-net for heavy bleeding.

1

Exclude serious causes

Assess perianal symptoms against cancer red flags (persistent mass, ulceration, unexplained bleeding with NG12 features) and refer on the suspected-cancer pathway when criteria are met. Consider perianal abscess, fistula, Crohn's disease and sexually transmitted infection where the picture is atypical, multiple, off-midline or recurrent.

2

Confirm the fissure

Typical history: sharp pain on defecation, often severe, lasting minutes to hours afterwards, with bright red blood on paper or stool. Gentle inspection (parting the buttocks) usually shows a posterior midline fissure; digital rectal examination is often too painful and is not required for a typical presentation. Acute = under 6 weeks; chronic = 6 weeks or more, often with a sentinel skin tag or exposed fibres.

3

Conservative care and pain relief

Advise soft, easily passed stools: fibre, fluids and a laxative (osmotic such as macrogol, plus a stimulant if needed) when stools are hard; avoid straining; simple analgesia; gentle anal hygiene. For extreme pain, consider topical lidocaine 5% ointment (1-2 mL as needed) for a few days only as an adjunct while other measures take effect.

4

Topical GTN when unhealed at one week

For a fissure not healed after 1 week of conservative measures, offer topical glyceryl trinitrate 0.4% ointment applied twice daily for 6-8 weeks. Warn about headache (around 1 in 4 people - usually settles; simple analgesia or pausing treatment can help) and postural hypotension. Avoid GTN in children, pregnancy and breastfeeding, and with hypotension or phosphodiesterase inhibitors; check the BNF for interactions and cautions.

Review at 6-8 weeks; second-line and referral

Review after 6-8 weeks. Healed: advise on preventing recurrence (bowel habit, fibre, fluids). Not healed: check adherence and diagnosis; options are a second GTN course or specialist advice about topical diltiazem 2% (off-label), and surgical referral for persistent or recurrent chronic fissure (options include botulinum toxin or lateral sphincterotomy). For children whose fissure has not healed after 2 weeks, refer to paediatrics.

1

History and examination

Ask about duration, change in size, shape or colour, bleeding, itching or pain, previous skin cancer, sun exposure and sunburn history, immunosuppression and family history. Examine the whole lesion (site, size, border, colour, surface, fixation) and check regional lymph nodes. Photograph with a scale marker where this supports referral or monitoring.

2

Suspected melanoma: 7-point checklist

Use the weighted 7-point checklist for pigmented lesions. Major features (2 points each): change in size, irregular shape, irregular colour. Minor features (1 point each): largest diameter 7 mm or more, inflammation, oozing, change in sensation. Refer on the suspected-cancer pathway (2-week wait) any lesion scoring 3 points or more, and refer if there is strong clinical suspicion whatever the score. Consider dermoscopy where trained. Also refer a new pigmented line under a nail, or a growing lesion under a nail.

3

Suspected squamous cell carcinoma

Consider a suspected-cancer pathway referral (2-week wait) for people with a skin lesion raising suspicion of SCC - typically a keratinising, crusting or ulcerated non-healing nodule or plaque, especially on sun-exposed skin, in older people, or in immunosuppression. High-risk sites and organ-transplant recipients lower the threshold.

4

Basal cell carcinoma: refer, not 2-week wait

Consider a routine (non-urgent) referral for lesions typical of basal cell carcinoma - pearly papule or nodule with telangiectasia, or a slowly enlarging scabbed ulcer. Use a suspected-cancer pathway referral only if there is concern that a delay could significantly affect management, for example a lesion near the eye or rapid growth, or if the diagnosis is uncertain between BCC and SCC.

5

Lumps: soft-tissue sarcoma criteria

For a subcutaneous or deep lump, consider an urgent direct-access ultrasound (or suspected-cancer referral per local pathway) when it is: 5 cm or more (about golf-ball size), rapidly increasing in size, painful, deep to fascia, or recurrent after previous excision. Do not assume a lump is a lipoma because it is soft; size and growth drive the route.

6

Benign lesions: reassure, treat, or refer routinely

Common benign causes - seborrhoeic keratosis, dermatofibroma, epidermoid cyst, skin tags, cherry angioma, lipoma without sarcoma features - usually need reassurance or routine management only. Treat per the relevant CKS topic; refer routinely for diagnostic uncertainty, cosmetic or functional problems that warrant removal, or lesions that keep changing without meeting 2-week-wait criteria. Never excise a possibly malignant pigmented lesion in primary care without the required competencies and pathways.

Review and safety-net

When monitoring a lesion judged benign, agree what change should trigger re-attendance (growth, bleeding, ulceration, colour change) and give sun-protection advice. If a referred patient re-attends with progression or no appointment, escalate. Document measurements and site clearly to support the receiving clinician.

Hyponatraemia: assessment and first management

Adults with a low serum sodium found in primary care. The CKS pathway covers severity grading, emergency criteria, the diagnostic work-up (volume status, serum and urine osmolality) and first management by cause. Specialist and inpatient correction protocols are out of scope.

1

Grade severity; emergency criteria

Mild: sodium 130-135 mmol/L; moderate: 125-129; severe: under 125. Acute means development within 48 hours. Arrange emergency admission for severe hyponatraemia, acute hyponatraemia, significant symptoms (confusion, seizures, reduced consciousness, vomiting) or clinical hypovolaemia. Rapid correction is itself dangerous - correction rate limits are a specialist, inpatient matter.

+

Original sources and qualifications

Read the full source, scope and exceptions. A check date is not a new evidence-review date.

  • CKS hyponatraemia: definitions
  • CKS hyponatraemia: admission criteria
2

Confirm and assess

Repeat the sodium (ideally within 24 hours when moderate, severe or symptomatic). Take a history: fluid intake, diuretics (especially thiazides), antidepressants, anticonvulsants, illness, alcohol and liver or heart disease; review all medicines. Assess volume status - hypovolaemia (postural drop, tachycardia, dry mucosa), euvolaemia or hypervolaemia (oedema). Check for symptoms and their onset.

+

Original sources and qualifications

Read the full source, scope and exceptions. A check date is not a new evidence-review date.

  • CKS hyponatraemia: assessment
3

Key investigations

Arrange paired serum and urine osmolality with urinary sodium. Serum osmolality separates hypertonic (over 295 - for example hyperglycaemia), pseudo/isotonic (275-295) from true hypotonic hyponatraemia (under 275). Urine osmolality under 100 suggests dilution (primary polydipsia, low solute); over 100 with urine sodium over 30 supports SIAD-type causes in euvolaemia. Check glucose, lipids/protein (pseudohyponatraemia), U&E, thyroid function and consider cortisol; chest imaging or further tests follow the suspected cause.

+

Original sources and qualifications

Read the full source, scope and exceptions. A check date is not a new evidence-review date.

  • CKS hyponatraemia: investigations
4

Malignancy and SIAD caution

SIAD can be a marker of underlying malignancy. Where SIAD is suspected without an obvious cause, assess for cancer features and use the NG12 suspected-cancer pathway when criteria are met. Also consider urgent endocrine input when an adrenal or pituitary cause is possible.

+

Original sources and qualifications

Read the full source, scope and exceptions. A check date is not a new evidence-review date.

  • CKS hyponatraemia: suspected cancer in SIAD
  • NG12 suspected cancer
5

First management by cause

Treat the underlying cause. For asymptomatic mild hyponatraemia: review medicines and stop or reduce a culprit drug where possible, then recheck sodium in about 2 weeks. For thiazide-associated hyponatraemia, avoid thiazides in future. If a culprit medicine cannot be stopped, or the cause is unclear, or sodium is moderate with symptoms or falling, seek specialist (endocrine/renal) advice - discuss asymptomatic moderate hyponatraemia with endocrinology. Hypervolaemic hyponatraemia follows heart failure, cirrhosis or CKD pathways.

+

Original sources and qualifications

Read the full source, scope and exceptions. A check date is not a new evidence-review date.

  • CKS hyponatraemia: management by cause
6

Monitor and document

Recheck sodium after each change (stop/start of medicines, fluid advice) at an interval matched to severity - typically about 2 weeks for mild asymptomatic cases, sooner if moderate or symptoms change. Document the suspected cause, medicines changes and patient advice. Refer to endocrinology or renal services when sodium fails to normalise, the cause remains uncertain, or specialist therapies are being considered.

+

Adults with suspected trigeminal neuralgia in primary care. The CKS pathway covers recognition, red flags for secondary causes, first-line carbamazepine and when to seek specialist advice. No other drug is recommended without specialist input.

1

Red flags: think secondary causes

Admit or refer urgently when there are: sensory changes in the trigeminal distribution, deafness or other ear problems, skin/oral lesions suggesting perineural tumour spread, ophthalmic-division-only or bilateral symptoms, optic neuritis, a family history of multiple sclerosis, or onset under age 40. These features suggest secondary trigeminal neuralgia (for example MS or tumour) needing specialist assessment and imaging.

+

Original sources and qualifications

Read the full source, scope and exceptions. A check date is not a new evidence-review date.

  • CKS trigeminal neuralgia: red flags
2

Recognise classical trigeminal neuralgia

Paroxysms of unilateral, severe, electric-shock or stabbing facial pain in one or more trigeminal divisions (usually V2/V3), lasting a fraction of a second to 2 minutes, triggered by innocuous stimuli (touching the face, chewing, talking, brushing teeth, wind), with refractory pain-free periods between attacks. Attacks can cluster over weeks and remit for months. Distinguish from dental pain, temporomandibular disorders, cluster headache and post-herpetic neuralgia.

+

Original sources and qualifications

Read the full source, scope and exceptions. A check date is not a new evidence-review date.

  • CKS trigeminal neuralgia: diagnosis
3

Start carbamazepine (only licensed drug)

Offer carbamazepine. Start 100 mg twice daily and titrate gradually (for example by 100-200 mg every 2 weeks) against response and tolerability; the usual effective dose is 200 mg three to four times daily, maximum 1600 mg/day. Modified-release (for example 600-800 mg twice daily) can smooth levels. Check the BNF for interactions, blood dyscrasia and hyponatraemia cautions, and HLA considerations where relevant; arrange baseline and monitoring bloods per local practice and the SPC.

+

4

If carbamazepine fails or is not tolerated

Seek specialist advice (neurology or pain/headache clinic, via advice-and-guidance where available) when carbamazepine is contraindicated, ineffective at maximum tolerated dose, or not tolerated. CKS recommends no other drug treatment in primary care - alternatives such as oxcarbazepine, lamotrigine, baclofen or gabapentinoids are specialist-initiated. Refer on when surgery (for example microvascular decompression or ablative procedures) may be appropriate.

5

Follow-up: dose, tolerability, mood

Review regularly while titrating: attack frequency and severity, adherence, adverse effects (dizziness, drowsiness, ataxia, rash - stop and seek advice for rash), and drug interactions. Screen for depression and anxiety - chronic severe facial pain carries significant mood and suicide risk - and address them. In sustained remission, discuss reducing carbamazepine gradually to the lowest effective maintenance dose; relapses restart the cycle.

Codeine and opioid dependence: review and down-titration

Adults taking prescribed or over-the-counter opioids (including codeine) where dependence, loss of benefit or harm is a concern, or who want to stop. NICE NG215 covers shared decisions and gradual, individualised withdrawal - it deliberately gives no universal tapering schedule. Medically managed detoxification for opioid use disorder follows specialist drug services.

1

Safety first

Assess overdose and respiratory risk (high dose, sedative co-prescription such as benzodiazepines or gabapentinoids, alcohol, sleep apnoea), withdrawal severity, suicidality, and unsafe use (injecting, sourcing additional supply). Consider take-home naloxone per local policy and signpost or refer to specialist drug and alcohol services when use is beyond prescribed supply. Abrupt stopping is rarely appropriate - plan reduction instead, except where rapid withdrawal is safer (for example severe harm).

2

Recognise problems and review

Signs suggesting problems with dependence: running out early, frequent requests for dose increases, loss of efficacy of a previously effective medicine, use for mood or sleep, hoarding, or withdrawal between doses. For chronic primary pain, NG193 says do not initiate opioids at all; for people already taking them, review prescribing through shared decision making - explain the lack of evidence and agree safe continuation only where there is benefit at a safe dose with few harms.

3

Discuss withdrawal openly

Discuss reducing or stopping when the medicine no longer benefits, problems with dependence have developed, the original condition has resolved, harms outweigh benefits, or the person wants to stop. Explain the expected benefits of reduction, acknowledge that dependence is an expected effect (not the person's fault), and allow time. Aim for agreement through shared decision making; involve family or carers if the person wishes.

4

Plan an individualised taper

Agree the plan with the person: urgency (gradual when no longer effective; more rapid when causing significant harm); whether the initial goal is complete withdrawal or dose reduction with review; which medicine first if more than one; and risk factors (long duration, high dose, past withdrawal symptoms, short half-life, concurrent medicines). Reduce gradually at a pace the person can tolerate, adjusting to symptoms - NG215 sets principles, not fixed percentages. Do not treat withdrawal symptoms with another dependence-forming medicine.

5

Support during reduction

Offer regular reviews (phone, video or face to face) at a frequency matched to risk. Treat the underlying condition with non-opioid options (see the pain management chart); offer psychological support and signpost peer support. Manage withdrawal symptoms with explanation, pacing and symptomatic measures that are not dependence-forming. For OTC codeine dependence, explain supply limits and agree structured stopping support.

6

Stuck, high risk or complex: refer

Seek specialist advice or refer to pain or drug services when: shared agreement on withdrawal cannot be reached and continuing is not in the person's best interests (NG215 1.5.24 route - document best-interests reasoning; do not stop abruptly), when misuse or diversion is suspected, when comorbid substance use or mental illness complicates care, or when high-dose or multi-medicine withdrawal exceeds primary-care confidence. Keep prescribing safe in the meantime - review intervals, quantities and co-prescriptions.
