Haemorrhoids: assessment and management

Checked against the cited sources by automated comparison, November 2026. Steps labelled Direction-supported follow the cited sources' direction in this chart's own wording, not the sources' verbatim text. Not clinically validated.

  1. Box 1URGENTAdmission and suspected cancer first: Consider

    admission for: extremely painful, acutely thrombosed external haemorrhoids presenting within 72 hours of onset; prolapsed internal haemorrhoids that have become swollen, incarcerated and thrombosed; or perianal sepsis (a rare but life-threatening complication). For any rectal bleeding, mass or bowel change that could be cancer, assess against NG12 and use the suspected-cancer pathway when criteria are met. In pregnancy, severe symptoms may warrant urgent specialist advice. NG12 colorectal triage (1.3.1-1.3.6): offer quantitative faecal immunochemical testing (FIT, HM-JACKarc or OC-Sensor) to adults with an abdominal mass, a change in bowel habit, iron-deficiency anaemia, or the specified age/symptom combinations including rectal bleeding (1.3.1); refer on a suspected cancer pathway if FIT is at least 10 micrograms of haemoglobin per gram of faeces (1.3.2); if no sample is returned or FIT is below 10, safety-net and do not delay referral where there is strong clinical concern (1.3.3). Consider a suspected cancer pathway referral for a rectal mass (1.3.5) or an unexplained anal mass or unexplained anal ulceration (1.3.6); these two recommendations sit outside the FIT-testing list, so referral is considered directly rather than through the FIT route (our reading of the NG12 1.3 list structure, not verbatim wording). Bleeding attributed to haemorrhoids still needs this FIT decision.

  2. Box 2ASSESSConfirm the diagnosis and grade

    Ask about bleeding pattern, pain, itching, swelling, prolapse, soiling, bowel habit and straining. Examine the perianal area and perform digital rectal examination; internal haemorrhoids usually need proctoscopy to confirm and grade (1-4). Consider other causes of perianal symptoms: anal fissure, abscess or fistula, skin tags, warts, inflammatory bowel disease.

  3. Box 3SUPPORTConservative measures for everyone

    Advise: avoid straining and prolonged time on the toilet; keep stools soft with adequate fibre and fluids; anal hygiene (gentle cleaning, avoid excessive wiping); respond promptly to the urge to defecate. Treat constipation where present. Review medicines that constipate (for example opioids) and consider alternatives.

  4. Box 4KEYSymptom relief: what to offer and what to avoid (CKS duration limits):

    Offer simple analgesia such as paracetamol for pain. Avoid opioid analgesics (constipation) and avoid NSAIDs if there is bleeding. Consider recommending a topical haemorrhoidal preparation for symptomatic relief; note that many topical anaesthetic or corticosteroid preparations are affected by NHS England OTC prescribing restrictions, so check current local formulary policy before prescribing. CKS duration limits (CKS haemorrhoids prescribing information, Topical haemorrhoidal preparations, last revised July 2021): 'Anaesthetic-containing preparations should only be used for a few days because they may cause sensitization of the anal skin. Corticosteroid-containing preparations should be used for no longer than 7 days because prolonged use may lead to skin atrophy, contact dermatitis, and skin sensitization.' Avoid excessive application, as absorption can occur through the rectal mucosa.

  5. Box 5REVIEW/LIMITRefer non-responders for procedures

    Refer to secondary care if symptoms persist or recur despite primary-care management once cancer is excluded. Refer for non-urgent assessment and management (CKS haemorrhoids): fourth-degree haemorrhoids, or third-degree haemorrhoids too large for non-operative measures (haemorrhoidectomy may be needed); perianal haematoma (a blue or dark coloured swelling at the anal verge) if symptoms are of less than 24 hours duration, for clot evaluation; combined internal and external haemorrhoids with severe symptoms (surgery may be required); thrombosed haemorrhoids when bleeding is problematic, or there is chronic irritation or leakage; and large skin tags (surgical excision may be required). Refer to an appropriate specialist (urgency by clinical judgement) if another serious pathology is suspected, such as inflammatory bowel disease or a sexually transmitted infection. Secondary-care options include rubber band ligation, sclerotherapy, and surgery for larger or persistent disease. Continue conservative measures meanwhile and safety-net for heavy bleeding.

  • NICE NG12 suspected cancer, 1.3 lower gastrointestinal (1.3.1-1.3.6)

Anal fissure: acute and chronic management

Checked against the cited sources by automated comparison, November 2026. Steps labelled Direction-supported follow the cited sources' direction in this chart's own wording, not the sources' verbatim text. Not clinically validated.

  1. Box 1URGENTExclude serious causes

    Assess perianal symptoms against cancer red flags (persistent mass, ulceration, unexplained bleeding with NG12 features) and refer on the suspected-cancer pathway when criteria are met. Consider perianal abscess, fistula, Crohn's disease and sexually transmitted infection where the picture is atypical, multiple, off-midline or recurrent. NG12 colorectal triage (1.3.1-1.3.6): offer quantitative FIT (HM-JACKarc or OC-Sensor) to adults with an abdominal mass, a change in bowel habit, iron-deficiency anaemia, or the specified age/symptom combinations including rectal bleeding (1.3.1); refer on a suspected cancer pathway if FIT is at least 10 micrograms of haemoglobin per gram of faeces (1.3.2); safety-net and do not delay referral where clinical concern is strong despite a low or absent FIT (1.3.3). The red flags above (persistent mass, ulceration) map to the NG12 entries outside the FIT list: consider a suspected cancer pathway referral for a rectal mass (1.3.5) or an unexplained anal mass or unexplained anal ulceration (1.3.6, anal cancer); these sit outside the FIT-testing list, so referral is considered directly rather than through the FIT route (our reading of the NG12 1.3 list structure, not verbatim wording).

  2. Box 2ASSESSConfirm the fissure

    Typical history: sharp pain on defecation, often severe, lasting minutes to hours afterwards, with bright red blood on paper or stool. Gentle inspection (parting the buttocks) usually shows a posterior midline fissure; digital rectal examination is often too painful and is not required for a typical presentation. Acute = under 6 weeks; chronic = 6 weeks or more, often with a sentinel skin tag or exposed fibres.

  3. Box 3SUPPORTConservative care and pain relief

    Advise soft, easily passed stools: fibre, fluids and a laxative (osmotic such as macrogol, plus a stimulant if needed) when stools are hard; avoid straining; simple analgesia; gentle anal hygiene. For extreme pain on defecation, consider a short course (a few days) of topical lidocaine 5% ointment for use before passing a stool - advise the person to apply 1-2 mL when required (CKS anal fissure management, last revised April 2021).

  4. Box 4SUPPORTTopical GTN when symptoms persist at one week: For an adult

    with a primary anal fissure who has had symptoms for 1 week or more without improvement, consider prescribing rectal glyceryl trinitrate (GTN) 0.4% ointment (CKS anal fissure management, April 2021): apply 2.5 cm of ointment (approximately 375 mg ointment, delivering 1.5 mg GTN) intra-anally every 12 hours; treatment may be continued until the pain abates, up to a maximum of 8 weeks (Rectogesic SmPC 4.2). Warn about headache: it is the most common treatment-related adverse reaction in the Rectogesic SmPC - very common and dose-related, with a 57% incidence in its clinical studies; it usually settles, and simple analgesia or pausing treatment can help (SmPC 4.8) and postural hypotension. Avoid GTN in children, pregnancy and breastfeeding, and with hypotension or phosphodiesterase inhibitors; check the BNF for interactions and cautions.

  5. Box 5REVIEW/LIMITReview at 6-8 weeks; second-line and referral

    Review after 6-8 weeks. Healed: advise on preventing recurrence (bowel habit, fibre, fluids). Unhealed in an adult after 6-8 weeks: if the person is asymptomatic or has had notable symptomatic improvement, consider (depending on clinical judgement) a second course of rectal GTN 0.4% with review after a further 6-8 weeks, or discussion with/referral to colorectal surgery. If there is no notable symptomatic improvement, confirm the GTN was used for 6-8 weeks: with inadequate adherence (for example it was stopped early due to intolerable headache), seek specialist advice on alternatives such as topical diltiazem 2% (unlicensed); with adequate adherence, refer to a general or colorectal surgeon for investigation and treatment. Unhealed in a child after 2 weeks (or earlier if the child is in a lot of pain): seek specialist advice or refer to a paediatrician (CKS anal fissure).

Skin lesion: suspected cancer referral

Checked against the cited sources by automated comparison, November 2026. Steps labelled Direction-supported follow the cited sources' direction in this chart's own wording, not the sources' verbatim text. Not clinically validated.

  1. Box 1ASSESSHistory and examination

    Ask about duration, change in size, shape or colour, bleeding, itching or pain, previous skin cancer, sun exposure and sunburn history, immunosuppression and family history. Examine the whole lesion (site, size, border, colour, surface, fixation) and check regional lymph nodes. For a suspicious pigmented lesion, assess against the NG12 weighted 7-point checklist - major features (2 points each): change in size, irregular shape, irregular colour; minor features (1 point each): largest diameter 7 mm or more, inflammation, oozing, change in sensation; a score of 3 or more meets the suspected-cancer (2-week-wait) melanoma referral threshold (NG12 1.7.1). Photograph with a scale marker where this supports referral or monitoring (PCDS dermoscopy and photography guidance). Direction-supported limbs: the general history items (previous skin cancer, sun exposure, immunosuppression, family history) are supported in direction by PCDS/CKS pages; wording is this chart's summary.

  2. Box 2URGENTSuspected melanoma: 7-point checklist

    Use the weighted 7-point checklist for pigmented lesions. Major features (2 points each): change in size, irregular shape, irregular colour. Minor features (1 point each): largest diameter 7 mm or more, inflammation, oozing, change in sensation (NG12 1.7.1, weighted 7-point checklist). Refer on a suspected cancer pathway for melanoma if the score is 3 or more, if dermoscopy suggests melanoma, and consider referral for a pigmented or non-pigmented lesion suggesting nodular melanoma. (NG12 1.7.1-1.7.3). Unresolved - not shown here: the previous version of this chart carried two further melanoma referral triggers. Neither appears in NG12 1.7 and the cited CKS melanoma page could not be confirmed against the current source, so their wording is not shown here. This card is not a complete statement of the NG12 melanoma referral criteria - check the current NG12 guideline.

  3. Box 3URGENTSuspected squamous cell carcinoma

    Consider a suspected-cancer pathway referral (2-week wait) for people with a skin lesion raising suspicion of SCC - typically a keratinising or ulcerating nodule/plaque. (NG12 1.7.4).

  4. Box 4ASSESSBasal cell carcinoma: refer, not 2-week wait

    Consider a routine (non-urgent) referral for lesions typical of basal cell carcinoma - pearly papule or nodule with telangiectasia, or a slow-growing ulcer with a raised rolled edge. Only consider a suspected-cancer pathway referral if there is particular concern that a delay may have a significant impact, for example because of site or size. (NG12 1.7.5-1.7.6).

  5. Box 5URGENTSuspected soft-tissue sarcoma: ultrasound first

    NG12 sarcoma route (1.11.4-1.11.7). Adults: consider an urgent, direct-access ultrasound scan for an unexplained lump that is increasing in size (1.11.4); consider a suspected cancer pathway referral if ultrasound findings are suggestive of soft tissue sarcoma, or are uncertain and clinical concern persists (1.11.5). Children and young people: consider a very urgent, direct-access ultrasound scan for an unexplained lump that is increasing in size (1.11.6), and consider a very urgent referral (for an appointment within 48 hours) if ultrasound findings suggest soft tissue sarcoma or are uncertain and clinical concern persists (1.11.7). Scope note: additional strengthening features sometimes listed (size 5 cm or more, rapid growth, pain, depth to fascia, recurrence after excision) do not appear in NG12 for soft-tissue sarcoma and are uncited here. Increasing size is the NG12 trigger - a small, painless, superficial but growing lump still meets the ultrasound criterion.

  6. Box 6SUPPORTBenign lesions: reassure, treat, or refer routinely

    Common benign causes usually need only reassurance or routine management: seborrhoeic keratosis and dermatofibroma are harmless and usually need no treatment beyond an accurate diagnosis and reassurance (British Association of Dermatologists patient leaflets); removal is an option if unsightly, itchy, catching or causing concern. Lipoma, skin tags and epidermoid (skin) cyst are usually harmless and often need no treatment (NHS; lipomas are removed on the NHS only if growing or causing symptoms from their site). Cherry angiomas (Campbell de Morgan spots) are common benign angiomas and most do not need treatment (PCDS patient leaflet). Refer routinely for diagnostic uncertainty, cosmetic or functional problems that warrant removal, or lesions that keep changing without meeting 2-week-wait criteria. A suspicious pigmented lesion meeting the NG12 checklist threshold is referred on the suspected-cancer pathway rather than excised in primary care (NG12 1.7.1); for suspected basal cell carcinomas, NG12 1.7.7 defers who-should-excise advice to the skin-tumour outcomes guidance. Direction-supported limb: the routine-referral criteria are supported in direction by NG12 referral criteria and CKS; wording is this chart's summary.

  7. Box 7REVIEW/LIMITReview and safety-net

    When monitoring a lesion judged benign, agree what change should trigger re-attendance (growth, bleeding, ulceration, colour change) and give sun-protection advice. If a referred patient re-attends with progression or no appointment, escalate. Document measurements and site clearly to support the receiving clinician.

Sources: NG12 recommendations organised by site of cancer, skin cancers section.

Hyponatraemia: assessment and first management

Checked against the cited sources by automated comparison, November 2026. Steps labelled Direction-supported follow the cited sources' direction in this chart's own wording, not the sources' verbatim text. Not clinically validated.

  1. Box 1URGENTGrade severity; emergency criteria

    Mild: sodium 130-135 mmol/L; moderate: 125-129; severe: under 125. Acute means development within 48 hours. Arrange emergency admission for severe hyponatraemia, acute hyponatraemia, ANY symptoms (the CKS admission criterion is unqualified), or clinical hypovolaemia. Rapid correction is itself dangerous - correction-rate limits are a specialist, inpatient matter.

  2. Box 2ASSESSConfirm and assess

    Repeat the sodium: in asymptomatic mild hyponatraemia managed in primary care, repeat in all people, ideally within 24 hours, to exclude a rapidly falling level that needs emergency admission (CKS, scoped to that branch); otherwise the timescale depends on clinical judgement (CKS assessment page). Scope: the CKS 24-hour figure sits inside the asymptomatic-mild primary-care branch. Take a history: fluid intake, diuretics (especially thiazides), antidepressants, anticonvulsants, illness, alcohol and liver or heart disease; review all medicines. Assess volume status - hypovolaemia (postural drop, tachycardia, dry mucosa), euvolaemia or hypervolaemia (oedema). Check for symptoms and their onset.

  3. Box 3ASSESSKey investigations

    Arrange paired serum and urine osmolality with urinary sodium. Serum osmolality separates hypertonic (over 295 - for example hyperglycaemia), pseudo/isotonic (275-295) from true hypotonic hyponatraemia (under 275). Urine osmolality of 100 mOsm/kg or below (maximally dilute urine) indicates absent ADH activity, as in primary polydipsia or low solute intake. Urine osmolality above 100 mOsm/kg means ADH is active and is interpreted with the urinary sodium: 30 mmol/L or below suggests hypovolaemic or dilutional causes (for example vomiting, diarrhoea, burns, excessive sweating, third spacing or recent diuretic use); above 30 mmol/L without diuretics or kidney disease supports SIAD-type causes in euvolaemia; with diuretics or kidney disease the urinary sodium cannot discriminate (CKS hyponatraemia, revised October 2025). Check glucose, lipids/protein (pseudohyponatraemia), U&E, thyroid function and consider cortisol; chest imaging or further tests follow the suspected cause.

  4. Box 4KEYMalignancy and SIAD caution

    SIAD can be a marker of underlying malignancy. Where SIAD is suspected without an obvious cause, assess for cancer features and use the NG12 suspected-cancer pathway when criteria are met. Also consider urgent endocrine input when an adrenal or pituitary cause is possible.

  5. Box 5SUPPORTFirst management by cause

    Treat the underlying cause. For asymptomatic mild hyponatraemia: review medicines and stop or reduce a culprit drug where possible, then recheck sodium in about 2 weeks. For thiazide-associated hyponatraemia, avoid thiazides in future. Discuss ASYMPTOMATIC moderate hyponatraemia with endocrinology; symptomatic patients route to the emergency-admission box. If a culprit medicine cannot be stopped, or the cause is unclear, seek specialist (endocrine/renal) advice. Hypervolaemic hyponatraemia follows heart failure, cirrhosis or CKD pathways.

  6. Box 6REVIEW/LIMITMonitor and document

    Recheck sodium after each change (stop/start of medicines, fluid advice) at an interval matched to severity - typically about 2 weeks for mild asymptomatic cases, sooner if moderate or symptoms change. Document the suspected cause, medicines changes and patient advice. Refer to endocrinology or renal services when sodium fails to normalise, the cause remains uncertain, or specialist therapies are being considered.

  • NICE CKS: hyponatraemia assessment (revised Oct 2025)

Trigeminal neuralgia: diagnosis and carbamazepine pathway

Checked against the cited sources by automated comparison, November 2026. Steps labelled Direction-supported follow the cited sources' direction in this chart's own wording, not the sources' verbatim text. Not clinically validated.

  1. Box 1URGENTRed flags: think secondary causes

    Admit, or refer urgently for specialist assessment, depending on clinical judgement, when there are red-flag symptoms and signs that may suggest a serious underlying cause (CKS management scenario): sensory changes; deafness or other ear problems; history of skin or oral lesions that could spread perineurally; pain only in the ophthalmic division or bilaterally; optic neuritis; family history of multiple sclerosis; age of onset before 40 years (CKS diagnosis page, all seven items). These features suggest secondary trigeminal neuralgia (for example MS or tumour) needing specialist assessment and imaging.

  2. Box 2ASSESSRecognise classical trigeminal neuralgia

    Paroxysms of unilateral, severe, electric-shock or stabbing facial pain in one or more trigeminal divisions (usually V2/V3), lasting a fraction of a second to 2 minutes, triggered by innocuous stimuli (touching the face, chewing, talking, brushing teeth, wind), with refractory pain-free periods between attacks. Attacks can cluster over weeks and remit for months. Distinguish from dental pain, temporomandibular disorders, cluster headache and post-herpetic neuralgia.

  3. Box 3SUPPORTStart carbamazepine (only licensed drug)

    Offer carbamazepine. Titration (Tegretol SmPC 4.2, trigeminal-neuralgia wording): slowly raise the initial dosage of 200-400 mg daily until freedom from pain is achieved (normally at 200 mg 3-4 times daily). Titration interval: UNRESOLVED - no specific titration interval is given here. The current CKS trigeminal-neuralgia page could not be confirmed for this chart, and an interval that appears in the same drug's BNF epilepsy dosing is not confirmed for trigeminal neuralgia; check the current CKS page and the Tegretol SmPC before titrating. Unresolved source difference - no recommendation is made here - maximum dose: Tegretol liquid SmPC maximum 1200 mg/day; Tegretol Prolonged Release SmPC 4.2 (updated 1 May 2026) says 1600 mg daily may be needed for trigeminal neuralgia yet states maximum recommended dose 1200 mg/day (repeated for elderly); BNF gives immediate- and prolonged-release up to 1.6 g/day. Modified-release preparations can smooth levels, particularly for overnight breakthrough pain (usual 600-800 mg daily in two divided doses). Interactions (Tegretol SmPC 4.4/4.5): carbamazepine is a CYP3A4 substrate and a potent CYP3A4 inducer. CYP3A4 inhibitors can raise carbamazepine levels and cause adverse reactions - named examples in the SmPC include macrolide antibiotics (e.g. erythromycin, clarithromycin), azole antifungals, diltiazem, verapamil and grapefruit juice; CYP3A4 inducers lower carbamazepine levels, and carbamazepine itself lowers the levels of many co-medications. Hormonal contraception (SmPC close paraphrase): women of child-bearing potential should be advised to consider using alternative forms of birth control while taking Tegretol; an oral contraceptive should contain not less than 50 micrograms oestrogen, or a non-hormonal method should be considered (see the pregnancy and contraception box below). HLA-B*1502 (SmPC 4.4): patients of Han Chinese and Thai origin should whenever possible be screened for this allele before starting carbamazepine (strongly associated with Stevens-Johnson syndrome; about 10% prevalence in those populations); if positive, carbamazepine should not be started unless there is no other therapeutic option. Also check the BNF for blood dyscrasia and hyponatraemia cautions. Monitoring (SmPC 4.4): complete pre-treatment blood counts, including platelets and possibly reticulocytes and serum iron, should be obtained as a baseline, and periodically thereafter. Serum sodium: measure before initiating in at-risk patients (pre-existing renal conditions associated with low sodium, or concomitant sodium-lowering medicines such as diuretics - note the thiazide/furosemide interaction), then after approximately two weeks and at monthly intervals for the first three months, or according to clinical need; if hyponatraemia is observed, water restriction is an important counter-measure if clinically indicated. Cross-refer to the hyponatraemia route.

  4. REVIEW/LIMITPregnancy and contraception safeguards

    Before starting in anyone who could become pregnant: consider pregnancy testing; counsel on the increased, dose-dependent risk of major congenital malformations (MHRA January 2021, about 9,000 exposed pregnancies); use highly effective contraception during treatment and for at least 2 weeks after stopping; warn that hormonal contraception can fail through enzyme induction and counsel other effective methods; if planning pregnancy, discuss switching before conception and before stopping contraception; contact a doctor immediately if pregnant. This is NOT a formal Pregnancy Prevention Programme - among antiepileptic medicines, formal PPPs apply to valproate and topiramate; carbamazepine has none.

  5. Box 4SUPPORTIf carbamazepine fails or is not tolerated

    Seek specialist advice (neurology or pain/headache clinic, via advice-and-guidance where available) when carbamazepine is contraindicated, ineffective at maximum tolerated dose, or not tolerated. CKS recommends no other drug treatment in primary care - alternatives such as oxcarbazepine, lamotrigine, baclofen or gabapentinoids are specialist-initiated. Refer on when surgery (for example microvascular decompression or ablative procedures) may be appropriate.

  6. Box 5REVIEW/LIMITFollow-up: dose, tolerability, mood, withdrawal

    Review regularly while titrating: attack frequency and severity, adherence, adverse effects (dizziness, drowsiness, ataxia, rash - stop and seek advice for rash), and drug interactions. Do not stop carbamazepine abruptly: abrupt withdrawal may precipitate seizures; reduce gradually, and if abrupt withdrawal is unavoidable, seek specialist advice about cover (SmPC 4.4; the specialist-cover-on-abrupt-withdrawal clause applies to epilepsy). Screen for depression and anxiety - chronic severe facial pain carries significant mood and suicide risk - and address them. In sustained remission, discuss reducing carbamazepine gradually to the lowest effective maintenance dose; relapses restart the cycle.

  • Tegretol Prolonged Release SmPC (updated 1 May 2026; 4.2 TN: 1600 mg may be needed, maximum recommended 1200 mg/day)
  • MHRA DSU Jan 2021: antiepileptic drugs in pregnancy

Codeine and opioid dependence: review and down-titration

Checked against the cited sources by automated comparison, November 2026. Steps labelled Direction-supported follow the cited sources' direction in this chart's own wording, not the sources' verbatim text. Not clinically validated.

  1. Box 1URGENTSafety first

    Assess overdose and respiratory risk (high dose, sedative co-prescription such as benzodiazepines or gabapentinoids, alcohol, sleep apnoea), withdrawal severity, suicidality, and unsafe use (injecting, sourcing additional supply). NG215 1.3.5: every management plan must include the symptoms and signs of an overdose and what the person should do if this happens. Overdose recognition: slow or shallow breathing, unresponsiveness, pinpoint pupils, blue lips. Emergency action: call 999, give naloxone if available, stay until help arrives - naloxone is not a substitute for emergency care. Consider take-home naloxone per local policy (for example local APC guidance) for people at high risk: high-dose opioids, benzodiazepine/gabapentinoid/alcohol co-use, history of overdose, or use beyond prescribed supply; arrange training for the person and household. Signpost or refer to specialist drug and alcohol services when use is beyond prescribed supply. Abrupt stopping is rarely appropriate - plan reduction instead, except where rapid withdrawal is safer (for example severe harm).

  2. Box 2ASSESSRecognise problems and review

    Signs suggesting problems with dependence (NG215 1.4.5): running out of a medicine early, making frequent requests for dose increases, or reporting loss of efficacy of a medicine that was previously working well. For chronic primary pain, NG193 says do not initiate opioids at all; for people already taking them, review prescribing through shared decision making - explain the lack of evidence and agree safe continuation only where there is benefit at a safe dose with few harms.

  3. Box 3ASSESSDiscuss withdrawal openly

    Discuss reducing or stopping when the medicine no longer benefits, problems with dependence have developed, the original condition has resolved, harms outweigh benefits, or the person wants to stop. Explain the expected benefits of reduction, acknowledge that dependence is an expected effect (not the person's fault), and allow time. Aim for agreement through shared decision making; involve family or carers if the person wishes.

  4. Box 4SUPPORTPlan an individualised taper

    Agree the plan with the person: urgency (gradual when no longer effective; more rapid when causing significant harm); whether the initial goal is complete withdrawal or dose reduction with review; which medicine first if more than one; and risk factors (long duration, high dose, past withdrawal symptoms, short half-life, concurrent medicines). Reduce gradually at a pace the person can tolerate, adjusting to symptoms - NG215 sets principles, not fixed percentages. Do not treat withdrawal symptoms with another dependence-forming medicine.

  5. Box 5SUPPORTSupport during reduction

    Offer regular reviews (phone, video or face to face) at a frequency matched to risk. Treat the underlying condition with non-opioid options (see the pain management chart); offer psychological support and signpost peer support. Manage withdrawal symptoms with explanation, pacing and symptomatic measures that are not dependence-forming. For OTC codeine dependence, explain supply limits and agree structured stopping support.

  6. Box 6REVIEW/LIMITStuck, high risk or complex: refer

    Seek specialist advice or refer to pain or drug services when: shared agreement on withdrawal cannot be reached and continuing is not in the person's best interests (NG215 1.5.24 route - document best-interests reasoning; do not stop abruptly), when misuse or diversion is suspected, when comorbid substance use or mental illness complicates care, or when high-dose or multi-medicine withdrawal exceeds primary-care confidence. Keep prescribing safe in the meantime - review intervals, quantities and co-prescriptions.

  • NICE NG215 recommendations (1.3.5; no naloxone recommendation in chapter)

Hair loss: assessment and management

Checked against the cited sources by automated comparison, November 2026. Steps labelled Direction-supported follow the cited sources' direction in this chart's own wording, not the sources' verbatim text. Not clinically validated.

  1. Box 1URGENTTinea capitis, kerion and scarring first

    Itchy, scaly scalp with patchy, irregular hair loss and post-auricular or cervical lymphadenopathy suggests tinea capitis: send skin and hair samples for mycology; start an oral antifungal on a positive result; if the mycology result is negative but clinical features are very suggestive of infection, arrange repeat skin and hair sampling and start oral antifungal treatment; and - because treatment should not be delayed in clinically obvious or severe cases - also when suspicion is strong before results are back, depending on clinical judgement (CKS fungal skin infection - scalp; culture can be falsely negative, particularly with kerion). In children, prescribe an oral antifungal in primary care only when the diagnosis is certain and there is appropriate prescribing experience and expertise; otherwise seek specialist advice from a paediatric dermatologist before starting. Suspected kerion (boggy, tender, pustular scalp swelling): arrange urgent dermatology referral. Scarring alopecia (shiny scalp with loss of follicular openings, as in discoid lupus erythematosus or cicatricial alopecia) causes permanent hair loss: refer to dermatology.

  2. Box 2ASSESSAssess pattern, timing and triggers

    Establish the pattern: patchy loss (alopecia areata - sudden well-defined patches, exclamation-mark hairs, possible nail pitting), diffuse shedding, or patterned thinning (male or female pattern). For diffuse shedding, ask about triggers 2-4 months earlier: childbirth, severe illness or infection, major surgery, haemorrhage, marked weight loss, and medicines such as lithium, valproate, some antidepressants, warfarin or retinoids. Ask about hair-care practices and traction, hair pulling (trichotillomania, more common in children and adolescents), family history, nail changes, and features of androgen excess in women (hirsutism, acne, menstrual disturbance).

  3. Box 3ASSESSBlood tests only when indicated

    Male and female pattern hair loss are diagnosed on history and examination; laboratory testing is generally unnecessary. Consider thyroid function, full blood count, ferritin and vitamin D when an underlying cause or alternative diagnosis (such as telogen effluvium) is suspected, the presentation is atypical, or there are features of hypothyroidism, anaemia or vitamin D deficiency. Women with clinical or biochemical evidence of androgen excess: refer to endocrinology to exclude an androgen-secreting tumour.

  4. Box 4SUPPORTMale pattern hair loss options

    Explain the usual course; treatment is optional and no treatment or cosmetic approaches are reasonable choices. Topical minoxidil 5% (solution licensed for ages 18-65; foam for ages 18-49) or oral finasteride 1 mg daily can improve or stabilise hair loss in some men: minoxidil solution and finasteride 1 mg are in the England and Wales Drug Tariff blacklist (Part XVIIIA) and minoxidil foam is generally also on local 'do not prescribe' lists, so they are bought over the counter or prescribed privately. Finasteride safeguards (MHRA DSU May 2026): ask about any history of depression or suicidal ideation before prescribing and review regularly; warn that depression, suicidal thoughts and sexual dysfunction (reduced libido, erectile and ejaculation disorders) can occur and may persist after stopping; anyone who develops depression or suicidal thoughts should stop immediately and contact their clinician as soon as possible (999 if at immediate risk); read and keep the patient alert card and leaflet; report breast changes; finasteride lowers PSA - double the result when interpreting. Assess response after 4-6 months and stop if there is no response after 12 months (4 months for the foam). Benefit is lost 3-12 months after stopping. Referral is usually unnecessary; consider it for atypical or uncertain diagnosis, a suspected underlying condition, no response to treatment, or significant psychosocial effects.

  5. Box 5SUPPORTFemale pattern hair loss options

    Explain the course and reassure that total hair loss is rare. Options include no treatment, cosmetic approaches, or topical minoxidil 2% solution or 5% once-daily foam (Regaine for Women), usually bought over the counter; the 2% solution is in the England and Wales Drug Tariff blacklist (Part XVIIIA, so no NHS GP prescription), while CKS notes the 5% foam is listed in Part VIIIA Category C - check the current Drug Tariff position before prescribing. Do not offer minoxidil 5% solution or twice-daily 5% foam (contraindicated in women), finasteride (not indicated for women; contraindicated in ALL women per the SmPC 4.1/4.3 - not only women of childbearing potential; women who are or may be pregnant must not handle crushed or broken tablets), or anti-androgens such as spironolactone without specialist advice. Assess response at 6 months and stop if there is no improvement: after 24 weeks for the 5% once-daily foam (Regaine for Women Once a Day Scalp Foam SmPC 4.2) or after 12 months for the 2% solution (Regaine for Women Regular Strength SmPC 4.2).

  6. KEYMinoxidil safety

    Topical minoxidil (all Regaine products): contraindicated with treated or untreated hypertension, any scalp abnormality (including psoriasis and sunburn), a shaved scalp, or with occlusive dressings or other topical scalp preparations; do not use during pregnancy or lactation. Known cardiovascular disease or cardiac arrhythmia: contact a physician before use. Stop and see a doctor if hypotension is detected or with chest pain, rapid heartbeat, faintness or dizziness, sudden unexplained weight gain, swollen hands or feet, persistent scalp redness or irritation, or other unexpected new symptoms. Unwanted hair growth can follow transfer to other areas - wash hands after applying; keep application sites away from children (hypertrichosis reported in infants after inadvertent contact). Accidental ingestion may cause serious cardiac adverse events - keep out of the reach of children. Foam products are extremely flammable aerosols. Licensed ages: men's solution 18-65, men's foam 18-49, women's products 18-65.

  7. Box 6SUPPORTAlopecia areata support and options

    Explain the natural history (regrowth is common) and offer support information (Alopecia UK); consider psychological support, including CAMHS referral for a distressed child. Visible regrowth (short, fine or depigmented hairs): no treatment needed. Limited, stable, inconspicuous loss without distress: watchful waiting is appropriate. Adults who want treatment: consider a 3-month trial of a potent topical corticosteroid (such as betamethasone valerate 0.1%) or a very potent one (clobetasol propionate 0.05%), an off-label indication; avoid facial sites. Seek specialist advice before treating children or pregnant or breastfeeding women. Refer to dermatology if the person wishes medical treatment, the diagnosis is uncertain, or there is no response to primary-care treatment.

  8. Box 7REVIEW/LIMITReferral, wigs and review

    Refer to dermatology: suspected kerion (urgent), scarring alopecia, alopecia areata where the person wishes medical treatment, the diagnosis is uncertain or there is no response, and atypical or extensive female pattern loss (a dermatologist with a special interest in hair where available). Androgen excess: endocrinology. Adverse psychosocial effects: psychological support or counselling. Wigs and camouflage products are mostly private; some people qualify for free or reduced-cost wigs on the NHS, which may require dermatology referral. Review treatment response at the intervals above and stop ineffective treatment.
