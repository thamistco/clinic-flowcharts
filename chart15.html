    1

    Recognise that the person may be dying

    ASSESS

    +

    Gather and document physiological, psychological, social and spiritual needs, current signs and symptoms, the clinical context, the person's goals and wishes and the views of those important to them (1.1.1). Look for changes such as agitation, Cheyne–Stokes breathing, falling level of consciousness, mottled skin, noisy respiratory secretions, progressive weight loss, increasing fatigue and loss of appetite (1.1.2). Improvement could mean the person is stabilising (1.1.3). Avoid investigations unlikely to affect care (1.1.4). Seek advice from more experienced colleagues when uncertain (1.1.7).

    2

    Shared decisions, hydration and medicine review

    SUPPORT

    +

    Establish how involved the person wishes to be, and check for an advance statement or advance decision to refuse treatment (1.3.1–1.3.2). Support the person to drink if they wish and are able; offer frequent mouth and lip care; assess hydration, preferably daily, and discuss the risks and benefits of clinically assisted hydration (1.4.1–1.4.5). Review current medicines and stop those not giving symptomatic benefit or that may cause harm (1.5.1).

    3

    Anticipatory medicines and symptom control

    DRUG

    +

    Prescribe anticipatory medicines and routes as early as possible, individualised, with indications and doses specified, for likely symptoms such as agitation, anxiety, breathlessness, nausea and vomiting, noisy respiratory secretions and pain (1.6.1–1.6.3). Avoid intramuscular injections; use subcutaneous or intravenous routes (1.5.5). Consider a syringe pump if more than 2 or 3 as-required doses are given within 24 hours (1.5.6). Breathlessness: consider an opioid, a benzodiazepine or both (1.5.17). Agitation or anxiety: trial of a benzodiazepine; delirium or agitation: trial of an antipsychotic (1.5.26–1.5.27). Noisy secretions causing distress: atropine, glycopyrronium bromide, hyoscine butylbromide or hyoscine hydrobromide (1.5.31). Sussex formulary appendix 4 lists, among others, morphine and alfentanil injection (pain and breathlessness), cyclizine, haloperidol and levomepromazine (nausea and vomiting), midazolam and haloperidol (agitation), and hyoscine butylbromide and glycopyrronium bromide (secretions); status: check colour. Check doses in the BNF and local palliative care guidance.

    4

    Review at least daily

    REVIEW/LIMIT

    +

    Monitor for changes at least every 24 hours and update the care plan (1.1.6). Reassess symptoms at least daily to guide titration (1.5.8). Seek specialist palliative care advice if symptoms do not improve promptly or there are undesirable side effects such as unwanted sedation (1.5.9). For someone unable to report pain, such as a person with dementia or learning disabilities, use a validated behavioural pain assessment (1.5.14).

    Approaching the end of life: identification, needs assessment and advance care planning

    1

    Identify people likely to be approaching the end of life

    ASSESS

    +

    Use systems to identify adults likely to be approaching the end of their life, for example the Gold Standards Framework, the Amber Care Bundle or SPICT, so that advance care planning discussions can start (1.1.1). Identify carers and other people important to them (1.1.2).

    2

    Holistic needs assessment and carers

    SUPPORT

    +

    Carry out and document an initial holistic needs assessment (1.2.1). Offer a carer's needs assessment under the Care Act 2014, and a young carer's needs assessment under the Children and Families Act 2014 (1.2.3). Think about practical and emotional support for carers, noting that young carers' needs differ (1.3.1–1.3.2). Review treatments to reduce unhelpful treatment burden (1.5.1–1.5.2).

    3

    Advance care planning and regular review

    REVIEW/LIMIT

    +

    Offer advance care planning, involving carers and people important to the person if they agree (1.6.1–1.6.2). Hold regular discussions about changing needs and preferences, and repeat holistic needs assessments and advance care plan reviews at key transition points, such as discharge from hospital or when the goals of treatment change (1.7.1). If the person may be entering the last days of life, see the last days of life pathway (NICE NG31).

    1

    Who should have an annual health check

    ASSESS

    +

    Anyone aged 14 or over on the practice learning disability register can have a free annual health check once a year (NHS). Offer it using NHS England's learning disability annual health check electronic template, involving a family member, carer or someone who knows the person well, and allow more time (NG54 1.5.1–1.5.2). People can ask to join the register (NHS).

    2

    Content of the check and reasonable adjustments

    SUPPORT

    +

    Include a mental health review, a physical health review covering conditions common in people with learning disabilities, a review of all medicines and their side effects, and a shared care plan for physical health problems including pain (NG54 1.5.3). Ask about epilepsy, constipation, dysphagia, eyesight and hearing; check vaccinations and carer support; plan transition to adult services at 18 (NHS). Record reasonable adjustments, such as pictures or simpler words, longer appointments or a quieter time of day, in the health action plan (NHS).

    3

    Changes in behaviour or skills

    ASSESS

    +

    Consider a mental health problem if behaviour changes, for example loss of skills, social withdrawal, irritability, avoidance or agitation (NG54 1.6.1). Review how any behaviour that challenges may be linked to a physical health problem (NG11 1.2.1). In adults with Down's syndrome, ask about changes in behaviour, loss of skills or more prompting that might suggest the need for a dementia assessment (NG54 1.5.4).

    4

    Antipsychotic and medication review (STOMP)

    REVIEW/LIMIT

    +

    For people taking antipsychotics without psychotic symptoms, consider reducing or stopping long-term prescriptions, review after any change, consider referral to a psychiatrist experienced in learning disabilities, and document yearly the reasons if continued (NG54 1.9.7). Antipsychotics for behaviour that challenges only alongside psychological or other interventions; review effectiveness after 3 to 4 weeks and stop if there is no response at 6 weeks (NG11 1.8). See NHS England STOMP and STAMP (NHS).

    1

    Possible HIV exposure: PEP without delay

    URGENT

    +

    Start HIV post-exposure prophylaxis as soon as possible after exposure, preferably within 24 hours; it should not be started beyond 72 hours (BASHH/BHIVA PEP guideline). Local (East Sussex Sexual Health, read 24 November 2026): the Specialist Sexual Health Service offers HIV PrEP, PEP, testing and treatment.

    2

    Which tests to take

    ASSESS

    +

    First void urine is the specimen of choice for chlamydia and gonorrhoea NAAT in anyone with a penile urethra; vaginal swabs are recommended for anyone with a vagina. Throat and rectal swabs are guided by the sexual history. Offer blood-borne virus testing (HIV and syphilis) (BASHH testing guidance 2023). Offer contacts of chlamydia the same screening tests (BASHH chlamydia 2026). Local: East Sussex residents can order an at-home STI test kit and find clinics through the East Sussex Sexual Health website.

    3

    Chlamydia: doxycycline first-line

    DRUG

    +

    Doxycycline 100 mg twice a day for 7 days is the sole first-line treatment unless contraindicated (for example in pregnancy); the alternative is azithromycin 1 g orally as a single dose followed by 500 mg daily for 2 days (BASHH 2026). Advise no sex, including oral sex, until treatment is completed (or 7 days after azithromycin) and symptoms have resolved. Retest 2 to 6 months after treatment. Arrange partner notification. Check the BNF and local sexual health service pathway.

    4

    Gonorrhoea: culture, ceftriaxone and test of cure

    REVIEW/LIMIT

    +

    Treatment for uncomplicated anogenital and pharyngeal gonorrhoea in adults is ceftriaxone 1 g intramuscularly as a single dose (BASHH 2025). Culture should be taken, but treatment should not be delayed and should be given immediately after culture (BASHH testing 2023). Pharyngeal testing is recommended for everyone with urogenital gonorrhoea and all contacts. Test of cure by NAAT at least two weeks after treatment; no sex until 7 days after the person and their partners have completed treatment; pursue partner notification (BASHH 2025). Follow the local sexual health referral pathway (BASHH 2025).
