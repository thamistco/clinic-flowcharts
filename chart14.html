    1

    Sight-threatening features: same-day emergency eye assessment

    URGENT

    +

    Red eye with any change in vision (wavy lines, flashing, loss of vision), photophobia, severe headache with nausea, a very dark red eye, an eye injury or foreign body, a chemical injury, or unequal pupils needs emergency assessment the same day (NHS red eye page). Same-day referral is advised for suspected gonococcal conjunctivitis (College of Optometrists CMG, January 2025). CKS (March 2026): refer for same-day ophthalmology assessment for moderate to severe pain or reduced visual acuity, suspected acute glaucoma or corneal ulcer, chemical injury (immediately irrigate with water or 0.9% saline until pH normalises; if pH cannot be checked, refer immediately) or trauma; treat a high-velocity injury or an injury involving glass as penetrating until proven otherwise.

    2

    Urgent appointment the same day

    URGENT

    +

    Arrange an urgent same-day GP or optometrist review for: a very painful red eye; a red eye in a contact lens wearer (possible eye infection); a baby under 30 days with red or sticky eyes (AI-reviewed decision, awaiting clinician sign-off: the wider 30-day cut-off from the NHS conjunctivitis page is used; the NHS red eye page says less than 28 days) (NHS pages).

    3

    Common causes and routine review

    ASSESS

    +

    Common causes include subconjunctival haemorrhage, conjunctivitis, dry eye, blepharitis and eyelash or eyelid problems. Routine GP or optometrist review for a child under 2 years with a red eye, a sticky discharge, or eyelid or eyelash problems lasting more than a few days. Refer to ophthalmology if no cause is found (NHS red eye page). CKS (March 2026): refer urgently (within 24 hours) to the local eye clinic for suspected anterior uveitis or scleritis; routinely for ectropion, entropion or trichiasis; seek ophthalmology advice if the diagnosis is unclear. Suspect child maltreatment if a child has retinal haemorrhages or an eye injury without a confirmed accidental cause or medical explanation.

    4

    Conjunctivitis: urgent ophthalmology referral

    URGENTREFER

    +

    Urgently refer conjunctivitis to ophthalmology for: a red flag for a serious cause of red eye; ophthalmia neonatorum (conjunctivitis within the first four weeks of life); suspected gonococcal or chlamydial conjunctivitis; possible herpes infection; suspected periorbital or orbital cellulitis; severe disease such as corneal ulceration, significant keratitis or a pseudomembrane; recent intraocular surgery; a severe systemic condition such as rheumatoid arthritis or immunocompromise; or corneal involvement with soft contact lens use (CKS infective, October 2022). For allergic conjunctivitis, red flags include pain, photophobia, change in visual acuity and pupillary abnormality; also refer urgently a neonate with a red sticky eye, a systemic disease with eye symptoms such as Sjögren's syndrome, or a contact lens wearer with suspected corneal involvement (CKS allergic, May 2022). Discuss with or refer to ophthalmology if the diagnosis is uncertain, equipment is lacking, or conjunctivitis is recurrent, persistent or due to molluscum contagiosum (CKS).

    5

    Conjunctivitis: self-care, antibiotics and safety-netting

    SUPPORT

    +

    Bacterial conjunctivitis often resolves in five to seven days without treatment; the benefit of antibiotics has been questioned (College of Optometrists CMG). Advise handwashing, not sharing towels or pillows, not rubbing the eyes, and no contact lenses until better. Staying off work or school is not needed unless very unwell. Review if not cleared within 7 days, or urgently if pain, photophobia or visual change develops (NHS pages). CKS (infective, October 2022): viral conjunctivitis usually resolves within 7 days and bacterial within 5 to 10 days (the College of Optometrists says five to seven days); viral and bacterial are hard to tell apart; swabs are not routine but may help if initial treatment fails. Self-care: bathe or clean the eyelids, cool compresses, lubricating drops or artificial tears, no contact lenses; infection control advice. Use topical antibiotics (such as chloramphenicol or fusidic acid) judiciously if bacterial infection is suspected; a delayed treatment strategy may be appropriate. BNF eye doses (child and adult, as listed on the BNF page): chloramphenicol eye drops 1 drop every 2 hours, reducing as infection is controlled and continuing for 48 hours after healing (3–4 times daily is generally enough for less severe infection); eye ointment once daily at night if drops are used during the day, or 3–4 times a day if used alone. MHRA (July 2021): chloramphenicol drops containing borax or boric acid can be used safely in children younger than 2 years as advised by a prescriber. Avoid in pregnancy and breastfeeding unless essential. Pharmacies can sell chloramphenicol for acute bacterial conjunctivitis in adults and children over 2 years, for up to 5 days. Fusidic acid eye drops (staphylococcal infection): apply twice daily.

    6

    Allergic conjunctivitis

    SUPPORT

    +

    Itching is the most consistent feature, with watery or mucoid discharge, redness and conjunctival or eyelid swelling. Exclude serious causes of red eye. Manage with patient information; avoiding allergens and eye rubbing; cold compresses and ocular lubricants; and topical antihistamines and/or mast cell stabilisers, with topical diclofenac as an adjunct if needed. Consult ophthalmology if atopic or vernal keratoconjunctivitis is suspected, or allergic conjunctivitis is severe or treatment-resistant (CKS allergic, May 2022). BNF: sodium cromoglicate eye drops 4 times a day (child and adult; 2% drops can be bought from pharmacies); olopatadine eye drops for seasonal allergic conjunctivitis twice daily for a maximum of 4 months (adults and children 3–17 years).

    1

    Unilateral or bloody symptoms: consider another cause

    URGENT

    +

    Unilateral nasal symptoms suggest a tumour, foreign body, nose picking or misapplied nasal spray. Bloody or purulent discharge with pain and blockage can occur with malignancy. Assess for these other causes rather than treating as allergic rhinitis (BSACI 2017, tables of causes and nasal discharge). CKS (January 2024): refer to an allergy or ENT specialist if there are red flag features suggesting an alternative or serious diagnosis.

    2

    Classify and assess

    ASSESS

    +

    Classify by severity (mild, or moderate to severe) and pattern: seasonal, perennial, occupational; intermittent (symptoms for less than four days a week, or less than four consecutive weeks) or persistent (more than four days a week and for more than four consecutive weeks). Suspect allergic rhinitis with classic symptoms after exposure to a known allergen, with possible allergic conjunctivitis, asthma or eczema. Assess type, frequency, timing, persistence and location of symptoms; severity and impact; housing, pets and occupation; drugs that may cause or relieve symptoms; family history of atopy; and examine (CKS, January 2024).

    3

    Mild or intermittent symptoms: antihistamine and saline

    SUPPORT

    +

    An oral or intranasal antihistamine is first-line for mild-to-moderate intermittent and mild persistent rhinitis; fexofenadine is the least sedating oral antihistamine. Isotonic saline irrigation is well tolerated and has a small benefit. Give allergen avoidance advice for pollen, house dust mite or pets as relevant (BSACI 2017). CKS (January 2024): for mild or intermittent symptoms, an as-needed intranasal antihistamine or a non-sedating oral antihistamine; saline nasal irrigation; and allergen avoidance if a causative allergen is identified. BNF oral antihistamines for hay fever (child doses as listed on the BNF pages): fexofenadine adults and 12–17 years 120 mg once daily, 6–11 years 30 mg twice daily; cetirizine adults and 12–17 years 10 mg once daily, 6–11 years 5 mg twice daily, 2–5 years 2.5 mg twice daily; loratadine adults and 12–17 years 10 mg once daily, 2–11 years 5 mg once daily (under 31 kg) or 10 mg once daily (31 kg and above). Drowsiness can occur and may affect driving or cycling. Most manufacturers advise avoiding antihistamines in pregnancy and breastfeeding, although there is no evidence of teratogenicity. Intranasal azelastine dose: BNF not yet supplied.

    4

    Moderate to severe persistent symptoms: intranasal corticosteroid

    DRUG

    +

    An intranasal corticosteroid is first-line for moderate-to-severe persistent symptoms; teach correct use of the device. If poorly controlled, an intranasal corticosteroid plus intranasal antihistamine combination is second-line. Use decongestants only short term (generally fewer than 10 days) because of rebound congestion, and avoid them in pregnancy. Oral steroid short courses are preferred to depot injections, which are not recommended (BSACI 2017). CKS (January 2024): use a regular intranasal corticosteroid during periods of allergen exposure for moderate to severe persistent symptoms, or if initial treatment is ineffective; review after 2–4 weeks if symptoms persist. Refractory symptoms: a combination intranasal antihistamine and corticosteroid spray; possible add-ons such as an intranasal decongestant, intranasal anticholinergic or leukotriene receptor antagonist; a short course of oral corticosteroid for severe, uncontrolled symptoms in adults that are significantly affecting quality of life. BNF doses. Mometasone nasal spray: adults and children 12–17 years 100 micrograms into each nostril once daily, increased if necessary to 200 micrograms once daily, reduced to 50 micrograms once daily when control is achieved; children 3–11 years 50 micrograms into each nostril once daily. Fluticasone propionate nasal spray: adults and children 12–17 years 100 micrograms into each nostril once daily, preferably in the morning, increased if necessary to twice daily, reduced to 50 micrograms once daily when controlled; children 4–11 years 50 micrograms into each nostril once daily, increased if necessary to twice daily. Avamys® (fluticasone furoate): adults and children 12–17 years 55 micrograms (2 sprays) into each nostril once daily, reduced to 27.5 micrograms once daily; children 6–11 years 27.5 micrograms, increased if necessary to 55 micrograms, using the minimum effective dose. Fluticasone with azelastine (moderate to severe allergic rhinitis when an antihistamine or corticosteroid alone is inadequate): adults and children 12–17 years 1 spray into each nostril twice daily. Intranasal corticosteroids: avoid after nasal surgery until healed, in pulmonary tuberculosis and with untreated nasal infection; systemic absorption may follow high doses or prolonged treatment; monitor the height of children on prolonged treatment and consider paediatric referral if growth slows.

    5

    Treatment failure, asthma and immunotherapy

    REVIEW/LIMIT

    +

    If treatment fails, check use, concordance and dose. Poor control of rhinitis is a risk factor for asthma exacerbations, so ask about asthma. Consider referral for immunotherapy if symptoms are mainly due to one allergen (BSACI 2017 treatment algorithm). CKS (January 2024): refer to an allergy or ENT specialist if symptoms persist despite optimal primary care management; if house dust mite or animal dander avoidance is being considered (allergy testing may be needed); if the person would like immunotherapy instead of long-term medication; or if the diagnosis is uncertain.

    1

    Airway swelling or breathing difficulty: 999

    URGENT

    +

    Call 999 for sudden swelling of the lips, mouth, throat or tongue, difficulty breathing or swallowing, cyanosis or pallor, sudden confusion or drowsiness, or collapse. These can be signs of anaphylaxis (NHS hives page).

    2

    Classify and look for causes

    ASSESS

    +

    Acute urticaria lasts less than 6 weeks; chronic spontaneous urticaria lasts more than 6 weeks with no obvious trigger (PCDS). Chronic spontaneous urticaria is a clinical diagnosis and blood tests are not routinely required (PCDS). Wheals lasting more than 24 hours, burning, painful or leaving bruising suggest urticarial vasculitis. Check prescribed and over-the-counter medicines: NSAIDs and opiates aggravate urticaria, and ACE inhibitors can cause angioedema without urticaria. Suspect hereditary angioedema in isolated angioedema under 20 years and check complement C4 (PCDS). CKS (January 2026): acute urticaria lasts less than 6 weeks and is likely to be self-limiting; chronic urticaria persists for 6 weeks or longer, on a nearly daily basis. Investigations are not usually required, but may help identify associated conditions or triggers or exclude other diagnoses; identify and manage the underlying cause where possible.

    3

    Second-generation antihistamine, stepped up

    DRUG

    +

    Start a second-generation H1-antihistamine at standard dose once daily. If the response is inadequate within 2 to 4 weeks, increase to twice daily, then up to 4 times the usual dose (for example fexofenadine 180mg QDS; off-licence but supported by national guidelines). Fexofenadine and cetirizine are renally excreted; loratadine and desloratadine are hepatically metabolised. Do not use first-generation antihistamines regularly; H2 antagonists have no role. Stop an ACE inhibitor in angioedema and avoid other ACE inhibitors (PCDS). CKS (January 2026): offer a non-sedating antihistamine daily for up to 6 weeks; for severe symptoms also offer a short course (up to 7 days) of an oral corticosteroid. If symptoms improve, consider daily antihistamine for 3–6 months if persistence or recurrence is likely, or as required or prophylactically for infrequent symptoms. If the response is inadequate: gradually increase to up to four times the licensed dose (off-label), try an alternative non-sedating antihistamine, add a topical antipruritic such as calamine lotion, or add a sedating antihistamine such as chlorphenamine at night if itch is interfering with sleep. Source difference (shown, not resolved): PCDS advises against regular first-generation antihistamines and says they have no role in chronic spontaneous urticaria, whereas CKS allows a sedating antihistamine at night for itch that disturbs sleep. BNF licensed doses: cetirizine 10 mg once daily (adults and 12–17 years; 5 mg twice daily at 6–11 years; 2.5 mg twice daily at 2–5 years); loratadine 10 mg once daily (adults and 12–17 years); fexofenadine for chronic idiopathic urticaria 180 mg once daily (adults and 12–17 years). Cetirizine: caution in epilepsy; avoid if eGFR is less than 10 mL/minute/1.73 m², half dose if eGFR is 30–50 mL/minute/1.73 m², and half dose on alternate days if eGFR is 10–30 mL/minute/1.73 m²; new-onset pruritus has been reported after stopping long-term treatment. Loratadine: in severe hepatic impairment, start on alternate days. Chlorphenamine: adults and 12–17 years 4 mg every 4–6 hours (older people, consider a maximum of 12 mg per day); 6–11 years 2 mg every 4–6 hours; 2–5 years 1 mg every 4–6 hours; 1–23 months 1 mg twice daily; caution with epilepsy, prostatic hypertrophy, urinary retention and susceptibility to angle-closure glaucoma; drowsiness may affect driving and is enhanced by alcohol; use late in the third trimester may affect the newborn. Calamine: BNF not yet supplied.

    4

    Continue, then refer if not controlled

    REVIEW/LIMITREFER

    +

    Continue at the lowest effective dose until there has been no activity for 12 weeks, restarting if urticaria recurs. If symptoms do not respond to the maximum antihistamine dose, refer to secondary care, check total IgE and document a UAS7 score in the letter; a leukotriene receptor antagonist trial (stopped if no response within four weeks) may be considered first (PCDS). Refer suspected C1 esterase inhibitor deficiency to immunology or allergy (PCDS). CKS (January 2026): also refer to a dermatologist or immunologist if vasculitic urticaria is suspected (for example painful and persistent urticaria), or if there is a food or latex allergy or a form of inducible urticaria that is hard to manage in primary care (for example solar or cold urticaria); refer to a clinical psychologist if symptoms cause significant social or psychological problems.

    1

    Pigmented nail or suspected kerion

    URGENT

    +

    A brown-black nail must be distinguished from melanoma: fungal discolouration usually starts at the distal nail, whereas melanoma usually shows a line growing continuously from the proximal nail (PCDS). Suspected kerion: urgent dermatology referral (CKS scalp, February 2025).

    2

    Recognise the site and sample when needed

    ASSESS

    +

    Body or groin: erythematous lesions with an annular border, scale most marked at the leading edge; topical steroids can mask tinea (tinea incognito) (PCDS). Foot: common in adolescents, rare before puberty. Sample skin for microscopy and culture if disease is severe or extensive in adults, or the diagnosis is uncertain (CKS body/groin and foot). Nails: suspect if the nail is discoloured, with white or yellow streaks, thickening, spots or destruction; paronychia may suggest Candida; send clippings or scrapings to confirm before antifungal treatment (CKS nail). Always send samples before long-term nail treatment: only 45% of dermatology samples are positive for fungi (UKHSA). Scalp: always send skin and hair samples (CKS scalp). Examine other sites: feet, hands, nails and groin.

    3

    Skin (body, groin, foot): topical treatment first

    SUPPORT

    +

    Mild, non-extensive disease in children and adults: a topical antifungal such as terbinafine or an imidazole, or for the foot undecenoic acid or tolnaftate (CKS). BNF terbinafine cream (adult): tinea pedis "Apply 1–2 times a day for up to 1 week, to be applied thinly"; tinea corporis and cruris "Apply 1–2 times a day for up to 1–2 weeks, to be applied thinly, review treatment after 2 weeks". PCDS: an imidazole such as miconazole twice daily for 2 to 4 weeks. Add a short-term mildly potent topical corticosteroid such as hydrocortisone if inflammation is marked (CKS). Treat soles, heels and interdigital spaces and advise breathable footwear (PCDS); manage concomitant nail, hand or groin infection (CKS). If infection persists: check adherence and reinfection from close contacts, sample, and consider oral treatment (CKS).

    4

    Skin: oral treatment for severe or extensive disease (adults)

    DRUG

    +

    Consider oral terbinafine first-line, guided by microscopy or culture; oral itraconazole or griseofulvin if terbinafine is not tolerated or contraindicated (CKS). BNF adult doses: terbinafine tablets – tinea pedis "250 mg once daily for 2–6 weeks"; tinea corporis "250 mg once daily for 4 weeks"; tinea cruris "250 mg once daily for 2–4 weeks". Itraconazole – tinea pedis or manuum "100 mg once daily for 30 days, alternatively 200 mg twice daily for 7 days"; tinea corporis or cruris "100 mg once daily for 15 days, alternatively 200 mg once daily for 7 days". Griseofulvin "500 mg daily, increased if necessary to 1 g daily, for severe infections; reduce dose when response occurs". See the safety step before prescribing.

    5

    Nails: self-care, topical or oral treatment

    DRUG

    +

    Advise keeping nails short, non-occlusive footwear and treating tinea pedis. Topical amorolfine lacquer only for early, limited or superficial dermatophyte or Candida infection (CKS nail, August 2023). Oral, once confirmed: terbinafine first-line for dermatophyte infection – BNF "250 mg once daily for 6 weeks–3 months (occasionally longer in toenail infections)"; itraconazole or fluconazole first-line for Candida or non-dermatophyte infection (off-label) (CKS) – BNF itraconazole onychomycosis "200 mg once daily for 3 months, alternatively 200 mg twice daily for 7 days, subsequent courses repeated after 21-day intervals; fingernails 2 courses, toenails 3 courses". BNF fluconazole for fungal nail infection (adults): 150 mg once weekly until the infected nail is replaced. Contraindicated in acute porphyrias; caution with susceptibility to QT prolongation; avoid in pregnancy unless the potential benefit outweighs the risk, and consider effective contraception during and for 1 week after longer courses; monitor liver function with high doses or extended courses; usual first dose, then halve subsequent doses if creatinine clearance is less than 50 mL/minute. Assess nail regrowth 3–6 months after starting. If treatment fails: manage the cause, re-sample, consider topical plus oral treatment. Consider podiatry for painful thickened toenails, footwear trauma or toenails damaging adjacent toes (CKS).

    6

    Scalp (tinea capitis): sample, oral treatment, review

    DRUG

    +

    Self-care: remove surface crusts, discard or disinfect objects that can spread infection, and check family members and pets. Adults: start oral treatment before or after culture results by clinical judgement. Children: start only with a confirmed diagnosis and enough prescribing experience in primary care; otherwise seek specialist advice first. Empirical choice: in urban areas, terbinafine (off-label), continued if Trichophyton tonsurans; in rural areas, griseofulvin (licensed), continued if Microsporum, with itraconazole (off-label) if griseofulvin is unsuitable. Co-prescribe a topical antifungal to reduce transmission. Review 4–8 weeks after the course; if it is improving while mycology remains positive, continue for a further 2–4 weeks. Sample known contacts (CKS scalp, February 2025). BNF adult griseofulvin for tinea capitis caused by T. tonsurans: "1 g once daily, alternatively 1 g daily in divided doses". BNFC tinea capitis in children: terbinafine (not licensed in children) by body weight, 10–19 kg 62.5 mg, 20–39 kg 125 mg, 40 kg and above 250 mg, once daily for 4 weeks; check liver function before treatment and after 4–6 weeks. Griseofulvin for T. tonsurans: 1 month–11 years 15–20 mg/kg once daily or in divided doses (maximum 1 g per dose); 12–17 years 1 g daily; for other dermatophyte infections, 1 month–11 years usually 10 mg/kg daily (maximum 500 mg per dose), increased if necessary to 20 mg/kg daily for severe infections. Itraconazole children's dose: BNFC not yet supplied.

    7

    Oral antifungal safety (BNF)

    REVIEW/LIMIT

    +

    Terbinafine (oral): monitor hepatic function before treatment and then periodically after 4–6 weeks, and stop if liver tests are abnormal; tell patients to report signs of liver dysfunction, such as pruritus, persistent nausea, jaundice, dark urine or pale stools; stop for a progressive skin rash (including Stevens-Johnson syndrome); caution in psoriasis and lupus; avoid in hepatic impairment; caution if creatinine clearance is less than 50 mL/minute; avoid in breastfeeding. Itraconazole: avoid in ventricular dysfunction, such as a history of congestive heart failure, unless the infection is serious; stop if signs of hepatitis; monitor liver function if treatment continues for longer than one month; ensure effective contraception until the next menstrual period after treatment; pregnancy only in life-threatening situations; oral solution and capsules are not interchangeable. Griseofulvin: contraindicated in systemic lupus erythematosus and acute porphyrias; effective contraception during and for at least 1 month after (it may reduce oral contraceptive effectiveness, so add a barrier method); men should avoid fathering a child for at least 6 months after; avoid in pregnancy; may impair driving (BNF).

    8

    When to refer to dermatology

    REVIEW/LIMITREFER

    +

    Refer, with urgency by clinical judgement, if disease is severe or extensive or topical treatment fails in a child; the diagnosis is uncertain; primary care treatment fails or recurrences are frequent; or the person is immunocompromised (CKS body/groin and foot). Nails: also if oral treatment is being considered for a child, or there is co-existent nail disease such as psoriasis or lichen planus (CKS nail). Scalp: suspected kerion (urgent); a child needs oral treatment and there is not enough experience in primary care; or a suspected complication is not responding (CKS scalp).

    1

    Priapism: admit

    URGENT

    +

    Arrange emergency hospital admission for priapism: a painful prolonged erection for more than 4 hours (CKS, July 2026). Warn patients started on treatment to seek advice if an erection lasts longer than 4 hours (BSW ICB guidance).

    2

    Assess cause, cardiovascular risk and blood tests

    ASSESS

    +

    Assess with the partner if appropriate. Organic cause: gradual onset, lack of tumescence, low-to-normal libido; psychogenic cause: sudden onset, low libido, good spontaneous or self-stimulated erections; consider the IIEF-5 (SHIM) questionnaire. Ask about cardiovascular disease, hypertension, heart failure, peripheral arterial disease or stroke; previous episodes and treatments, including over-the-counter and online; smoking, alcohol, prescribed or recreational drugs, cycling more than 3 hours a week, and exercise tolerance. Examine: blood pressure, heart rate, waist circumference and BMI; gynaecomastia, sparse body hair and reduced muscle mass (testosterone deficiency); consider genital examination, and DRE if obstructive LUTS, a history of prostate cancer or age over 50. Blood tests for all men: HbA1c or fasting glucose; lipid profile with 10-year cardiovascular risk; fasting total testosterone taken between 9–11 am, repeated with FSH, LH, SHBG and prolactin if low or borderline (CKS assessment, July 2026). Source note: regional BSW guidance says fasting 8 to 10 am testosterone with SHBG and albumin. Consider PSA, thyroid, liver and renal function by clinical judgement (CKS). Screen people with type 2 diabetes every year (BSW). If a drug cause is suspected, stop it only if onset matched starting the drug (CKS).

    3

    Cardiac risk of sexual activity

    ASSESS

    +

    Low risk: asymptomatic and able to do modest exercise with fewer than 3 coronary risk factors; mild stable angina; uncomplicated previous MI; successful revascularisation; NYHA class I–II heart failure; controlled hypertension; mild valve disease. Intermediate risk: 3 or more coronary risk factors; moderate stable angina; MI within the last 2–6 weeks; NYHA class III; peripheral arterial disease or stroke disease. High risk: unstable or refractory angina; MI within the last 2 weeks; NYHA class IV; uncontrolled hypertension; high-risk arrhythmia; hypertrophic obstructive or other cardiomyopathy; moderate-to-severe valve disease (CKS, July 2026). High (or intermediate, by judgement) risk: advise stopping sexual activity and seek cardiology advice before treatment. Source difference: CKS uses MI within 2 weeks for high risk, but for prescribing the BNF contraindications apply – tadalafil: acute myocardial infarction in the past 90 days; sildenafil: recent history of myocardial infarction.

    4

    Lifestyle, information and underlying conditions

    SUPPORT

    +

    Advise weight loss and regular exercise, smoking cessation and alcohol reduction where indicated, and stopping cycling for a trial period if cycling more than 3 hours a week. Optimise diabetes, hypertension and dyslipidaemia. Do not recommend unlicensed herbal or complementary medicines. Offer information, such as the NHS and BAUS pages (CKS).

    5

    PDE-5 inhibitor: choice and BNF doses

    DRUG

    +

    For men not at high cardiac risk, consider a PDE-5 inhibitor irrespective of the cause; options are sildenafil, tadalafil, vardenafil and avanafil, and sildenafil 50 mg (Viagra Connect) can be bought from pharmacies (CKS). Sildenafil (BNF): "Initially 50 mg, to be taken approximately 1 hour before sexual activity, adjusted according to response to 25–100 mg (max. per dose 100 mg) as required, to be taken as a single dose; maximum 1 dose per day"; start at 25 mg with moderate or potent CYP3A4 inhibitors, or if creatinine clearance is less than 30 mL/minute. Tadalafil (BNF): "Initially 10 mg (max. per dose 20 mg), to be taken at least 30 minutes before sexual activity", maximum 1 dose per day; for sexual activity at least twice a week, "5 mg once daily, reduced to 2.5 mg once daily, adjusted according to response" (not on the Sussex formulary; see the next step); in severe renal impairment avoid once-daily dosing and use a maximum of 10 mg intermittently.

    6

    Local choice, SLS and contraindications

    DRUG

    +

    Local choice (Sussex Formulary, read 24 November 2026; AI-reviewed decision, awaiting clinician sign-off): generic sildenafil first-line – generic sildenafil is no longer on the SLS restrictions list, so no SLS endorsement is needed. Tadalafil 10 mg on demand is the formulary tadalafil option (the formulary lists tadalafil 10 mg tablets). Tadalafil 5 mg once daily, which the BNF gives for men who anticipate sexual activity at least twice a week, is not listed on the Sussex formulary: use it only as a non-formulary option or on specialist advice. Note only: regional BSW guidance (not Sussex) prefers tadalafil 5 mg daily or 10 mg on demand first-line. Branded Viagra and Cialis and vardenafil remain SLS (Sussex). Source note: CKS (July 2026) says to endorse SLS when prescribing "tadalafil (Cialis®)" and that generic sildenafil needs no SLS endorsement; the BNF restricts the Cialis brand; Sussex says generic tadalafil is no longer on the SLS list; check the current Drug Tariff Part XVIIIB. BNF contraindications: tadalafil – acute myocardial infarction in the past 90 days, history of non-arteritic anterior ischaemic optic neuropathy, hypotension (avoid if systolic below 90 mmHg), heart failure, recent stroke, uncontrolled arrhythmias, uncontrolled hypertension, unstable angina; sildenafil – hereditary degenerative retinal disorders, non-arteritic anterior ischaemic optic neuropathy, recent myocardial infarction or stroke, systolic below 90 mmHg, recent unstable angina. Nitrates (including nicorandil and recreational amyl nitrate) are absolutely contraindicated (BSW). Sussex Formulary status and line preference (AI-reviewed decision, awaiting clinician sign-off; read from the formulary page text on 24 November 2026; check current formulary): sildenafil: Mixed Status – see individual drug entries (no line preference shown); tadalafil: Non-Specialist Drugs (no line preference shown); vardenafil: Specialist recommendation (or initiation where noted) (no line preference shown); alprostadil: Mixed Status – see individual drug entries (no line preference shown).

    7

    Review at 6 to 8 weeks; dose and drug changes

    REVIEW/LIMIT

    +

    Follow up 6 to 8 weeks after starting treatment for efficacy, tolerability and satisfaction (CKS). Check it is taken correctly and consider increasing to the maximum dose. CKS: try each PDE-5 inhibitor 4 to 8 times at the maximum tolerated dose before switching; the BNF says six doses at the maximum dose (with sexual stimulation) before classing someone as a non-responder (source difference shown, not resolved); in Sussex, alprostadil cream is only prescribed after 8 doses of at least 2 oral PDE-5 inhibitors and must meet SLS criteria. Try at least 2 PDE-5 inhibitors before classing someone as a non-responder; consider once-daily tadalafil. Hypogonadism can reduce the response. If treatment is ineffective, not tolerated or contraindicated, offer urology referral; alprostadil is second-line under careful medical supervision (BNF).

    8

    When to refer

    ASSESSREFER

    +

    Urology: young age or lifelong difficulty, pelvic, perineal or genital trauma, penile structural abnormality or abnormal testicular examination (including Peyronie's disease), or no response to at least 2 PDE-5 inhibitors. Endocrinology: suspected hypogonadism, abnormal testosterone, FSH, LH or prolactin, or if testosterone replacement is being considered. Cardiology or specialist advice: high (or, by clinical judgement, intermediate) cardiac risk, with advice to stop all sexual activity until assessed. Mental health services, psychosexual or relationship counselling: suspected psychogenic or relationship cause, or a complex or severe mental health condition (CKS, July 2026).

    1

    Red flags: admit or refer urgently

    URGENTREFER

    +

    Suspected central cause (stroke, TIA, cerebellar tumour, multiple sclerosis): admit or urgently refer (CKS). Always consider stroke with new unilateral hearing loss and vertigo. Red flags needing urgent brain imaging: isolated, persistent (more than 24 hours) vertigo of hyperacute (seconds) onset; a normal head impulse test; new headache; new unilateral deafness; cranial nerve or limb weakness or sensory loss; severe ataxia (CKS central vertigo). Also admit or refer urgently for severe nausea and vomiting with inability to tolerate oral fluids or symptomatic treatment; very sudden (within seconds), persistent vertigo not provoked by position; central signs such as new (especially occipital) headache, gait disturbance, truncal ataxia or vertical nystagmus; or acute deafness without other features of Ménière's disease (CKS peripheral vertigo). SWL ICB guidance: chest pain or breathlessness to the emergency department; abnormal ECG, family history of sudden death, known coronary disease, blackouts or dizziness on exercise to urgent cardiology; sudden deafness to urgent ENT.

    2

    History and focused examination

    ASSESS

    +

    History: type of dizziness (rotational vertigo, unsteadiness, light-headedness), single or recurrent episodes, duration, positional triggers, and a medication review (SWL). Examination: postural blood pressure, HINTS plus (head impulse, nystagmus type, test of skew, hearing), cranial nerves, cerebellar signs and gait (SWL); CKS also lists facial asymmetry, ear and eye examination, signs of peripheral neuropathy, and Romberg's, Unterberger's and alternate cover tests. Check blood glucose (SWL). Suspect a central cause when features do not match a peripheral cause, for example prolonged severe vertigo, new headache or recent trauma, focal signs, central-type nystagmus, an abnormal Dix-Hallpike response, or inability to stand or walk with eyes open (CKS).

    3

    BPPV: diagnose, reposition, review at 4 weeks

    SUPPORT

    +

    Diagnose from vertigo brought on by specific head movements (turning over in bed, looking up, bending over) and the Dix-Hallpike manoeuvre if appropriate; consider Ménière's disease, vestibular neuronitis and anxiety; imaging is not needed unless another condition must be excluded, such as atypical nystagmus or other neurological symptoms. Discuss watchful waiting; if treatment is preferred, offer a particle repositioning manoeuvre such as the Epley manoeuvre and consider Brandt-Daroff exercises. Symptomatic drug treatment is not usually helpful for BPPV. Review in 4 weeks if not resolved. Refer to a balance specialist if repositioning expertise is not available, physical limitations make it unsafe, symptoms persist after repeated repositioning, features are atypical, or symptoms have not resolved in 4 weeks (CKS BPPV, November 2025). SWL ICB guidance refers to vestibular physiotherapy if not improved.

    4

    Other peripheral causes and vertigo of uncertain cause

    SUPPORT

    +

    Vestibular neuritis (prolonged vertigo, positive head impulse, unidirectional nystagmus): refer to vestibular rehabilitation if not improved in two to three weeks; labyrinthitis (with hearing loss): refer as per local pathway; postural drop of more than 20 mmHg: review medicines and lifestyle, cardiology if no improvement (SWL). All other vertigo of undetermined cause: refer to a balance specialist (ENT, audiovestibular physician, neurologist or care of the elderly physician with a special interest), with urgency by clinical judgement (CKS).

    5

    Short-term symptom relief: BNF doses

    DRUG

    +

    While awaiting referral, consider prochlorperazine, or the antihistamines cinnarizine, cyclizine or promethazine teoclate, for no longer than 1 week; prolonged use may delay vestibular compensation; benzodiazepines are not recommended. For severe nausea or vomiting, consider buccal prochlorperazine or deep intramuscular prochlorperazine or cyclizine (CKS). Do not let treatment delay referral of a suspected central cause. BNF doses (adult): prochlorperazine acute attack, by mouth "Initially 20 mg for 1 dose, then 10 mg for 1 dose, to be given 2 hours after first dose"; by deep intramuscular injection "12.5 mg as required, to be followed if necessary after 6 hours by an oral dose"; labyrinthine disorders, by mouth "5 mg 3 times a day, dose can be increased gradually to 30 mg daily in divided doses if necessary"; buccal "3–6 mg twice daily" (the BNF lists this dose for nausea and vomiting in previously diagnosed migraine). Cinnarizine "30 mg 3 times a day". Cyclizine, by mouth, intravenous or intramuscular injection, "50 mg up to 3 times a day". Promethazine teoclate "25–75 mg daily; maximum 100 mg per day". BNF cautions: prochlorperazine – avoid in hepatic impairment; caution in the elderly, hypotension, QT prolongation and parkinsonism; acute dystonias can occur. All four can cause drowsiness that affects driving (BNF).

    6

    Recurrent episodes and deterioration

    REVIEW/LIMIT

    +

    Vestibular migraine: manage as migraine (SWL); people with known migrainous vertigo can be managed at home, but admit or refer suspected migrainous vertigo for investigation (CKS). Possible Ménière's disease (recurrent severe vertigo, roaring tinnitus, fluctuating low-frequency hearing loss, aural pressure): low salt diet, less caffeine and alcohol, stay mobile; refer to audiology if more than one episode (SWL). If symptoms deteriorate before specialist review, seek specialist advice (CKS).

    1

    Cancer red flags with fatigue

    URGENT

    +

    Consider a very urgent full blood count to assess for leukaemia in adults with pallor, persistent fatigue, unexplained fever, unexplained persistent or recurrent infection, generalised lymphadenopathy, or unexplained bruising or bleeding (NG12 1.10.1). Offer an urgent chest X-ray to people aged 40 and over with 2 or more unexplained symptoms, or who have ever smoked with 1 or more, from: cough, fatigue, shortness of breath, chest pain, weight loss, appetite loss (NG12 1.1.2).

    2

    Assessment and tests to exclude other causes

    ASSESS

    +

    Carry out a medical assessment (symptoms, history, comorbidities, physical and mental health), physical examination and assessment of psychological and social impact. Tests include urinalysis for protein, blood and glucose, full blood count, urea and electrolytes, liver function, thyroid function, ESR or plasma viscosity, CRP, calcium and phosphate, HbA1c, serum ferritin, coeliac screening and creatine kinase, with others by clinical judgement (NG206 1.2.3). CKS (June 2025): ask about onset, severity, frequency, duration, fluctuations, triggers and the effect of rest, sleep and activity; features of a physical or mental health condition or stress; impact and support; diet, smoking, alcohol, recreational drug, social, sexual and travel history; and medicines. Arrange blood tests, a urine pregnancy test and urinalysis depending on clinical judgement.

    3

    Fatigue without ME/CFS features: primary care management

    SUPPORT

    +

    Manage any underlying or coexisting condition and refer if indicated; give information and sources of support; advise on sleep hygiene, healthy eating, exercise and activity, healthy working and psychosocial support; offer referral for cognitive behavioural therapy, stress management or relaxation therapies by clinical judgement; review symptom response and arrange further tests or referral if needed (CKS, June 2025). If ME/CFS is suspected, follow the next step instead.

    4

    When to suspect ME/CFS and early advice

    SUPPORT

    +

    Suspect ME/CFS if debilitating fatigue worsened by activity, post-exertional malaise, unrefreshing sleep and cognitive difficulties have persisted for at least 6 weeks in adults (4 weeks in children and young people), with significantly reduced activity and no other explanation (NG206 1.2.2). Advise not to push through symptoms, to rest as needed, and to keep a balanced diet with adequate fluid (1.3.1). Explain that diagnosis can only be confirmed after 3 months (1.3.2). Refer children and young people with suspected ME/CFS to a paediatrician (1.2.7).

    5

    Diagnosis at 3 months and referral

    REVIEW/LIMITREFER

    +

    Diagnose ME/CFS when the symptoms have persisted for 3 months and are not explained by another condition (NG206 1.4.1), and refer adults directly to an ME/CFS specialist team (1.4.3). Do not offer graded exercise therapy or any exercise programme as a cure (1.11.14).
