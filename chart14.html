    1

    Sight-threatening features: same-day emergency eye assessment

    URGENT

    +

    Red eye with any change in vision (wavy lines, flashing, loss of vision), photophobia, severe headache with nausea, a very dark red eye, an eye injury or foreign body, a chemical injury, or unequal pupils needs emergency assessment the same day (NHS red eye page). Same-day referral is advised for suspected gonococcal conjunctivitis (College of Optometrists CMG, January 2025). CKS (March 2026): refer for same-day ophthalmology assessment for moderate to severe pain or reduced visual acuity, suspected acute glaucoma or corneal ulcer, chemical injury (immediately irrigate with water or 0.9% saline until pH normalises; if pH cannot be checked, refer immediately) or trauma; treat a high-velocity injury or an injury involving glass as penetrating until proven otherwise.

    2

    Urgent appointment the same day

    URGENT

    +

    Arrange an urgent same-day GP or optometrist review for: a very painful red eye; a red eye in a contact lens wearer (possible eye infection); a baby under 28 days with red eyes. The NHS conjunctivitis page uses less than 30 days for a baby with red, sticky eyes (NHS pages).

    3

    Common causes and routine review

    ASSESS

    +

    Common causes include subconjunctival haemorrhage, conjunctivitis, dry eye, blepharitis and eyelash or eyelid problems. Routine GP or optometrist review for a child under 2 years with a red eye, a sticky discharge, or eyelid or eyelash problems lasting more than a few days. Refer to ophthalmology if no cause is found (NHS red eye page). CKS (March 2026): refer urgently (within 24 hours) to the local eye clinic for suspected anterior uveitis or scleritis; routinely for ectropion, entropion or trichiasis; seek ophthalmology advice if the diagnosis is unclear. Suspect child maltreatment if a child has retinal haemorrhages or an eye injury without a confirmed accidental cause or medical explanation.

    4

    Conjunctivitis: self-care, antibiotics and safety-netting

    SUPPORT

    +

    Bacterial conjunctivitis often resolves in five to seven days without treatment; the benefit of antibiotics has been questioned (College of Optometrists CMG). Advise handwashing, not sharing towels or pillows, not rubbing the eyes, and no contact lenses until better. Staying off work or school is not needed unless very unwell. Review if not cleared within 7 days, or urgently if pain, photophobia or visual change develops (NHS pages).

    1

    Unilateral or bloody symptoms: consider another cause

    URGENT

    +

    Unilateral nasal symptoms suggest a tumour, foreign body, nose picking or misapplied nasal spray. Bloody or purulent discharge with pain and blockage can occur with malignancy. Assess for these other causes rather than treating as allergic rhinitis (BSACI 2017, tables of causes and nasal discharge).

    2

    Mild or intermittent symptoms: antihistamine and saline

    SUPPORT

    +

    An oral or intranasal antihistamine is first-line for mild-to-moderate intermittent and mild persistent rhinitis; fexofenadine is the least sedating oral antihistamine. Isotonic saline irrigation is well tolerated and has a small benefit. Give allergen avoidance advice for pollen, house dust mite or pets as relevant (BSACI 2017).

    3

    Moderate to severe persistent symptoms: intranasal corticosteroid

    DRUG

    +

    An intranasal corticosteroid is first-line for moderate-to-severe persistent symptoms; teach correct use of the device. If poorly controlled, an intranasal corticosteroid plus intranasal antihistamine combination is second-line. Use decongestants only short term (generally fewer than 10 days) because of rebound congestion, and avoid them in pregnancy. Oral steroid short courses are preferred to depot injections, which are not recommended (BSACI 2017). Check doses in the BNF.

    4

    Treatment failure, asthma and immunotherapy

    REVIEW/LIMIT

    +

    If treatment fails, check use, concordance and dose. Poor control of rhinitis is a risk factor for asthma exacerbations, so ask about asthma. Consider referral for immunotherapy if symptoms are mainly due to one allergen (BSACI 2017 treatment algorithm).

    1

    Airway swelling or breathing difficulty: 999

    URGENT

    +

    Call 999 for sudden swelling of the lips, mouth, throat or tongue, difficulty breathing or swallowing, cyanosis or pallor, sudden confusion or drowsiness, or collapse. These can be signs of anaphylaxis (NHS hives page).

    2

    Classify and look for causes

    ASSESS

    +

    Acute urticaria lasts less than 6 weeks; chronic spontaneous urticaria lasts more than 6 weeks with no obvious trigger (PCDS). Chronic spontaneous urticaria is a clinical diagnosis and blood tests are not routinely required (PCDS). Wheals lasting more than 24 hours, burning, painful or leaving bruising suggest urticarial vasculitis. Check prescribed and over-the-counter medicines: NSAIDs and opiates aggravate urticaria, and ACE inhibitors can cause angioedema without urticaria. Suspect hereditary angioedema in isolated angioedema under 20 years and check complement C4 (PCDS).

    3

    Second-generation antihistamine, stepped up

    DRUG

    +

    Start a second-generation H1-antihistamine at standard dose once daily. If the response is inadequate within 2 to 4 weeks, increase to twice daily, then up to 4 times the usual dose (for example fexofenadine 180mg QDS; off-licence but supported by national guidelines). Fexofenadine and cetirizine are renally excreted; loratadine and desloratadine are hepatically metabolised. Do not use first-generation antihistamines regularly; H2 antagonists have no role. Stop an ACE inhibitor in angioedema and avoid other ACE inhibitors (PCDS).

    4

    Continue, then refer if not controlled

    REVIEW/LIMITREFER

    +

    Continue at the lowest effective dose until there has been no activity for 12 weeks, restarting if urticaria recurs. If symptoms do not respond to the maximum antihistamine dose, refer to secondary care, check total IgE and document a UAS7 score in the letter; a leukotriene receptor antagonist trial (stopped if no response within four weeks) may be considered first (PCDS). Refer suspected C1 esterase inhibitor deficiency to immunology or allergy (PCDS).

    Fungal skin and nail infection (tinea): diagnosis, samples and treatment

    1

    Pigmented nail: exclude melanoma

    URGENT

    +

    A brown-black nail must be distinguished from melanoma. Fungal discolouration usually starts at the distal nail; melanoma usually shows a line growing continuously from the proximal nail (PCDS).

    2

    Recognise tinea and decide on samples

    ASSESS

    +

    Tinea corporis or cruris: erythematous lesions with an annular border, with scale most marked at the leading edge; examine and treat the feet if involved. Topical steroids can mask tinea (tinea incognito) (PCDS). Samples are not needed for uncomplicated athlete's foot, mild groin infection or mild skin ringworm. Take scrapings from the advancing edge when oral treatment is being considered (scalp or nail), in severe or extensive infection, if refractory to treatment, or if the diagnosis is uncertain (UKHSA). Always send samples before long-term nail treatment: only 45% of dermatology samples are positive for fungi (UKHSA). CKS (July 2023): arrange skin sampling for fungal microscopy and culture if there is severe or extensive disease in adults, or the diagnosis is uncertain.

    3

    Skin: topical antifungal first

    SUPPORT

    +

    Milder tinea corporis or cruris: terbinafine cream twice daily for 2 weeks, or an imidazole cream such as miconazole twice daily for 2 to 4 weeks; very extensive or inflammatory (pustular) rash: systemic terbinafine for two to four weeks (PCDS). Tinea pedis: treat the soles, heels and interdigital spaces; advise breathable footwear, and prophylactic topical antifungal once or twice a week can help recurrence (PCDS). Check doses and interactions in the BNF. CKS (July 2023): a topical antifungal such as terbinafine or an imidazole for mild, non-extensive disease in children and adults, adding a short-term mildly potent topical corticosteroid such as hydrocortisone if there is marked inflammation; consider oral terbinafine first-line for severe or extensive disease in adults, guided by microscopy or culture, with oral itraconazole or griseofulvin if terbinafine is not tolerated or contraindicated; treat any fungal nail, foot or hand infection too. If infection persists after topical treatment, look for non-adherence, reinfection from close contacts or tinea incognito, send samples, and consider oral treatment.

    4

    Nails and scalp: confirm, then oral treatment

    DRUG

    +

    Nails: topical treatment (for example amorolfine lacquer) has a low cure rate and suits only distal or superficial white infection. Oral terbinafine 250 mg once daily for 6 weeks (fingernails) or 3 to 4 months (toenails) is the most effective adult treatment; itraconazole pulse therapy is an alternative (PCDS). Scalp (tinea capitis, mainly children): send skin scrapings and plucked hairs, treat systemically because of the risk of scarring alopecia, use ketoconazole shampoo for the family, and children need not stay off school (PCDS).

    5

    When to refer to dermatology

    REVIEW/LIMITREFER

    +

    Refer to dermatology, with urgency by clinical judgement, if there is severe or extensive disease or topical treatment fails in a child; the diagnosis is uncertain; treatment in primary care fails or recurrences are frequent; or the person is immunocompromised (CKS, July 2023).

    1

    Priapism: admit

    URGENT

    +

    Arrange emergency hospital admission for priapism: a painful prolonged erection for more than 4 hours (CKS, July 2026). Warn patients started on treatment to seek advice if an erection lasts longer than 4 hours (BSW ICB guidance).

    2

    Assess cause, cardiovascular and cardiac risk

    ASSESS

    +

    Take a medical, medication and lifestyle history, including previous and present erectile quality, and examine. Assess 10-year cardiovascular risk (for example QRisk with blood pressure); erectile dysfunction is a marker for cardiovascular disease (BSW). Check serum HbA1c, lipid profile and fasting morning total testosterone in all men, with other blood tests depending on the likely cause (CKS). Regional BSW guidance also advises fasting 8 to 10 am testosterone, SHBG and albumin to calculate free testosterone. Screen people with type 2 diabetes for erectile dysfunction every year (BSW). If a drug cause is suspected, consider stopping it only if onset matched starting the drug; for most men, treating the underlying condition is more likely to help (CKS).

    3

    Lifestyle, information and underlying conditions

    SUPPORT

    +

    Advise weight loss and regular exercise, smoking cessation and alcohol reduction where indicated, and stopping cycling for a trial period if cycling more than 3 hours a week. Optimise diabetes, hypertension and dyslipidaemia. Do not recommend unlicensed herbal or complementary medicines. Offer information, such as the NHS and BAUS pages (CKS).

    4

    PDE-5 inhibitor: choice and BNF doses

    DRUG

    +

    For men not at high cardiac risk, consider a PDE-5 inhibitor irrespective of the cause; options are sildenafil, tadalafil, vardenafil and avanafil, and sildenafil 50 mg (Viagra Connect) can be bought from pharmacies (CKS). Sildenafil (BNF): "Initially 50 mg, to be taken approximately 1 hour before sexual activity, adjusted according to response to 25–100 mg (max. per dose 100 mg) as required, to be taken as a single dose; maximum 1 dose per day"; start at 25 mg with moderate or potent CYP3A4 inhibitors, or if creatinine clearance is less than 30 mL/minute. Tadalafil (BNF): "Initially 10 mg (max. per dose 20 mg), to be taken at least 30 minutes before sexual activity", maximum 1 dose per day; for sexual activity at least twice a week, "5 mg once daily, reduced to 2.5 mg once daily, adjusted according to response"; in severe renal impairment avoid once-daily dosing and use a maximum of 10 mg intermittently.

    5

    Contraindications, SLS and local formulary

    DRUG

    +

    BNF contraindications: tadalafil – acute myocardial infarction in the past 90 days, history of non-arteritic anterior ischaemic optic neuropathy, hypotension (avoid if systolic below 90 mmHg), heart failure, recent stroke, uncontrolled arrhythmias, uncontrolled hypertension, unstable angina; sildenafil – hereditary degenerative retinal disorders, non-arteritic anterior ischaemic optic neuropathy, recent myocardial infarction or stroke, systolic below 90 mmHg, recent unstable angina. Nitrates (including nicorandil and recreational amyl nitrate) are absolutely contraindicated (BSW). Sussex: generic sildenafil and generic tadalafil are no longer on the SLS restrictions list and can be prescribed to all men with erectile dysfunction; branded Viagra and Cialis and vardenafil remain SLS. The Sussex formulary lists sildenafil 25 mg, 50 mg and 100 mg and tadalafil 10 mg tablets; prescribing status: check colour on the formulary page. Regional BSW guidance (not Sussex) prefers tadalafil 5 mg daily or 10 mg on demand first-line. Source note: CKS (July 2026) says to endorse SLS when prescribing "tadalafil (Cialis®)" and that generic sildenafil needs no SLS endorsement; the BNF restricts the Cialis brand; check the current Drug Tariff Part XVIIIB.

    6

    Review at 6 to 8 weeks; dose and drug changes

    REVIEW/LIMIT

    +

    Follow up 6 to 8 weeks after starting treatment for efficacy, tolerability and satisfaction (CKS). Check it is taken correctly and consider increasing to the maximum dose. CKS: try each PDE-5 inhibitor 4 to 8 times at the maximum tolerated dose before switching; the BNF says six doses at the maximum dose (with sexual stimulation); in Sussex, alprostadil cream is only prescribed after 8 doses of at least 2 oral PDE-5 inhibitors and must meet SLS criteria. Try at least 2 PDE-5 inhibitors before classing someone as a non-responder; consider once-daily tadalafil. Hypogonadism can reduce the response. If treatment is ineffective, not tolerated or contraindicated, offer urology referral; alprostadil is second-line under careful medical supervision (BNF).

    7

    When to refer

    ASSESSREFER

    +

    Urology: young age or lifelong difficulty, pelvic, perineal or genital trauma, penile structural abnormality or abnormal testicular examination (including Peyronie's disease), or no response to at least 2 PDE-5 inhibitors. Endocrinology: suspected hypogonadism, abnormal testosterone, FSH, LH or prolactin, or if testosterone replacement is being considered. Cardiology or specialist advice: high (or, by clinical judgement, intermediate) cardiac risk, with advice to stop all sexual activity until assessed. Mental health services, psychosexual or relationship counselling: suspected psychogenic or relationship cause, or a complex or severe mental health condition (CKS, July 2026).

    1

    Red flags: stroke, cardiac or sudden deafness

    URGENT

    +

    Refer urgently to the stroke team for acute continuous vertigo with: new or severe headache or neck pain, inability to stand or sit up unaided, focal weakness or paraesthesia, dysarthria, dysphonia, diplopia, dysphagia or dysmetria, spontaneous vertical nystagmus at rest, deafness, or cerebellar signs. Chest pain or breathlessness: emergency department. Abnormal ECG, family history of sudden death, known coronary disease, blackouts or dizziness on exercise: urgent cardiology. Sudden deafness: urgent ENT (SWL ICB). CKS (December 2022): features increasing suspicion of a central cause include prolonged, severe vertigo; new-onset headache or recent trauma; focal neurological signs and symptoms; central-type nystagmus; an abnormal response to the Dix-Hallpike manoeuvre; and inability to stand or walk even with the eyes open. Suspect a central cause when the features do not match a peripheral cause.

    2

    History and focused examination

    ASSESS

    +

    History: type of dizziness (rotational vertigo, unsteadiness, light-headedness), single or recurrent episodes, duration, and a medication review. Examination: postural blood pressure, HINTS plus (head impulse, nystagmus type, test of skew, hearing), cranial nerves, weakness, sensation, ataxia, cerebellar signs, gait, and the modified Hallpike manoeuvre. Check blood glucose. Refer if you do not have the skills for these manoeuvres (SWL ICB, citing NICE). CKS also lists facial asymmetry, ear examination, eye examination, signs of peripheral neuropathy, and Romberg's, Unterberger's and alternate cover tests.

    3

    Treat by likely cause

    SUPPORT

    +

    BPPV (seconds to minutes of positional vertigo, upbeat torsional nystagmus): Epley or Semont repositioning; refer to vestibular physiotherapy if not improved. Vestibular neuritis (prolonged vertigo, positive head impulse, unidirectional nystagmus): prochlorperazine for one week; refer to vestibular rehabilitation if not improved in two to three weeks. Labyrinthitis (as neuritis plus hearing loss): antiemetic and refer as per local pathway. Postural drop of more than 20 mmHg: review medicines and lifestyle; cardiology if no improvement (SWL ICB). CKS lists prescribing information for cinnarizine, cyclizine, prochlorperazine and promethazine teoclate; check doses and duration in the BNF.

    4

    Recurrent episodes: migraine or Ménière's

    REVIEW/LIMIT

    +

    Vestibular migraine (migrainous headache with dizziness): manage as migraine. Possible Ménière's disease (recurrent severe vertigo, roaring tinnitus, fluctuating low-frequency hearing loss, aural pressure, episodes of minutes to hours): vestibular sedative for an acute episode, low salt diet, less caffeine and alcohol, stay mobile; refer to audiology if more than one episode (SWL ICB).

    1

    Cancer red flags with fatigue

    URGENT

    +

    Consider a very urgent full blood count to assess for leukaemia in adults with pallor, persistent fatigue, unexplained fever, unexplained persistent or recurrent infection, generalised lymphadenopathy, or unexplained bruising or bleeding (NG12 1.10.1). Offer an urgent chest X-ray to people aged 40 and over with 2 or more unexplained symptoms, or who have ever smoked with 1 or more, from: cough, fatigue, shortness of breath, chest pain, weight loss, appetite loss (NG12 1.1.2).

    2

    Assessment and tests to exclude other causes

    ASSESS

    +

    Carry out a medical assessment (symptoms, history, comorbidities, physical and mental health), physical examination and assessment of psychological and social impact. Tests include urinalysis for protein, blood and glucose, full blood count, urea and electrolytes, liver function, thyroid function, ESR or plasma viscosity, CRP, calcium and phosphate, HbA1c, serum ferritin, coeliac screening and creatine kinase, with others by clinical judgement (NG206 1.2.3). CKS (June 2025): ask about onset, severity, frequency, duration, fluctuations, triggers and the effect of rest, sleep and activity; features of a physical or mental health condition or stress; impact and support; diet, smoking, alcohol, recreational drug, social, sexual and travel history; and medicines. Arrange blood tests, a urine pregnancy test and urinalysis depending on clinical judgement.

    3

    Fatigue without ME/CFS features: primary care management

    SUPPORT

    +

    Manage any underlying or coexisting condition and refer if indicated; give information and sources of support; advise on sleep hygiene, healthy eating, exercise and activity, healthy working and psychosocial support; offer referral for cognitive behavioural therapy, stress management or relaxation therapies by clinical judgement; review symptom response and arrange further tests or referral if needed (CKS, June 2025). If ME/CFS is suspected, follow the next step instead.

    4

    When to suspect ME/CFS and early advice

    SUPPORT

    +

    Suspect ME/CFS if debilitating fatigue worsened by activity, post-exertional malaise, unrefreshing sleep and cognitive difficulties have persisted for at least 6 weeks in adults (4 weeks in children and young people), with significantly reduced activity and no other explanation (NG206 1.2.2). Advise not to push through symptoms, to rest as needed, and to keep a balanced diet with adequate fluid (1.3.1). Explain that diagnosis can only be confirmed after 3 months (1.3.2). Refer children and young people with suspected ME/CFS to a paediatrician (1.2.7).

    5

    Diagnosis at 3 months and referral

    REVIEW/LIMITREFER

    +

    Diagnose ME/CFS when the symptoms have persisted for 3 months and are not explained by another condition (NG206 1.4.1), and refer adults directly to an ME/CFS specialist team (1.4.3). Do not offer graded exercise therapy or any exercise programme as a cure (1.11.14).
